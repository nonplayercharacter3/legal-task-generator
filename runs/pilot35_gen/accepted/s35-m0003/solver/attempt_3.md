## II. Critical Issues

### A. Non-Renewal Notice Was Likely Late – Automatic Renewal May Have Triggered

**Where:** License Agreement §12.1–12.2; Letter §1.

**The problem.** Section 12.2 provides that the Agreement renews automatically for successive two-year terms "unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal at least 120 days before the end of the then-current term." The Initial Term ends **June 30, 2026** (§12.1). Counting back 120 days from June 30, 2026 yields **March 2, 2026**. Gliruth's non-renewal letter is dated **March 20, 2026** – eighteen days after the deadline.

**Why it matters.** If the notice is untimely, the Agreement has already renewed (or will renew on June 30, 2026) for a two-year Renewal Term through June 30, 2028. Consequences for Gliruth:

- **Exclusivity lock-in.** Section 3.3 prohibits Gliruth and its Affiliates from using *any* other inventory management, booking, logistics, or point-of-sale software "anywhere in the world" during the Term. A two-year extension would prevent Gliruth from migrating to a different platform, which is presumably the whole point of the non-renewal.
- **Fee exposure.** The Annual License Fee ($384,000) would continue, subject to a possible increase of up to 5% under §4.4.
- **Change of Control acceleration.** See Issue B below – the Ostrander merger would occur *during* a Renewal Term, and §14.2 would accelerate all License Fees for the remainder of that (now two-year) term, not just the few weeks remaining in the Initial Term.
- **Data return delay.** Section 7.4's 30-day export window would not be triggered until 2028 rather than 2026.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Immediately assess whether Breibeith has accepted the late notice or invoiced for a Renewal Term. If Breibeith has not yet acted, send a follow-up letter acknowledging the timing issue and requesting that Breibeith confirm in writing it will honor the non-renewal despite the 18-day delay, or alternatively agree to a mutual amendment terminating the Agreement effective June 30, 2026.
2. If Breibeith insists the renewal is effective, negotiate a short-term extension (e.g., 60–90 days past June 30, 2026) with a mutual release from exclusivity and a data-export obligation, in exchange for a modest transition fee.
3. Document the delivery date of the March 20 letter (courier receipt) to confirm it was in fact dispatched on or before March 20. If there is any argument that the notice was *given* (dispatched) before March 2, preserve that evidence. However, §17.1 states notices are "effective on receipt," so the receipt date controls, making this argument weak.

### B. Ostrander Merger Triggers Change-of-Control Consent Requirement – Consent Not Sought

**Where:** License Agreement §1.1 (definition of "Change of Control"), §14.2; Letter §3.

**The problem.** Section 14.2 provides:

> "Any Change of Control of Licensee, including by merger, is deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Licensee and requires the prior written consent of Licensor. If Licensee undergoes a Change of Control without obtaining that consent, Licensor may terminate this Agreement on written notice, and all License Fees for the remainder of the then-current term become immediately due and payable."

The Letter discloses that on **March 12, 2026**, Gliruth signed a merger agreement under which **Ostrander Venue Group, Inc.** will acquire Gliruth. The Letter characterizes this as "for information only" and does **not** request Breibeith's consent.

**Why it matters.**

- If the Ostrander merger closes before the Agreement ends (whether June 30, 2026 or later if renewal triggered), it is a Change of Control under §1.1(b) (acquisition of Control by a person or group).
- Breibeith would have the right to **terminate immediately** and **accelerate all remaining License Fees** for the then-current term. If a Renewal Term has been triggered (Issue A), that could mean up to two years of fees (~$768,000+) accelerated.
- Even if no Renewal Term has been triggered, acceleration of fees for the remainder of the Initial Term (from closing date to June 30, 2026) is a monetary exposure, and termination would cut off Gliruth's access to the Software mid-transition.
- The Letter's disclosure, while framed as "for information only," gives Breibeith actual notice of the pending Change of Control. Breibeith could argue Gliruth is in breach of §14.2 the moment the merger closes without consent, and that the Letter evidences Gliruth's awareness of the transaction.
- There is a tension: Gliruth has told Breibeith it will *not* renew, yet the merger (if it closes during a renewal term) would give Breibeith a termination right that Gliruth did not anticipate.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Before the Ostrander merger closes**, obtain Breibeith's written consent under §14.2, or negotiate a waiver. Frame it as: Gliruth is not renewing; the merger is expected to close before or around June 30, 2026; Gliruth requests consent to the Change of Control on the condition that (a) the Agreement will not be terminated for this reason, (b) no fees are accelerated, and (c) Breibeith cooperates with the data-export and transition obligations under §7.4.
2. If the merger is expected to close *after* June 30, 2026 and the Agreement has ended, the Change-of-Control clause may not be triggered (the Agreement would no longer be in effect). Confirm the expected closing date and align it with the Agreement's end date.
3. If Breibeith refuses consent or demands a fee, evaluate whether the cost of consent is less than the risk of termination and fee acceleration. Given that Gliruth is exiting the relationship, a modest consent fee may be the pragmatic path.
4. Correct the Letter's characterization going forward: in any further correspondence, explicitly state that Gliruth is seeking consent under §14.2 (if not already done) to avoid any argument that the "for information only" language was an attempt to circumvent the consent requirement.

### C. Conflicting Governing Law and Forum Selection Clauses

**Where:** License Agreement §16.1–16.2 (Delaware law; Wilmington, Delaware forum); Quality Agreement §9.1–9.2 (New York law; New York County forum); License Agreement §17.4 (order of precedence).

**The problem.** The two agreements select different governing laws and different exclusive forums:

| | License Agreement | Quality Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Governing Law | Delaware (§16.1) | New York (§9.1) |
| Forum | Wilmington, DE (§16.2) | New York County, NY (§9.2) |

Section 17.4 of the License Agreement attempts to resolve conflicts: "the Quality Agreement controls on matters it addresses; otherwise this License Agreement controls." The Quality Agreement's §9.1 states it governs "any dispute arising out of or relating to the performance, availability or accuracy of the Software." This is broad enough to swallow most operational disputes (outages, data errors, support failures), pushing them into New York. But termination rights, fee disputes, assignment, and Change of Control issues arise under the License Agreement and would be governed by Delaware law.

**Why it matters.**

- In a multi-issue dispute (e.g., Breibeith terminates for Change of Control *and* Gliruth counterclaims for chronic service-level failures), the parties could end up litigating in two different forums under two different bodies of law.
- The "matters it addresses" language in §17.4 is itself ambiguous and could be the subject of a threshold motion.
- New York and Delaware differ on issues like the enforceability of exclusive forum clauses, the availability of certain remedies, and the standard for material breach.

**Recommended fix.**

1. If renegotiating any aspect of the Agreement (e.g., to resolve Issues A or B), seek a conforming amendment to align both agreements to a single governing law and forum. Delaware is the law of both parties' state of organization and is a neutral, well-developed commercial law.
2. If no amendment is possible, be prepared to argue that §17.4 channels Quality Agreement disputes (service levels, data accuracy, support) to New York and all other disputes (termination, fees, assignment, Change of Control) to Delaware. Document this position in any dispute correspondence.
3. Flag to the client that if a dispute arises, the first step will likely be a fight over which court has jurisdiction, adding cost and delay.

## III. Significant Issues

### D. Exclusivity Clause Is Extremely Broad

**Where:** License Agreement §3.3.

**The problem.** Section 3.3 prohibits Gliruth and its Affiliates from licensing, developing, or using "any inventory management, booking, logistics or point-of-sale software other than the Software, anywhere in the world" during the Term. This is not limited to event-rental-specific software; it could arguably encompass any POS system used at Gliruth's showrooms or warehouses.

**Why it matters.**

- If the Agreement renews (Issue A), Gliruth is locked out of alternative platforms for two more years.
- Even during the transition period after a valid non-renewal, if there is any dispute about whether the Agreement has truly ended, Gliruth risks a breach claim for testing or implementing a replacement system.
- The "Affiliates" restriction could reach Ostrander after the merger if Ostrander is deemed an Affiliate (though post-merger, Gliruth would be the subsidiary, so the restriction would run to Ostrander and its other entities as well, depending on how "Affiliate" is read).

**Recommended fix.**

1. If the Agreement is ending June 30, 2026, obtain Breibeith's written confirmation that the exclusivity obligation terminates on that date and that Gliruth (and Ostrander, post-closing) may implement alternative software effective July 1, 2026.
2. If a renewal has been triggered, negotiate a carve-out or sunset for the exclusivity clause as part of any amendment.
3. Until the Agreement definitively ends, avoid signing any new software licenses that could be characterized as "inventory management, booking, logistics or point-of-sale" without first obtaining a release from §3.3.

### E. Data Return and Export – Timing Risk for Peak Season

**Where:** License Agreement §7.4; Quality Agreement §4.3; Letter §2.

**The problem.** Section 7.4 requires Breibeith to make Licensee Data available for export "within thirty (30) days after expiration or termination." If the Agreement ends June 30, 2026, the export must be available by **July 30, 2026**. Gliruth's letter notes its "peak spring and early-summer event season" and requests a call in April 2026 to discuss export formats and timing.

**Why it matters.**

- Gliruth needs its inventory records, customer information, event bookings, delivery schedules, and dispatch history (all defined as "Licensee Data" in §1.1) to operate. A 30-day window after June 30 means Gliruth could be without full data access during the height of its summer season if the export is not completed by early July.
- The "standard, machine-readable format" requirement is helpful but does not specify which format. If Breibeith exports in a format Gliruth's replacement system cannot ingest, Gliruth may need to pay for professional services (at Breibeith's rates under §4.3) to convert the data.
- If the Agreement renews (Issue A), §7.4 is not triggered at all, and Gliruth has no contractual right to demand an export until 2028.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Push Breibeith to confirm the export format (CSV, JSON, SQL dump, API pull, etc.) and to provide a sample or schema **before** June 30, 2026, so Gliruth can validate that its replacement system can ingest the data.
2. Request that Breibeith make the export available **on or before June 30, 2026** (i.e., at the moment the Agreement ends, not 30 days later). This is a reasonable ask given that §7.4 sets a 30-day outer limit, not a target.
3. If the Agreement renews, negotiate a data-export right as part of any amendment or short-term extension, so Gliruth is not held hostage to the data for two years.
4. Confirm that Breibeith's backup and retention practices (Quality Agmt. §4.3: hourly backups, 30-day daily backup retention, separate data center) mean the data will actually be available and intact at the time of export.

### F. Service Credits Are the Exclusive Remedy for Most Performance Failures – and Are Capped Low

**Where:** Quality Agreement §6.1–6.6; License Agreement §8.2, §10.1–10.2.

**The problem.** Under Quality Agreement §6.6, Service Credits (plus the chronic-failure termination right in §6.5) are Gliruth's **exclusive** remedies for Breibeith's failure to meet uptime, data-accuracy, or support standards. The maximum monthly credit is 25% of the Monthly Fee Amount (§6.3). The Monthly Fee Amount is 1/12 of $384,000 = **$32,000**. So the maximum credit per month is **$8,000**.

**Why it matters.**

- If RentFlow is down for an entire day during a critical event dispatch, Gliruth's actual losses (missed deliveries, angry customers, manual workarounds) could far exceed $8,000, but that is all Gliruth can recover as a Service Credit.
- The chronic-failure trigger (§6.5: Availability below 99.0% in two months within a rolling six-month period, or more than three Severity 1 Incidents in a quarter) is the only path to termination for service failures, and it is a high bar.
- The general limitation of liability (§10.2) caps all other damages at $384,000 (12 months of fees), and §10.1 excludes consequential damages, lost profits, etc.

**Recommended fix.**

1. For the remaining term (whether through June 30, 2026 or beyond), track all Incidents, Availability figures, and Service Credits meticulously. Use the quarterly reporting obligation (Quality Agmt. §5.4) and governance meetings (§5.5) to build a record.
2. If chronic failures are occurring, consider invoking §6.5 to establish a material breach, which would support termination under License Agreement §13.2 and potentially strengthen Gliruth's negotiating position on exit terms.
3. If renegotiating, seek to increase the Service Credit cap or add a super-cap for data-loss events. At minimum, clarify that a data breach (as distinct from a data *error*) is not limited to Service Credits but falls under the §10.3(c) carve-out for confidentiality breaches.

### G. Breibeith's Insurance Certificate Is Not in the File

**Where:** License Agreement §11.2, §11.3.

**The problem.** Section 11.2 requires Breibeith to maintain technology E&O and cyber liability insurance of not less than **$5,000,000 per claim**. Section 11.3 allows either party to request a certificate within 30 days. The file contains only Gliruth's certificate (via Pellacott). There is **no evidence** that Breibeith has provided its certificate or that Gliruth has requested one.

**Why it matters.**

- Gliruth is entrusting all of its operational data (inventory, customer PII, booking records) to Breibeith's hosted platform. If Breibeith suffers a cyber event or data breach, Gliruth's recovery may depend on Breibeith's insurance.
- If Breibeith is not actually carrying the required $5M tech E&O / cyber coverage, Gliruth's practical protection is limited to the $384,000 liability cap (§10.2), which may be insufficient for a major breach.
- The absence of Breibeith's certificate in the file may simply mean it was never requested, but it is a gap in Gliruth's risk management.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Send a written request to Breibeith under §11.3 for its certificate of insurance evidencing the §11.2 coverage (tech E&O and cyber liability, $5M per claim). This is a low-cost, high-value step.
2. If Breibeith cannot produce the certificate, escalate: a failure to maintain required insurance is a breach of the Agreement that could support a termination claim under §13.2 (material breach) if uncured.
3. Verify that the coverage is in force for the full remaining term (including any renewal period).

### H. Per-User Rate for Additional Authorized Users Is Unspecified

**Where:** License Agreement §4.1–4.2.

**The problem.** Section 4.1 includes 150 named Authorized Users in the $384,000 Annual License Fee. Section 4.2 allows additional users "at Licensor's then-current per-user rate, prorated for the remainder of the applicable contract year." The per-user rate is **not stated** in the Agreement.

**Why it matters.**

- If Gliruth has added users beyond 150 (e.g., for seasonal peak staffing or for Ostrander integration planning), Breibeith could assert that Gliruth owes additional fees at whatever rate Breibeith unilaterally sets.
- The absence of a fixed rate or a rate-card attachment creates a potential dispute, especially during a transition when Gliruth may be adding users to manage the export or onboarding a replacement system.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Confirm internally how many Authorized Users are currently active in the RentFlow platform. If the number exceeds 150, request Breibeith's written confirmation of the per-user rate applied and the total amount owed (if any).
2. If renegotiating, seek to fix the per-user rate or cap it (e.g., not to exceed a stated dollar amount or a stated percentage of the Annual License Fee divided by 150).
3. Ensure that any users added for transition purposes (e.g., to manage data export) are either within the 150-user cap or pre-approved in writing at a known rate.

## IV. Moderate / Housekeeping Issues

### I. Insurance Certificate Delivery Method

**Where:** License Agreement §17.1; Pellacott Letter (transmission line).

**The problem.** Section 17.1 permits notices by hand, nationally recognized overnight courier, or certified mail (return receipt requested). The Pellacott letter was sent "VIA EMAIL AND FIRST-CLASS MAIL." First-class mail is not a permitted method under §17.1.

**Why it matters.** This is likely of minimal practical consequence because (a) the letter is from a third-party broker, not a "notice" under the Agreement in the strict sense, and (b) Breibeith clearly received it. However, if Breibeith were to argue it never properly received the certificate (e.g., in a dispute about whether Gliruth was in compliance with §11.1), the delivery method could be raised.

**Recommended fix.** No action needed now, but for any future certificate deliveries or formal notices, use overnight courier or certified mail as specified in §17.1. Keep the courier receipt.

### K. No Statement of Work in the File

**Where:** License Agreement §4.3.

**The problem.** Section 4.3 contemplates that implementation, data migration, training, or custom integration services will be documented in a written statement of work. No SOW is in the file.

**Why it matters.** If Gliruth received any professional services from Breibeith (e.g., initial data migration at onboarding, custom integrations), the scope, deliverables, and fees would be governed by the SOW. Without it, there is no record of what was agreed. This could matter if there is a dispute about whether certain functionality was "included" in the license or was a paid service.

**Recommended fix.** Ask the client whether any SOWs were executed. If so, obtain copies for the file. If the client believes services were rendered without a signed SOW, flag as a potential gap.

**Where:** License Agreement and Quality Agreement signature blocks.

## VII. Conclusion

The two most urgent issues are the **late non-renewal notice** (which may have triggered an unwanted two-year renewal) and the **Ostrander Change of Control** (which requires Breibeith's consent before closing or risks termination and fee acceleration). These two issues are interrelated: if the renewal has triggered, the Change-of-Control exposure is significantly worse because the accelerated fees would cover a two-year term rather than a few weeks. I recommend addressing both in a single negotiation with Breibeith, using the non-renewal notice as the opening and the consent request as the follow-up, with the goal of a clean exit on June 30, 2026 with data in hand and no fee acceleration.
