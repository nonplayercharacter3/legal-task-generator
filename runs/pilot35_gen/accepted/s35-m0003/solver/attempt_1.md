## II. ISSUES IDENTIFIED

### A. CRITICAL: Non-Renewal Notice Was Sent Late

**Where:** License Agreement §12.2; Letter §1.

**The problem.** Section 12.2 requires "written notice of non-renewal at least 120 days before the end of the then-current term." The Initial Term ends **June 30, 2026** (§12.1). Counting back 120 days from June 30, 2026 lands on **March 2, 2026**. The letter is dated **March 20, 2026**—18 days after the deadline.

**Why it matters.** Breibeith has a colorable argument that the notice is ineffective and that the Agreement will auto-renew for a two-year Renewal Term beginning July 1, 2026. If that happens:

- Gliruth is locked in through June 30, 2028.
- Breibeith may issue a fee-increase notice under §4.4 (up to 5%, i.e., to ~$403,200/year) by May 1, 2026 (60 days before the Renewal Term).
- The Change-of-Control acceleration risk in §14.2 (see Issue B below) becomes far more expensive because the "then-current term" would be the full two-year Renewal Term rather than the last few weeks of the Initial Term.

Gliruth's letter anticipates this by stating it "disputes that any automatic renewal…has occurred or will occur" and reserves rights, but a dispute is not the same as a valid notice.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Immediately** send a supplemental letter to Breibeith acknowledging the timing issue and requesting that Breibeith confirm in writing it will not treat the Agreement as having renewed. Frame it as a request for waiver of the 120-day requirement, citing the parties' course of dealing and the fact that Gliruth is preparing for an orderly exit.
2. If Breibeith refuses to waive, consider whether there is an argument that the notice was effective despite the 18-day delay (e.g., substantial compliance, waiver by Breibeith's conduct if it has already begun transition planning, or equitable estoppel if Breibeith represented it would accept a later date). This is a weaker position but worth preserving.
3. In parallel, begin negotiating a mutual early-termination or transition agreement with Breibeith that sets out data-export timing, a wind-down period, and a release from further fee obligations. This is the cleanest path and gives Gliruth control over the narrative.
4. If the Ostrander merger closes before June 30, 2026, the Change-of-Control issue (below) will force the conversation regardless—use that leverage.

### B. CRITICAL: Pending Ostrander Merger Triggers §14.2 Change-of-Control Provision

**Where:** License Agreement §14.2, §1.1 (definition of "Change of Control"); Letter §3.

**The problem.** Section 14.2 provides:

> "Any Change of Control of Licensee, including by merger, is deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Licensee and requires the prior written consent of Licensor. If Licensee undergoes a Change of Control without obtaining that consent, Licensor may terminate this Agreement on written notice, and all License Fees for the remainder of the then-current term become immediately due and payable."

The letter discloses that Gliruth signed a merger agreement on March 12, 2026 to be acquired by Ostrander Venue Group, Inc. The transaction is "subject to customary closing conditions." If it closes before June 30, 2026 (or before the end of any Renewal Term, if auto-renewal occurs), §14.2 is triggered.

Gliruth has **not** sought Breibeith's consent. The letter merely discloses the merger "for information only" and asks Breibeith to treat it as confidential.

**Why it matters.**

- If Breibeith withholds consent after closing, it can terminate and accelerate all remaining License Fees. If the Initial Term is in effect, the exposure is modest (a few weeks of the $384,000 annual fee). But if auto-renewal has occurred (see Issue A), the "then-current term" is two years, and the accelerated amount could approach **$768,000**.
- Even without termination, the consent requirement gives Breibeith negotiating leverage over the transition.
- There is a tension with the non-renewal notice: Gliruth is telling Breibeith it does not want to renew, but simultaneously its ownership is changing in a way that Breibeith may not want to accept. Breibeith could argue it is entitled to consent before the merger closes, or that the merger voids the non-renewal notice.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Before the Ostrander merger closes**, send a formal request for consent under §14.2, with a proposed transition/exit plan attached. Do not wait for Breibeith to raise it.
2. Negotiate a consent that is conditioned on (a) Gliruth's (or Ostrander's assumption of) payment of fees through the actual exit date, (b) completion of data export under §7.4, and (c) a mutual release. This converts a potential dispute into a managed transition.
3. If Ostrander is willing, consider having Ostrander (as successor) provide a direct letter to Breibeith confirming it will not renew and will cooperate on transition. This addresses Breibeith's likely concern that a new owner might be less cooperative.
4. Confirm with the Ostrander deal team whether the merger agreement contains any covenant requiring Gliruth to obtain third-party consents as a closing condition, and whether Breibeith's consent is identified. If the merger agreement is silent, flag this to the client as a potential gap in the closing checklist.
5. **Document missing:** We do not have the Ostrander merger agreement in the file. Recommend obtaining it to confirm closing timeline, conditions, and any covenants regarding material contracts.

### C. SIGNIFICANT: Governing Law and Forum Conflict Between the Two Agreements

**Where:** License Agreement §16.1–16.2; Quality Agreement §9.1–9.2; License Agreement §17.4 (order of precedence).

**The problem.**

| | License Agreement | Quality Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Delaware (§16.1) | New York (§9.1) |
| Exclusive forum | Wilmington, Delaware (§16.2) | New York County, New York (§9.2) |

Section 17.4 of the License Agreement provides: "If this License Agreement and the Quality Agreement conflict, the Quality Agreement controls on matters it addresses; otherwise this License Agreement controls."

The Quality Agreement's jurisdiction clause (§9.2) covers "any action arising out of or relating to this Quality Agreement **or to the performance, availability or accuracy of the Software**." That is an extremely broad grant that likely swallows most performance disputes, even though they arise under the License Agreement.

**Why it matters.** In any dispute about software performance (downtime, data errors, support failures), Breibeith will argue New York law and forum apply. Gliruth will argue Delaware. The §17.4 tiebreaker helps the Quality Agreement side, but the scope of "matters it addresses" is ambiguous. This creates:

- Uncertainty about which substantive law governs (Delaware and New York differ on issues like the enforceability of liability caps, the standard for material breach, and the availability of equitable remedies).
- Potential venue fights that consume time and money before the merits are reached.
- Complication in the current transition context: if Gliruth later asserts that Breibeith failed to meet service levels during the wind-down, the governing law and forum are unclear.

**Recommended fix.**

1. If negotiating a transition or early-termination agreement with Breibeith (see Issues A and B), include a clause specifying that the transition agreement is governed by Delaware law and that disputes will be resolved in Wilmington, Delaware. This avoids the conflict for the most likely near-term dispute.
2. For the longer term, if the relationship continues (e.g., if auto-renewal is unavoidable), seek an amendment reconciling the governing-law and forum provisions. A reasonable compromise: Delaware law for all disputes, with the Quality Agreement's service-level standards incorporated as the performance benchmark but not as a separate governing-law regime.
3. Flag to the client that if a service-level dispute arises before the agreements are resolved, we will need to make a forum choice quickly and be prepared to litigate a threshold venue issue.

### D. SIGNIFICANT: Exclusivity Clause Is Overly Broad

**Where:** License Agreement §3.3.

**The problem.** Section 3.3 provides:

> "During the Term, Licensee and its Affiliates shall not license, develop or use any inventory management, booking, logistics or point-of-sale software other than the Software, anywhere in the world."

RentFlow is defined as "a hosted inventory, booking and dispatch platform for rental businesses" (Recital A). It is not described as a point-of-sale system. Yet §3.3 prohibits Gliruth from using *any* point-of-sale software. It also prohibits any "logistics" software, a term that could encompass transportation management, route optimization, warehouse management, or even fleet-tracking tools used by Gliruth's delivery crews.

**Why it matters.**

- If Gliruth uses (or has used) any POS terminal, tablet app, or payment-processing software at its showrooms or event sites, it may be in ongoing breach of §3.3.
- The restriction covers "Affiliates" as well, which under §1.1 includes any entity that controls Gliruth. After the Ostrander merger, Ostrander and its subsidiaries would be bound by this restriction if the Agreement continues.
- A breach of §3.3 would be a material breach under §13.2, giving Breibeith termination rights and potentially undermining Gliruth's non-renewal position (i.e., Breibeith could argue Gliruth is in breach and therefore cannot walk away cleanly).

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Audit immediately** what software Gliruth and its Affiliates currently use. Identify anything that could be characterized as inventory management, booking, logistics, or point-of-sale. Document the findings.
2. If any potential breach exists, disclose it to Breibeith proactively as part of the transition negotiation and seek a waiver or a narrow reading. It is far better to raise this on Gliruth's terms than to have Breibeith discover it and use it as leverage.
3. In any transition or early-termination agreement, include a mutual release of all claims arising under the Agreement through the effective date of the release, which would extinguish any exclusivity-breach exposure.
4. For the client's post-exit operations (under Ostrander), confirm that the exclusivity restriction ends with the Agreement. It should, because it applies only "During the Term," but verify that no residual obligations (e.g., non-compete in §3.1(e)) survive.

### E. MODERATE: Gliruth's Remedies for Service Failures Are Severely Limited

**Where:** Quality Agreement §6.1–6.6; License Agreement §8.2, §10.1–10.2.

**The problem.** The Quality Agreement makes Service Credits the exclusive remedy for failure to meet uptime, data-accuracy, or support standards (§6.6, cross-referenced by License Agreement §8.2). The credits are capped at **25% of the Monthly Fee Amount per month** (§6.3). With a $384,000 annual fee, the Monthly Fee Amount is $32,000, so the maximum credit in any month is **$8,000**.

On top of that, License Agreement §10.1 excludes all consequential, indirect, and lost-profits damages, and §10.2 caps total liability at 12 months of fees (~$384,000).

**Why it matters.** If RentFlow goes down during a major event weekend, Gliruth could lose tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars in event revenue, incur overtime costs to manually process bookings, and face claims from its own customers. Yet its maximum recovery from Breibeith would be $8,000 in service credits for that month, and it would have no claim for the actual business loss. The only escalation is termination for "chronic failure" under §6.5 (availability below 99.0% in two months within a rolling six-month period, or more than three Severity 1 Incidents in a quarter), but termination near the end of the term provides little practical benefit.

**Recommended fix.**

1. In the transition negotiation, seek a provision that if a Severity 1 Incident occurs during the final 60 days of the term (the peak-season window), the service credit is uncapped or the liability cap in §10.2 is lifted for that incident. Breibeith has an incentive to keep the system stable during wind-down to avoid a dispute.
2. For the client's operational planning: ensure that Gliruth's delivery and event teams have manual fallback procedures (paper booking logs, spreadsheet-based dispatch) so that a software outage does not become a catastrophic operational failure. This is a business recommendation, not a legal fix, but it mitigates the legal gap.
3. If the Agreement does renew (against Gliruth's wishes), negotiate an amendment to §6.6 that carves out Severity 1 Incidents occurring during peak season (April–October) from the exclusive-remedy provision, or at minimum increases the credit cap for those months.

### F. MODERATE: Quality Agreement Ends Simultaneously with the License Agreement—No Post-Term Service Protection

**Where:** Quality Agreement §8.1; License Agreement §7.4.

**The problem.** Quality Agreement §8.1: "This Quality Agreement…ends automatically on the expiration or termination of the License Agreement, except that Sections 6.4 and 9 survive." Section 6.4 (payment of accrued Service Credits) and Section 9 (governing law/jurisdiction) are the only surviving provisions.

License Agreement §7.4 requires Breibeith to make Licensee Data available for export "within thirty (30) days after expiration or termination." During that 30-day window, the Quality Agreement is no longer in effect. There is no service-level commitment, no support obligation, no uptime guarantee.

**Why it matters.** Gliruth needs the software to be functional during the 30-day export window so its team can actually pull data. If Breibeith lets the system degrade or takes it offline before the export is complete, Gliruth has no contractual remedy under the Quality Agreement. Its only recourse would be a general breach-of-contract claim under the License Agreement (subject to the Delaware/New York governing-law conflict in Issue C and the liability cap in §10.2).

**Recommended fix.**

1. In the transition agreement, require Breibeith to maintain the Software in production (with the same uptime commitment) for a defined post-term period (e.g., 60 days) specifically to facilitate data export. Make this an express covenant in the transition agreement, not a reliance on the Quality Agreement.
2. Specify the export format, the point of contact, and a deadline for completion. The letter already requests this, but it should be memorialized in a signed side letter or transition agreement, not left to an informal email exchange.
3. Confirm that Breibeith will not reduce support staffing or take the system into "maintenance mode" during the export window.

### G. MODERATE: Data Export Format and Timing Are Unspecified

**Where:** License Agreement §7.4; Letter §2.

**The problem.** Section 7.4 requires export "in a standard, machine-readable format" but does not define what that means. The letter asks Breibeith to confirm "the procedure and timing" within 15 business days and proposes an April 2026 call to discuss "export file formats for inventory records, event bookings, delivery schedules and dispatch history."

**Why it matters.** "Standard, machine-readable format" could mean anything from a CSV dump to a full API export. If Breibeith provides a minimal CSV that lacks relational integrity (e.g., bookings disconnected from inventory items, dispatch history without associated event data), Gliruth may find it unusable for migration to a replacement system. Given that the Ostrander merger may bring its own technology stack, format compatibility is critical.

**Recommended fix.**

1. At the April call (or by written specification if the call does not happen), provide Breibeith with a detailed data-export requirements document listing:
   - Each data category (inventory, bookings, customers, delivery schedules, dispatch history, user accounts, audit logs)
   - Required fields per category
   - Format (CSV with specified column headers, JSON, or native database export)
   - Relational integrity requirements (foreign keys preserved)
   - Time range (full history vs. trailing 3 years)
   - Deadline for delivery
2. Get Breibeith's written confirmation of the format and timeline. Do not rely on a verbal agreement.
3. Coordinate with Ostrander's IT team (if accessible pre-closing) to confirm the export format is compatible with their target system.

### H. LOW: Insurance Compliance Appears Satisfied, but Monitor for Gaps

**Where:** License Agreement §11.1–11.3; Pellacott Cover Letter §§2–4.

**The problem (or lack thereof).** Section 11.1 requires CGL of not less than $2,000,000 per occurrence, with insurers rated A- or better by A.M. Best and authorized in the states where Gliruth operates. The Pellacott letter confirms:

- CGL: $2,000,000 per occurrence (Harbor Meridian, A-rated) ✓
- Authorized in Ohio and other states where Gliruth operates ✓
- Policy period: January 1, 2026 – January 1, 2027 (covers the remainder of the Initial Term) ✓

**Potential gap.** If the Agreement auto-renews (Issue A), the term extends to at least June 30, 2028. The current policy expires January 1, 2027. Pellacott has committed to send an updated certificate at renewal (§6 of the cover letter), but Gliruth should confirm that the renewal will maintain the same limits and that the insurer will continue to meet the A- rating threshold. Additionally, if Ostrander's acquisition changes Gliruth's operational footprint (new states, new locations), the "states where Licensee operates" requirement in §11.1 may expand.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Confirm with Pellacott that the January 2027 renewal will maintain CGL at $2,000,000+ per occurrence and that the insurer's A.M. Best rating will remain A- or better.
2. If the Ostrander merger expands Gliruth's operating states, verify that Harbor Meridian (and any other required insurer) is authorized in those additional states before the new policy period begins.
3. The actual ACORD 25 certificate is referenced as an enclosure to the Pellacott letter but **is not in our file**. Recommend obtaining a copy for the record.
