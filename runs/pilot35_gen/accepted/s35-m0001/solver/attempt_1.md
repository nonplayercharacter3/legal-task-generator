## Summary

I have reviewed the Asset Purchase Agreement (APA), the Seller's board consent, the form of Bill of Sale, and the Brooklyn studio lease. The package contains several material exposures for Stepair, the most serious of which is that the deal is structured to close without the third-party consent required to assign the Membrane Technology License Agreement — a license that is central to the shell product line Buyer is acquiring for $18.4 million. Below are the issues in order of significance.

---

## 1. Membrane Technology License: Assignment Without Required Consent

**Where:**
- APA §2.1(c) and Schedule 2.1(c), item 1: The license is a Purchased Asset.
- APA §4.4 and Schedule 4.4: Seller represents that no third-party consent is required "except for the Landlord Consent." Schedule 4.4 states "None."
- APA §7.2(d): The only third-party consent made a condition to Buyer's closing obligation is the Landlord Consent.
- Seller Board Consent §1.4(a)–(b): Acknowledges that §14.2 of the license prohibits assignment without Fjellbrand's prior written consent, and that §14.3 permits Fjellbrand to terminate the license upon an unconsented assignment.
- Seller Board Consent §4.2: Directly authorizes Seller's officers to "proceed to close without first obtaining the prior written consent of Fjellbrand."
- Bill of Sale §2.1(c): Conveys the license to Buyer.

**Why it matters:**
The membrane technology license is the core IP underpinning the shell product line. Without it, the Business cannot manufacture its principal products. The Seller's own board consent concedes that assigning the license without Fjellbrand's consent exposes the license to termination. Yet the APA (i) does not list Fjellbrand's consent among the required consents in §4.4/Schedule 4.4, (ii) does not make Fjellbrand's consent a condition to Buyer's obligation to close in §7.2, and (iii) does not require Seller to deliver Fjellbrand's consent as a closing deliverable in §3.2. The Board Consent affirmatively instructs Seller to close without it.

If Fjellbrand terminates the license post-closing under §14.3, Buyer would have paid $18.4 million for a business that cannot produce its core product, and its indemnity recovery would be capped at $1,840,000 (see Issue 4 below) because the IP representation (§4.8) is not among the carve-outs from the cap in §8.4.

**Recommendation:**
- Add Fjellbrand's written consent to the assignment as a condition to Buyer's closing obligation in §7.2 (new subsection, e.g., §7.2(g)).
- Add delivery of Fjellbrand's consent to Seller's closing deliverables in §3.2.
- Correct Schedule 4.4 to list the Fjellbrand consent.
- Consider whether to add §4.8 (IP) to the list of representations in §8.4 that are carved out from the indemnity cap (i.e., subject only to the Purchase Price cap rather than the $1.84M cap).
- If Seller resists making Fjellbrand consent a closing condition, at minimum require Seller to obtain the consent before signing (i.e., make it a condition to the effectiveness of the APA) or provide a specific indemnity for loss of the license that is not subject to the general cap.
- **Open question:** We do not have a copy of the Membrane Technology License Agreement itself. We should request it to confirm the scope of §14.2 and §14.3, whether there are any change-of-control provisions beyond the assignment restriction, and whether Fjellbrand has any right of first refusal or approval standard (e.g., "not to be unreasonably withheld").

## 2. Seller's Right to Assign the APA and Be Released from Indemnity and Non-Compete

**Where:**
- APA §11.5: "Seller may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, without the consent of Buyer to any Affiliate or to any Person acquiring all or substantially all of Seller's remaining assets. Any such assignment shall include Seller's obligations under Article VIII (Indemnification) and Section 6.8 (Non-Competition), and upon such assignment Seller shall be released from those obligations."

**Why it matters:**
After the Closing, Seller will retain only its insulated apparel and footwear lines (APA §2.2, Schedule 2.2). If Seller sells those remaining assets to a third party, that acquirer steps into Seller's shoes under the APA, and Seller is released from both its indemnification obligations (Article VIII) and its five-year non-compete (§6.8).

Two distinct risks follow:

*(a) Indemnity risk.* Buyer's post-closing remedies for breaches of Seller's representations, covenants, and Excluded Liabilities would rest with an unknown third party that may lack the financial capacity to satisfy an indemnity claim. The indemnity is only as good as the indemnitor's balance sheet.

*(b) Non-compete risk.* The non-compete in §6.8 prohibits "Seller" and its "Affiliates" from competing in technical outerwear shell products in North America or Europe for five years. If Seller assigns to an acquirer of its insulated apparel or footwear lines, that acquirer may have no existing shell product business, making the non-compete less meaningful as a practical deterrent. Conversely, if the acquirer *does* have a shell product line, the non-compete binds it — but only if the assignment is properly made under §11.5. There is no requirement that the assignee affirmatively agree to the non-compete or that Buyer consent to the assignment.

**Recommendation:**
- Strike or substantially narrow the Seller assignment right in §11.5. At minimum, require Buyer's prior written consent to any assignment of Seller's indemnification or non-compete obligations.
- If Seller insists on retaining the assignment right, add a requirement that any assignee of the indemnification obligations provides Buyer with a financial guaranty or letter of credit in an amount equal to the remaining indemnity cap.
- Add a requirement that the assignee execute a joinder or assumption agreement specifically acknowledging the non-compete in §6.8.
- Consider adding a provision that Seller's release is effective only upon Buyer's receipt of the assignee's executed assumption and any required financial security.

## 3. Member Approval Not Yet Confirmed

**Where:**
- Seller Board Consent §5.1–5.2: The Board "recommends" member approval and directs the Secretary to "submit the transaction to the members for approval by written consent." The consent is dated September 11, 2026.
- APA §4.2: Seller represents that the Agreement "has been duly authorized by all requisite action of Seller's board of managers **and members**."
- APA §3.2(c): Seller must deliver at Closing a certificate "certifying the resolutions of Seller's board of managers authorizing the execution, delivery and performance of this Agreement."

**Why it matters:**
The board consent contemplates that member approval is still to be solicited. If members have not yet approved by the time the APA is signed (September 14, 2026) or by Closing (October 30, 2026), Seller's representation in §4.2 is inaccurate. While §4.2 is one of the "fundamental" representations carved out from the indemnity cap (see §8.4), a breach of an authority representation could also give rise to a claim that the contract is voidable, creating uncertainty for both sides. More practically, if members disapprove or condition their approval, the deal could fall through after Buyer has incurred significant costs.

**Recommendation:**
- **Open question:** We do not have the member written consent. Request confirmation that member approval has been obtained, or at minimum that the consents have been solicited and are expected before signing.
- Add delivery of the member consent (or a certificate confirming it) to Seller's closing deliverables in §3.2.
- Consider adding a condition to Buyer's closing obligation in §7.2 that Seller has obtained all requisite member approvals.

## 4. Indemnity Cap Does Not Protect Against IP Loss

**Where:**
- APA §8.4: Seller's liability for breaches of representations (§8.2(a)) is subject to a $184,000 deductible (basket) and a $1,840,000 cap. The cap does not apply to "fraud or from any inaccuracy in or breach of the representations in Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.5 or 4.14."
- APA §4.8: The IP representation (covering the Membrane Technology License and all other IP) is not among the carved-out sections.

**Why it matters:**
If the membrane license is lost (whether by Fjellbrand termination under Issue 1, by a defect in title, or by an infringement claim), Buyer's maximum recovery under the indemnity is $1.84 million — roughly 10% of the purchase price. Given that the license is the foundation of the shell product line, the actual value at risk could be far greater. The same limitation applies to any other IP problem (trademark defects, trade secret misappropriation, etc.).

**Recommendation:**
- Add §4.8 (IP) to the list of representations in §8.4 that are excluded from the $1.84M cap (i.e., subject only to the Purchase Price cap).
- Alternatively, negotiate a specific indemnity for the Membrane Technology License that is uncapped or capped at the Purchase Price, separate from the general indemnity.
- At a minimum, ensure that the Fjellbrand consent condition (Issue 1) is in place so that the risk of license loss is addressed pre-closing rather than through post-closing indemnity.

## 5. Conflicting Governing Law and Jurisdiction Provisions

**Where:**
- APA §11.7: New York law governs the APA.
- APA §11.8: Exclusive jurisdiction in state or federal courts in New York County, NY.
- Bill of Sale §6.1: Delaware law governs the Bill of Sale.
- Bill of Sale §6.2: Exclusive jurisdiction in the Court of Chancery of the State of Delaware.
- APA §11.9 (Order of Precedence): "In the event of any inconsistency between this Agreement and any Ancillary Document, the Ancillary Documents control as to matters specifically addressed therein."

**Why it matters:**
The Bill of Sale is an Ancillary Document. Under §11.9, its governing law and jurisdiction provisions control "as to matters specifically addressed therein" — which arguably includes any dispute about the conveyance of title to the Purchased Assets. This creates a split: a claim that Seller breached the title representation (§4.5) in the APA would be brought in New York under New York law, while a claim that the Bill of Sale failed to effectively convey the assets would be brought in Delaware under Delaware law. This could lead to parallel proceedings, inconsistent outcomes, and added litigation cost. It also raises the question of which court has jurisdiction over a claim that straddles both documents.

**Recommendation:**
- Conform the Bill of Sale's governing law and jurisdiction to match the APA (New York law; New York County courts). The Bill of Sale is a standard conveyance instrument; there is no apparent reason it needs to be governed by Delaware law.
- If Seller resists (perhaps because its remaining entity is a New York LLC and the Delaware provision was boilerplate from a different form), at minimum add a provision in the APA clarifying that disputes arising out of both the APA and an Ancillary Document shall be resolved exclusively in the APA's forum.

## 6. No Landlord Estoppel Certificate Required at Closing

**Where:**
- APA §3.2 (Seller Closing Deliverables): Lists the Bill of Sale, Lease Assignment with Landlord Consent, secretary's certificate, officer's certificate, payoff letters, and W-9. No estoppel certificate from the Landlord is required.
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §12.2: Provides that each party shall deliver an estoppel certificate within ten business days of the other's written request.

**Why it matters:**
The Studio Lease is a Purchased Asset (APA §2.1(c), Schedule 2.1(c) item 2). Buyer is paying part of the $18.4M for the leasehold interest. Without an estoppel certificate from Kestrow Canal Properties LLC confirming that (a) the lease is in full force and effect, (b) rent is current, (c) no defaults exist, and (d) the Landlord has no claims against Tenant, Buyer could inherit unknown lease problems (e.g., unpaid CAM charges, prior defaults, disputed rent abatements) that are not covered by Seller's representations.

**Recommendation:**
- Add delivery of a Landlord estoppel certificate (in form and substance reasonably acceptable to Buyer) to Seller's closing deliverables in §3.2, or as a condition to Buyer's closing obligation in §7.2.
- Seller should request the estoppel from the Landlord now, well before the October 30 closing date, to allow time for the Landlord to respond (the lease gives ten business days).

## 7. Survival Period for IP and Other Key Representations

**Where:**
- APA §8.1: Representations and warranties survive for eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date, except §§4.1, 4.2, 4.5, and 4.14 (which survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations).

**Why it matters:**
Eighteen months from the October 30, 2026 closing is April 30, 2028. For a product business with seasonal cycles, this means Buyer may not have a full two product seasons to discover IP defects, inventory quality issues, or customer contract problems before the survival period expires. The IP representation (§4.8), the inventory representation (§4.9), and the material contracts representation (§4.7) all get only 18 months.

**Recommendation:**
- Negotiate to extend survival for §4.8 (IP) and §4.7 (Material Contracts) to at least three years, or to the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations.
- If Seller resists a general extension, at minimum tie IP survival to the term of the underlying licenses and registrations.

## 8. Security Deposit Treatment on Lease Assignment

**Where:**
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §4.1: Security deposit is $65,250 (three months' base rent).
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §4.4: "On an assignment of this Lease permitted under Article 8, Landlord shall transfer the Security Deposit to the account of the assignee, provided the assignee delivers a replacement security deposit or Tenant authorizes the transfer in writing."

**Why it matters:**
The lease requires either (a) Buyer to post a new $65,250 security deposit with the Landlord, or (b) Seller to authorize in writing the transfer of the existing deposit to Buyer's account. If neither happens, the Landlord may be entitled to hold the deposit in limbo or demand a new one. This is a $65,250 cash item that should be addressed in the closing mechanics.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with the Landlord (as part of the consent process) whether it will transfer the existing deposit to Buyer upon assignment or require a new deposit.
- If a new deposit is required, add it to Buyer's closing funds checklist and consider whether it should be treated as an adjustment to the purchase price (i.e., Seller reimburses Buyer) or as a post-closing expense of Buyer.
- Ensure the Lease Assignment document addresses the security deposit transfer expressly.

## 9. Employee Transition: Benefit Plan Liabilities Not Addressed

**Where:**
- APA §6.5: Buyer "may, but is not required to, offer employment" to Business employees. Seller is responsible for "all wages, bonuses, accrued paid time off and other compensation of those employees through the Closing Date."
- APA §2.4 (Excluded Liabilities): Seller retains "all liabilities relating to Seller's employees for periods before the Closing."

**Why it matters:**
The APA draws the line at the Closing Date for compensation, but does not address:
- Employer-side obligations under 401(k) or other benefit plans for pre-Closing service (e.g., matching contributions that vest after closing).
- COBRA notification and subsidy obligations if Buyer terminates employees after closing.
- Workers' compensation claims that arise from pre-Closing employment but are reported post-Closing.
- Unemployment insurance or wage claim liabilities that may follow the business.

If Buyer hires the employees and then they make claims relating to pre-Closing periods, the APA's excluded liability provision (§2.4) should protect Buyer, but the indemnity cap (§8.4) limits recovery to $1.84M for non-fundamental breaches.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with the client whether it intends to hire the Business employees and, if so, how many.
- Add a specific provision in §2.4 or §6.5 clarifying that Seller retains all employer-side benefit plan obligations, COBRA obligations, and workers' compensation liabilities attributable to pre-Closing employment, regardless of when the claim is made or paid.
- If Buyer will hire employees, consider requiring Seller to deliver a benefits plan summary and any pending claim notices as a closing deliverable.

## 10. Non-Compete Enforceability If Seller Dissolves

**Where:**
- APA §6.8: Five-year non-compete binding on "Seller" and its "Affiliates."
- APA §2.2 / Schedule 2.2: Seller retains only its insulated apparel and footwear lines post-closing.
- APA §11.5: Allows Seller to assign and be released (see Issue 2).

**Why it matters:**
After the closing, Seller's remaining business is insulated apparel and footwear — not shell products. If Seller determines that the remaining business is not viable and dissolves the LLC without making an assignment under §11.5, there may be no entity left to enforce the non-compete against. The non-compete binds "Seller," but a dissolved entity cannot be sued. The §11.5 assignment right helps only if Seller actually makes an assignment before dissolving.

**Recommendation:**
- Add a provision requiring Seller to notify Buyer if it intends to dissolve, merge, or sell its remaining assets, and to make the §11.5 assignment (with Buyer's consent, per Issue 2) before any such transaction is effective.
- Consider adding the individual managers or members of Seller as guarantors of the non-compete, or adding a liquidated damages provision to increase the deterrent effect.
- **Open question:** We should confirm with the client whether the non-compete is a key value driver for the deal. If the $18.4M price reflects significant goodwill that depends on Seller not competing, this issue is more acute.

## 11. Lease Use Restrictions: Confirm Buyer's Intended Use

**Where:**
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §5.1: Permitted Use is "executive and administrative offices, apparel design studio, pattern-making and sample-room use (including sewing, cutting and seam-taping equipment customary for apparel sample production), product showroom use by appointment." The lease expressly prohibits "retail sales to the public or production manufacturing."

**Why it matters:**
If Buyer intends to use the space for anything beyond sample-room scale production (e.g., small-batch production runs, retail pop-ups, or e-commerce fulfillment), it could be in breach of the use clause. A use violation is an Event of Default under §11.1(b) and could give the Landlord termination rights.

**Recommendation:**
- **Open question:** Confirm with the client what it intends to do in the space post-closing. If its plans exceed "sample-room use," negotiate a use amendment with the Landlord as part of the consent process, or factor the need for additional/alternative space into the deal model.

## 12. Lease Renewal Option: Calendar the Deadline

**Where:**
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §2.2: Tenant may extend for five years by giving written notice at least twelve (12) months before the Expiration Date (February 28, 2031). Notice must be given by **February 28, 2030**. Tenant must not be in default when notice is given.

**Why it matters:**
If Buyer misses the February 28, 2030 deadline, it loses the renewal option and faces a hard expiration in February 2031. The renewal is on favorable terms (95% of fair market rent), so losing it would force a market-rate renegotiation or relocation.

**Recommendation:**
- Calendar the renewal notice deadline immediately. Assign responsibility internally at the client.
- Ensure no lease defaults exist (or are cured) before the notice date, as a default beyond the cure period would disqualify the renewal.

## 13. Landlord's Attorney Fees for Consent Review

**Where:**
- Brooklyn Studio Lease §8.5: "Tenant shall reimburse Landlord for its reasonable attorneys' fees incurred in reviewing any request for consent, not to exceed $2,500 per request."

**Why it matters:**
After the assignment, Buyer (as assignee/tenant) would be responsible for this cost if it needs to seek further consents from the Landlord (e.g., for alterations, subletting, or a future assignment). The $2,500 cap is modest, but it is a cost item to be aware of.

**Recommendation:**
- No action required beyond noting this in the client's lease compliance file. If the Landlord Consent negotiation involves significant legal work, confirm that the fee will be borne by Seller (as current tenant) rather than Buyer.
