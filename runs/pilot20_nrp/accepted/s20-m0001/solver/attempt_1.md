## Issue 1 (Critical): Ardentis Merger Triggers Change-of-Control Default Under the Note

**Where:** Note § 1(b)(ii) (definition of "Change of Control"), Note § 6(e) (Event of Default), APA § 5.5 (Parent Transaction representation).

**Explanation:** APA § 5.5 discloses that Muvux Orthopedics Holdings, Inc. ("Parent") has entered into a merger agreement dated August 27, 2026 with Ardentis Surgical Partners, L.P., under which an Ardentis affiliate will merge with and into Parent, with Parent surviving. Note § 1(b)(ii)(B) defines "Change of Control" to include any merger or business combination after which the pre-transaction holders of voting equity "hold less than fifty percent (50%) of the voting equity interests of the surviving or resulting entity or its ultimate parent." Note § 6(e) makes it an Event of Default if "Maker, or any Person that directly or indirectly controls Maker, undergoes a Change of Control without the prior written consent of Holder."

Parent directly controls Maker (it is the sole member per APA § 5.1). If the Ardentis merger closes, the pre-merger holders of Parent's equity will almost certainly hold less than 50% of the surviving entity or its ultimate parent. This would constitute a Change of Control of a Person that directly controls Maker, triggering an Event of Default under Note § 6(e). Under Note § 7(a), Pisen could then accelerate the entire $14,000,000 Principal Amount plus accrued interest, and under Note § 7(c) the Default Rate (10% per annum) would apply.

APA § 5.5 represents that the Parent Merger Agreement "does not prohibit Buyer from entering into or performing this Agreement or the Seller Note," but that representation says nothing about the Note's own Change of Control covenant. The APA is silent on whether the Ardentis transaction constitutes a permitted Change of Control under the Note.

**Why it matters:** This is the single most dangerous provision in the deal documents for Muvux. The Ardentis merger is a strategic transaction that Muvux's parent has already committed to. If it closes after the Pisen transaction, Pisen holds an immediate acceleration right over the $14 million note. Muvux would face a forced refinancing or cash payoff on short notice, at a time when its capital structure is in flux due to the merger. The timing of the two closings relative to each other is unknown from the documents.

**Recommended fix:**
- Obtain Pisen's prior written consent to the Ardentis merger as a Change of Control, either before signing the APA or as a condition to the Note being delivered at Closing. This consent should be documented in a side letter or an amendment to the Note.
- Alternatively, negotiate a carve-out in Note § 6(e) for the specific Ardentis transaction (or for any Change of Control of Parent that does not result in a Change of Control of Maker itself).
- Confirm the expected closing date of the Ardentis merger and its relationship to the Pisen Closing Date. If Ardentis closes before the Pisen Closing, the Change of Control has already occurred and the Note should be drafted to reflect that the existing ownership structure (post-Ardentis) is the baseline, not a triggering event.

**Information needed:** The expected or agreed closing date of the Ardentis merger; whether the Parent Merger Agreement contains any provisions addressing subsidiary indebtedness or change-of-control restrictions that could interact with the Note.

## Issue 2 (Critical): APA Expands Product Liability Assumption Beyond the LOI

**Where:** APA § 1.3(c) (Assumed Liabilities) vs. LOI ¶ 4(a)(iii) and ¶ 4(b) (Assumed and Excluded Liabilities).

**Explanation:** LOI ¶ 4(a)(iii) limited Buyer's assumption to "liabilities for product liability, personal injury, product warranty and field corrective action claims relating to products **manufactured and sold by Buyer after the Closing**." LOI ¶ 4(b) expressly stated: "Pre-closing product liability will be retained by Seller as an Excluded Liability. This includes all liabilities for product liability, personal injury, property damage, product warranty, recall and field corrective action claims relating to products **manufactured or sold by Seller before the Closing**."

APA § 1.3(c), however, provides that Buyer assumes "all liabilities for product liability, personal injury, property damage, product warranty and field corrective action claims relating to the Products, **whether manufactured or sold before or after the Closing**."

The APA also adds "property damage" to the assumed categories (the LOI's assumed-liability clause did not include property damage, though the LOI's excluded-liability clause did).

**Why it matters:** In the medical device industry, product liability exposure for spinal implants can be severe (surgical complications, device failure, revision surgery). By assuming pre-Closing product liability, Muvux takes on the risk of claims arising from products that Pisen manufactured and sold—products Muvux never designed, produced, or quality-controlled. The LOI specifically negotiated this boundary, and the APA crosses it. This is a material adverse deviation from the agreed commercial terms.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend APA § 1.3(c) to track the LOI: limit Buyer's assumption to products manufactured or sold after the Closing Date.
- Confirm that pre-Closing product liability remains an Excluded Liability under APA § 1.4 (it is not currently listed as a specific excluded category in § 1.4, though it would fall within the residual "all liabilities of Seller other than the Assumed Liabilities" in the chapeau of § 1.4 if § 1.3(c) is corrected).
- If Seller resists, negotiate a specific indemnity or escrow for pre-Closing product liability claims, with a survival period adequate for the statute of limitations on implant-related claims (which can be long given the latency of some injuries).

## Issue 3 (High): Note § 8(b) Caps Excluded-Liability Recovery, Contradicting the APA

**Where:** Note § 8(b) (Limitation on Holder Liability) vs. APA § 9.5(b) (Caps – Excluded Liabilities uncapped); APA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence).

**Explanation:** APA § 9.5(b) provides that "The Seller Indemnity Cap does not apply to Losses arising from Fundamental Representations, Fraud, and claims under Section 9.2(c) for Excluded Liabilities." In other words, under the APA, Seller's indemnification obligation for Excluded Liabilities is uncapped.

Note § 8(b) provides: "Holder's aggregate liability for all claims under Section 9.2 of the Purchase Agreement, **including claims under Section 9.2(c) for Excluded Liabilities**, whether recovered by set-off under this Section 8 **or otherwise**, shall not exceed the Seller Indemnity Cap, except for Losses arising from Fraud."

The Seller Indemnity Cap is $8,160,000 (12% of the $68M Purchase Price, per APA § 9.5(a)).

APA § 11.9 states: "In the event of any conflict or inconsistency between this Agreement and the Seller Note, the Seller Note shall control."

Therefore, the Note's cap governs. Excluded-Liability claims are capped at $8,160,000 (less any Fraud carve-out), even though the APA purports to leave them uncapped.

**Why it matters:** Excluded Liabilities include the Warning Letter remediation costs (APA § 1.4(c)), pre-Closing taxes (§ 1.4(a)), environmental liabilities (§ 1.4(b)), and pre-Closing employment liabilities (§ 1.4(e)). If Issue 2 is corrected, pre-Closing product liability would also be an Excluded Liability. Any one of these categories—particularly Warning Letter remediation or product liability—could exceed $8.16 million. The "or otherwise" language in Note § 8(b) is especially concerning because it appears to cap not just set-off recovery but all recovery under Section 9.2, effectively overriding the APA's indemnification structure entirely.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend Note § 8(b) to conform to APA § 9.5(b): delete the phrase "including claims under Section 9.2(c) for Excluded Liabilities" so that the cap in Note § 8(b) applies only to Section 9.2(a) and 9.2(b) claims (breach of reps and covenants), consistent with the APA.
- Alternatively, if the parties intend the Note to control (per § 11.9), amend APA § 9.5(b) to acknowledge the cap on Excluded Liabilities and negotiate a higher cap or a specific carve-out for Warning Letter and product liability Excluded Liabilities.
- In either case, the "or otherwise" language should be deleted or clarified so that it does not purport to limit recovery outside the set-off mechanism.

## Issue 4 (High): No Material Adverse Change Condition in the APA

**Where:** LOI ¶ 9(e) (MAC condition) vs. APA Article VII (Conditions to Closing – no MAC condition).

**Explanation:** LOI ¶ 9(e) made Closing subject to "the absence of any material adverse change in the Business." The APA's conditions to Closing (Article VII) do not include a MAC condition. The only conditions are: absence of a prohibiting order or law (§ 7.1), accuracy of Seller's reps and performance of covenants (§ 7.2), and accuracy of Buyer's reps and performance of covenants (§ 7.3).

**Why it matters:** The gap between signing (September 15, 2026) and the expected Closing (December 4, 2026) is approximately eleven weeks. During that period, the Business could suffer a material adverse change—for example, a new FDA enforcement action, a major product recall, loss of a key distribution agreement, or a significant quality system failure. Without a MAC out, Muvux would be obligated to close even if the Business has materially deteriorated, and its only recourse would be a post-Closing indemnification claim (subject to the cap and basket issues in Issue 3).

The conduct-of-business covenant in APA § 6.1 provides some protection (Seller must operate in the ordinary course), but a breach of § 6.1 would still be subject to the indemnification cap and basket.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add a MAC condition to APA § 7.2, modeled on standard formulation: "No Material Adverse Change has occurred in the Business since the date of this Agreement." Define "Material Adverse Change" in APA § 1.1 with appropriate carve-outs (general economic conditions, the effects of the pandemic if relevant, etc.).
- If Seller resists a full MAC out, negotiate at minimum a specific condition that no new FDA enforcement action (beyond the existing Warning Letter) has occurred and no recall has been initiated between signing and Closing.

## Issue 5 (Moderate): Inventory Valuation Methodology Not Specified

**Where:** APA § 2.3(a) (references "book value"); Email Thread,msgs 1–3 (discuss standard cost, QA-hold items, component-level counting).

**Explanation:** APA § 2.3(a) provides that if "the book value of inventory determined by that count" is less than the $6,200,000 Target Inventory Value, the cash purchase price is reduced dollar for dollar. The APA does not define "book value" or specify the valuation methodology (standard cost, net realizable value, FIFO, etc.).

The Email Thread reveals operational complexities:
- Renata Halvorsen's October listing uses "standard cost" (Email Thread, msg 2).
- Marcus Whitfield raises the question of how to value quarantined, MRB, expired, and near-expiry items, stating "The value assigned to those items is something we can work through in the reconciliation" (Email Thread, msg 3, point 3).
- Opened or partially used consignment sets are to be counted at the component level (Email Thread, msg 3, point 2).

**Why it matters:** The valuation methodology directly affects the purchase price. If QA-hold or expired items are included at full standard cost, the count value will be higher, reducing or eliminating the price adjustment in Muvux's favor. If they are excluded or written down, the count value will be lower, increasing the adjustment. The $6.2M target was presumably set with a particular valuation convention in mind, but the APA does not lock it in.

**Recommended fix:**
- In the count memorandum that Priya Venkataraman is preparing (Email Thread, msg 5), specify the valuation methodology: (a) standard cost for finished goods, WIP, and raw materials in good condition; (b) exclusion (or write-down to zero) for items on QA hold, in MRB, expired, or near-expiry; (c) component-level valuation for opened consignment sets.
- Have both controllers sign the count memorandum before the count begins, as agreed in the Email Thread.
- If the parties cannot agree on valuation during the count, the item goes to the disputed-items log and ultimately to the independent accounting firm under APA § 2.3(b).

## Issue 6 (Moderate): Warning Letter – Operational and Regulatory Risk Transfers to Buyer at Closing

**Where:** APA § 1.1(i) (definition), § 1.4(c) (Excluded Liability), § 4.7 (Regulatory Matters rep, Warning Letter carved out), § 6.3 (Regulatory Transfer covenant).

**Explanation:** The Warning Letter relates to Seller's quality system for the Products (APA § 1.1(i)). At Closing, the Regulatory Authorizations (510(k) clearances, establishment registration, etc.) transfer to Buyer (APA § 1.2(a)(ii), § 3.2(b)). Seller remains responsible for remediation costs and corrective/preventive actions until FDA close-out (APA § 1.4(c), § 6.3). However, the establishment registration and the physical quality system will be operating Buyer's products post-Closing.

**Why it matters:** Although Seller bears the cost of remediation, the regulatory relationship with FDA will be in Buyer's name post-Closing. If Seller fails to complete corrective actions promptly, or if FDA escalates (e.g., consent decree, injunction, seizure), Buyer's operations and the Regulatory Authorizations are at risk. The Excluded Liability for "any related FDA enforcement action" (APA § 1.4(c)) provides financial protection, but only up to the cap discussed in Issue 3. Operationally, Buyer would be managing a product line under an active FDA Warning Letter with the former owner performing the remediation.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate a specific covenant in the APA (or the Transition Services Agreement) requiring Seller to provide Buyer with a detailed remediation plan and milestone schedule for Warning Letter close-out, with reporting obligations.
- Add a condition to Closing that Seller has not received any new FDA Form 483 or enforcement communication after the date of the APA.
- Ensure the Transition Services Agreement addresses cooperation on FDA inspections that may occur during the remediation period.
- Consider requiring an escrow or holdback of a portion of the cash purchase price sufficient to cover estimated Warning Letter remediation costs, to be released upon FDA close-out.

## Issue 7 (Moderate): Consigned Inventory Verification Relies Heavily on Seller's Personnel

**Where:** Email Thread, msgs 2–3 (consignment verification approach); APA § 2.3(a) (joint count).

**Explanation:** The agreed approach for consigned inventory (held at hospitals and independent distributors) is that Pisen's field reps perform physical verification using signed count sheets, with Muvux personnel accompanying only at the ten largest accounts by value. Independent distributors provide signed count attestations. The APA requires a "joint" count (§ 2.3(a)), but the operational reality is that the majority of consignment locations will be counted by Seller's own people without Buyer's direct observation.

**Why it matters:** Consigned inventory is inherently difficult to verify accurately (items may be opened, partially used, misplaced, or written off without documentation). If Pisen's reps undercount or misclassify items, the inventory count could be lower than actual, triggering a larger price reduction in Muvux's favor (which is good for Muvux but could create a dispute). More problematically, if the count is higher than actual (e.g., reps include items that are no longer at the account, or include expired/opened sets at full value), Muvux overpays. The 30-day dispute window in APA § 2.3(b) may be insufficient to resolve complex consignment discrepancies across many hospital and distributor accounts.

**Recommended fix:**
- In the count memorandum, specify that the consignment count is subject to reconciliation against the consignment ledger, and that any variance between the physical count and the ledger exceeding a de minimis threshold (e.g., 2% by value) must be investigated before the count is finalized.
- Consider expanding Muvux's on-site presence beyond the ten largest accounts if the consignment inventory represents a material portion of the $6.2M target.
- Document the component-level counting protocol for opened sets clearly in the count memorandum to avoid disputes about valuation.

## Issue 8 (Lower): Note is a Bullet Obligation with No Amortization

**Where:** Note § 3 (Maturity – entire principal due on fourth anniversary); Note § 4(c) (voluntary prepayment permitted without penalty).

**Explanation:** The $14,000,000 Note matures in a single bullet payment on the fourth anniversary of the Closing Date. There is no amortization schedule. Muvux may prepay voluntarily without penalty (Note § 4(c)), but there is no obligation to make interim principal payments.

**Why it matters:** A $14M bullet due in four years creates refinancing risk. If Muvux's credit profile has weakened by year 4 (potentially exacerbated by the Ardentis merger integration), it may face difficulty refinancing on favorable terms or may need to pay the Note in cash from operations. The subordination to Senior Indebtedness (Note § 5) means that if Muvux has senior debt at maturity, the Note's payment is already deferred, but the principal still comes due.

**Recommended fix:**
- This is a business-term issue rather than a drafting defect. If Muvux wants more flexibility, negotiate an optional amortization schedule (e.g., 10% per year starting year 2) or a maturity extension option (e.g., one additional year at Muvux's election upon notice).
- Ensure that the Ardentis merger does not result in Senior Indebtedness that would subordinate the Note's payment indefinitely (see Note § 5(b) – Permitted Payments are allowed unless there is a payment default under the Senior Indebtedness).
