## Summary

I have reviewed the Master Services Agreement (effective March 1, 2025), Schedule B (Service Level Schedule), the draft Amendment No. 1, and the Q2 2026 business review meeting notes (July 16, 2026). I identify seventeen issues, ranging from a potentially deal-breaking insertion in the draft amendment to definitional conflicts that erode Peigirt's service-level protections. The three most urgent items are: (1) the draft amendment's unexplained expansion of exclusivity to all channels worldwide; (2) the conflicting "Peak Season" definitions that, combined with Schedule B § 3.3, eliminate Peigirt's dispatch SLA protection for roughly five months of the year; and (3) the five-year auto-renewal with a narrow 30-day notice window.

## Issue 1 – Draft Amendment § 2: Unilateral Expansion of Exclusivity to All Channels, Worldwide

**Where:** Draft Amendment No. 1, Section 2 ("Replacement of MSA Section 2.4").

**What it says:** The draft deletes MSA § 2.4 in its entirety and replaces it with: *"During the Term, Peigirt shall use Giseind as its exclusive provider of all warehousing, fulfillment and distribution services for Products in any channel worldwide."*

**Why it matters:** The original § 2.4 limits exclusivity to E-Commerce Orders shipped to addresses in the Territory (US and Canada) and expressly preserves Peigirt's right to use other providers for any other channel, product, or geography. The draft amendment expands this to *all* channels (wholesale, retail, B2B, marketplaces), *all* geographies (worldwide), and *all* logistics services (warehousing, fulfillment, and distribution). This is a fundamental commercial shift that would lock Peigirt into a single 3PL for its entire global supply chain for the duration of the Term (including any 5-year renewal).

Critically, the Q2 2026 meeting notes (items 9.1–9.3) record only a discussion of adding Canadian returns processing at Mississauga. There is no reference to any negotiation about expanding exclusivity. The stated purpose of the amendment (Recital C) is to "add returns processing for Peigirt's Canadian e-commerce customers." The exclusivity change is entirely outside that scope.

**Recommended fix:** Delete Section 2 of the draft amendment in its entirety. If Giseind raises the exclusivity point separately, it should be negotiated as a distinct commercial discussion with appropriate consideration (e.g., volume commitments, fee concessions, or a shorter exclusivity period). Do not sign the amendment with this provision included.

## Issue 2 – Conflicting "Peak Season" Definitions Eliminate Dispatch SLA for Five Months

**Where:** MSA § 1.1 defines "Peak Season" as **November 15 through December 31**. Schedule B § 1 defines "Peak Season" as **September 1 through January 31**. Schedule B § 3.3 provides that during Peak Season, "the Dispatch Service Level does not apply and no Service Credits accrue."

**Why it matters:** Under MSA § 17.3(b), the Service Level Schedule takes precedence over the MSA body "with respect to service levels and service credits." The suspension of the Dispatch SLA is a service-level matter, so Schedule B's broader definition (September 1 – January 31, approximately 5 months) likely controls. This means Peigirt has **no enforceable dispatch SLA and no service credit remedy** during the period that includes back-to-school, fall outerwear launch, holiday peak, and New Year's returns—an enormous share of annual volume for an outdoor apparel brand.

The only protection during this window is Giseind's obligation to use "commercially reasonable efforts" (Schedule B § 3.3), which is not independently enforceable as a service level. Peigirt's chronic-failure termination right (MSA § 13.2(c)) is also measured against the Dispatch SLA, so it is effectively suspended during Peak Season as well.

**Recommended fix:**
- Align the definitions. If the commercial intent is to protect only the true holiday peak, conform Schedule B's definition to November 15 – December 31 (or at most November 1 – December 31).
- Alternatively, if Giseind needs a broader relief window, negotiate a reduced (not eliminated) Dispatch SLA during the extended period (e.g., 95% instead of 98%) with corresponding (reduced) service credits.
- Add a carve-out so that the chronic-failure termination right (§ 13.2(c)) is measured across the full rolling six-month period regardless of Peak Season status.

## Issue 3 – Five-Year Auto-Renewal with Narrow Notice Window

**Where:** MSA § 12.2.

**What it says:** At the end of the Initial Term (March 1, 2028), the Agreement automatically renews for successive **five-year** terms unless either Party gives written notice of non-renewal **no earlier than 300 days and no later than 270 days** before expiration.

**Why it matters:** The notice window is only 30 days wide (roughly September 5 – October 5, 2027 for the first renewal). If Peigirt misses it, it is locked in for another five years (through March 2033) with no exit right short of termination for cause. Five-year renewal terms are unusually long for a fulfillment relationship where market conditions, technology, and Peigirt's own strategy can shift materially. The narrow window creates a significant calendar risk.

**Recommended fix:**
- Shorten renewal terms to one or two years.
- Widen the notice window (e.g., 180–90 days before expiration) to reduce the risk of missing it.
- Add a requirement that Giseind send a written renewal reminder no later than 330 days before expiration.
- Consider adding a Peigirt right to renew on modified terms (e.g., with updated SLAs) rather than on the same terms.

## Issue 4 – Giseind's Unilateral Assignment Rights Are Overly Broad and Asymmetric

**Where:** MSA § 16.3.

**What it says:** Giseind may assign the Agreement **without notice to or consent of Peigirt** to (a) any Affiliate; (b) any successor by merger, consolidation, reorganization, or change of control; or (c) any acquirer of all or substantially all of its fulfillment business or assets.

**Why it matters:** MSA § 16.1 requires mutual consent for assignment, but § 16.3 carves out a one-way exception in Giseind's favor. Peigirt selected Giseind based on its specific facilities, personnel, and systems. A change of control or asset sale could result in a different (potentially less capable or less motivated) entity performing Peigirt's fulfillment with no opportunity for Peigirt to object. There is no requirement that the assignee assume the same obligations, maintain the same insurance, or meet the same SLAs. The right extends to *any* Affiliate, which could include a newly formed entity with no operational history.

**Recommended fix:**
- Require Peigirt's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment under § 16.3, or at minimum advance written notice (e.g., 60 days) with a Peigirt right to terminate without penalty if the assignee does not meet specified criteria (financial capacity, insurance, facility equivalency).
- Delete or narrow the "any Affiliate" prong.
- Make the assignment provision mutual or at least add a Peigirt termination right triggered by assignment.

## Issue 5 – Liability Cap May Swallow Inventory Loss Claims

**Where:** MSA § 11.5 (Limitation of Liability) read with § 4.4 (Shrinkage).

**What it says:** § 11.5 caps each Party's aggregate liability at "the Fees paid or payable in the twelve (12) months preceding the event giving rise to the claim," with carve-outs for indemnity obligations (§§ 11.3–11.4), confidentiality breach (§ 9), and gross negligence or willful misconduct. § 4.4 makes Giseind responsible for replacement cost of inventory lost, damaged, or destroyed beyond the 0.25% shrinkage threshold.

**Why it matters:** Inventory loss caused by Giseind's *ordinary* negligence (e.g., a forklift accident, water damage from a facility leak, mis-shipment that is never recovered) is not covered by the § 11.3 indemnity (which addresses third-party claims) and does not rise to gross negligence. It would fall within the § 11.5 cap. If Peigirt holds, say, $5 million in inventory at a facility and Giseind's negligence destroys $2 million of it in a single event, Peigirt's recovery is capped at trailing 12 months of fees—which may be well below the loss. The 0.25% shrinkage threshold in § 4.4 further reduces recoverable amounts.

**Recommended fix:**
- Carve out inventory loss/damage from the § 11.5 cap, or set a separate, higher cap for inventory claims (e.g., 2× trailing 12-month fees or the full insured value of inventory).
- Confirm that the warehouse legal liability insurance required by § 10(b) (full replacement value of Inventory) is the primary source of recovery and that the § 11.5 cap does not limit Peigirt's right to proceed against the insurance proceeds.
- Consider reducing the shrinkage threshold from 0.25% to 0.1% or making Giseind responsible for all shrinkage above a de minimis amount.

## Issue 6 – "Near Real Time" Integration Standard Is Undefined and Unenforceable

**Where:** MSA § 3.4.

**What it says:** Giseind shall maintain the Integration so that "Orders are received and acknowledged in **near real time**" and shipment confirmations are returned within one hour of carrier tender.

**Why it matters:** "Near real time" is not defined anywhere in the Agreement or Schedule B. The only Integration-related SLA is Schedule B § 2.6 (99% of shipment confirmations within 1 hour of carrier tender), which covers the *output* side (confirmations back to Peigirt) but not the *input* side (order acknowledgment). The Q2 2026 meeting notes (item 6.1) record a 40-minute Integration outage on May 14, 2026, during which order acknowledgments were queued. No service credit was triggered because the outage affected order acknowledgment, not shipment confirmations. Peigirt currently has no contractual remedy for delays in order acknowledgment.

**Recommended fix:**
- Define a specific order-acknowledgment SLA (e.g., 99.5% of orders acknowledged within 5 minutes of release through the Integration) and add a corresponding service credit in Schedule B.
- Alternatively, define "near real time" as a specific threshold (e.g., 60 seconds) and make failure to meet it a service-level breach.
- Add an Integration availability SLA (e.g., 99.9% monthly uptime) with credits for downtime exceeding a de minimis threshold.

## Issue 7 – Chronic-Failure Termination Right Is Limited to Dispatch SLA Only

**Where:** MSA § 13.2(c); Schedule B § 6.

**What it says:** Peigirt may terminate if Giseind fails to meet the **Dispatch Service Level** in any three months in a rolling six-month period. No equivalent right exists for chronic failure of Order Accuracy, Inventory Accuracy, Receiving, Returns Processing, or Integration Confirmations.

**Why it matters:** A fulfillment provider could chronically miss Order Accuracy (shipping wrong items) or Inventory Accuracy (losing stock) every month for a year, and Peigirt's only remedy would be the general material-breach termination in § 13.1, which requires a 30-day cure period and a judicial determination that the breach is "material." The Dispatch-only termination right is also undermined by Issue 2 (no Dispatch SLA during Peak Season).

**Recommended fix:**
- Extend the chronic-failure termination right to cover any Service Level missed in three or more months in a rolling six-month period, or at minimum to Order Accuracy and Inventory Accuracy.
- Alternatively, add a provision that chronic failure of any SLA (three months in six) constitutes a material breach per se, eliminating the need to litigate materiality.

## Issue 8 – Service Credit Cap of 15% May Be Inadequate for Systemic Failures

**Where:** Schedule B § 4.2.

**What it says:** Aggregate Service Credits for any Measurement Period shall not exceed 15% of Monthly Fulfillment Fees.

**Why it matters:** Monthly Fulfillment Fees exclude storage, receiving, returns processing, carrier pass-through, and packaging (per Schedule B § 1 definition). If Peigirt processes 10,000 orders/month at an average of 2 units, fulfillment fees are roughly $35,500/month (10,000 × $2.85 + 10,000 × $0.55). A 15% cap yields a maximum monthly credit of ~$5,325. If Giseind has a bad month across multiple SLAs, this cap may provide only token compensation relative to the customer experience damage (missed deliveries, wrong items, lost stock).

**Recommended fix:**
- Increase the cap to 25–30% of Monthly Fulfillment Fees, or
- Add a "super cap" provision: if Giseind misses three or more Service Levels in the same month, the cap does not apply (or is raised to 50%).
- Clarify whether the cap applies per-facility or in aggregate (currently aggregate, which further dilutes the remedy).

## Issue 9 – Peigirt's Non-Binding Forecast Is Used Against It for SLA Exclusions

**Where:** MSA § 2.5 (forecasts are "non-binding"); Schedule B § 3.2(d) (orders above 130% of forecast are excluded from SLA calculation).

**Why it matters:** Peigirt is contractually obligated to provide forecasts, and those forecasts are used as the baseline for excluding excess volume from SLA measurement. If Peigirt under-forecasts (which is common and commercially prudent to avoid over-stocking), Giseind can exclude the excess orders from SLA calculations, effectively giving Giseind a free pass on the very orders that caused the strain. The Q2 2026 notes (item 8.1) show actual volume ran at 94% of forecast—close enough that a modest demand spike could push a month above 130%.

**Recommended fix:**
- Raise the exclusion threshold from 130% to 150% or 175% of forecast.
- Or provide that the exclusion applies only if the forecast was delivered on time and was within a reasonable range (e.g., not more than 20% below the prior year's actual for the same period).
- Or make the forecast binding within a band (e.g., Giseind must staff for forecast ± 15%), with the exclusion applying only above the upper bound of the band.

## Issue 10 – No Consequence for Late or Missing Monthly Performance Reports

**Where:** MSA § 6.3; Schedule B § 5.1.

**What it says:** Giseind must deliver a monthly performance report within 10 Business Days after month-end. No remedy is stated for failure to do so.

**Why it matters:** Peigirt's ability to identify SLA misses, calculate service credits, and raise performance concerns depends on timely reporting. If Giseind consistently delivers reports late (or not at all), Peigirt has no contractual lever to compel performance or to treat the failure as a breach. The Q2 2026 notes indicate reports were delivered on time for April–June, but the structural gap remains.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add a provision that failure to deliver the monthly report within 10 Business Days constitutes a breach, and failure to deliver in two consecutive months constitutes a material breach.
- Alternatively, provide that if the report is not delivered by day 15, Peigirt may calculate service credits from its own data (using the audit right in Schedule B § 3.1) and apply them unilaterally, with Giseind having 10 days to object.

## Issue 11 – Draft Amendment § 1.3(c): 60-Day Hold on Non-A-Stock with No Consequence for Peigirt's Delay

**Where:** Draft Amendment No. 1, § 1.3(c).

**What it says:** Giseind shall hold non-A-stock products "in a segregated area of the Facility for up to sixty (60) days pending Peigirt's written disposition instructions."

**Why it matters:** The clause gives Giseind the right to hold inventory for 60 days but does not state what happens if Peigirt fails to provide instructions within that period. Does Giseind have the right to dispose of the goods? Does the holding period extend? Is Peigirt liable for storage costs during the hold? The silence creates uncertainty and potential leverage for Giseind.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add: "If Peigirt has not provided disposition instructions within sixty (60) days, Giseind shall notify Peigirt in writing and the holding period shall extend for an additional thirty (30) days. Giseind shall not dispose of, liquidate, or otherwise deal with the Products without Peigirt's prior written consent."
- Clarify that storage during the hold period is included in the standard storage fee (not billed separately).

## Issue 12 – Draft Amendment § 1.4: Canadian Returns Pooled with US Returns for SLA Measurement

**Where:** Draft Amendment No. 1, § 1.4.

**What it says:** The Returns Processing SLA (Schedule B § 2.5) applies to Canadian returns "measured with returns received at the Reno, Nevada Facility."

**Why it matters:** Pooling means that if US returns volume is significantly higher (likely), Canadian returns performance can be masked. A chronic problem at Mississauga (e.g., customs delays, different carrier mix) could be invisible in the aggregate number. Conversely, strong Reno performance could offset weak Mississauga performance.

**Recommended fix:**
- Require separate reporting by facility (the monthly report under Schedule B § 5.1 already requires facility-level data, but the SLA measurement is in aggregate).
- Add a facility-specific SLA threshold: if returns processed at any single facility fall below the target by more than 3 percentage points for two consecutive months, Peigirt may require a corrective action plan specific to that facility.

## Issue 13 – Draft Amendment Does Not Update the "Territory" Definition

**Where:** MSA § 1.1 defines "Territory" as "the United States and Canada." Draft Amendment § 2 (if retained) references "worldwide" but does not amend the Territory definition.

**Why it matters:** Even setting aside the exclusivity issue (Issue 1), the Territory definition is used in § 8.4 (data protection obligations apply to personal information "in the Territory") and § 2.3 (returns for customers "in the United States" / "in Canada" per the amendment). If the commercial relationship expands beyond US/Canada (as the draft's worldwide exclusivity would imply), the data protection and other Territory-based provisions would not automatically extend. This creates a compliance gap for personal data of customers in other countries.

**Recommended fix:** If any worldwide expansion is agreed, conform the Territory definition and all Territory-based provisions (especially data protection in § 8.4) to the expanded geography, with appropriate local-law compliance obligations.

## Issue 14 – Shrinkage Measured Annually; No Interim Visibility

**Where:** MSA § 4.4.

**What it says:** Giseind is responsible for replacement cost of inventory lost, damaged, or destroyed "to the extent annual shrinkage exceeds one-quarter of one percent (0.25%) of the average value of Inventory held during the relevant contract year."

**Why it matters:** The measurement is annual. If Giseind loses 2% of inventory value in Q1 and makes it up (or the loss is not discovered) by year-end, the net annual figure may fall below 0.25% and Giseind owes nothing. Peigirt has no interim right to claim for quarterly or monthly shrinkage. The cycle count requirement (§ 4.3) provides some visibility, but there is no contractual mechanism to convert a cycle count discrepancy into a claim before year-end.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add a quarterly shrinkage measurement: if shrinkage in any quarter exceeds 0.25% of the average inventory value for that quarter, Giseind shall make good the excess within 30 days of the quarterly business review.
- Or at minimum, provide that cycle count variances above a threshold (e.g., 1% of SKU value) trigger an immediate investigation and make-good obligation, without waiting for the annual true-up.

## Issue 15 – No SLA or Remedy for Giseind's Account Manager Turnover

**Where:** MSA § 3.1.

**What it says:** Giseind shall designate an account manager as Peigirt's primary point of contact and may replace the account manager on 15 days' written notice.

**Why it matters:** The account manager is the operational linchpin for day-to-day coordination, issue escalation, and business review preparation. A 15-day notice period is short, and there is no requirement for a knowledge transfer, no continuity obligation, and no SLA consequence for frequent turnover. If Giseind cycles through account managers, Peigirt's relationship quality degrades without remedy.

**Recommended fix:**
- Extend notice to 30 days.
- Require Giseind to provide a written transition plan and a minimum overlap period (e.g., 2 weeks) between outgoing and incoming account managers.
- Add that three or more account manager changes in any 12-month period constitutes a material breach (or at minimum gives Peigirt the right to require a senior-level replacement).

## Issue 16 – Force Majeure: 15-Day Threshold for Peigirt's Alternative-Provider Right

**Where:** MSA § 15.

**What it says:** If a force majeure event prevents Giseind's performance of a material part of the Services for more than 15 consecutive days, Peigirt may use alternative providers without breaching § 2.4, or terminate.

**Why it matters:** Fifteen days is a long time for an e-commerce fulfillment operation to be disrupted. During those 15 days, Peigirt is still bound by the exclusivity clause (§ 2.4) and cannot engage an alternative provider without technically breaching the Agreement. For a consumer brand, 15 days of unfulfilled orders can cause significant customer harm, chargebacks, and marketplace penalty points.

**Recommended fix:**
- Reduce the threshold to 5 or 7 consecutive days.
- Add that during any force majeure event exceeding 48 hours, Peigirt may use alternative providers for the affected services on a temporary basis without breach, with the 15-day (or reduced) threshold triggering the right to terminate.

## Issue 17 – Fee Structure: No Volume Breaks or Peak-Season Pricing Protection

**Where:** MSA § 5.1; § 5.2.

**What it says:** Fees are flat per-unit/per-pallet rates. Giseind may increase fees once per year by up to the lesser of 3% or CPI.

**Why it matters:** There are no volume discounts or tiered pricing. As Peigirt's order volume grows (which is the point of scaling a DTC brand), it pays the same per-unit rate. Conversely, there is no peak-season surcharge protection—the flat rate applies, but Giseind's cost structure likely changes during peak (overtime labor, temporary staff). While the current flat rate protects Peigirt from peak surcharges, the absence of volume breaks means Peigirt is not rewarded for growing the relationship. Over a 5-year renewal term with annual increases, the compounding effect is meaningful.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate volume-tiered pricing (e.g., reduced per-unit fee above 50,000 orders/month).
- Cap cumulative fee increases over any renewal term (e.g., no more than 10% over 5 years).
- Confirm that the 3%/CPI cap in § 5.2 applies to all fee components, not just the base rates (the current language is ambiguous as to whether it covers the packaging markup or carrier pass-through—though those are stated as "at cost," which should be verified in practice).
