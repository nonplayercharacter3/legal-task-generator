## Summary

I reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA), the Indemnity Escrow Agreement form (Escrow Agreement), the Kestrelle board consent (Board Consent), and the Pellford insurance certificate cover letter (Pellford Letter). The documents contain several material issues that, if left unaddressed, would leave Seipal significantly under-protected. The two most critical are (1) a direct conflict between the Escrow Agreement and the SPA that effectively caps all of Seller's indemnification liability—including fraud—at the $13.95 million escrow, and (2) the absence of any protection for the Ashgrove Supply Agreement, which represents approximately 38% of Company revenue and which Ashgrove can terminate immediately upon the change of control. Details and recommended fixes follow, in order of priority.

## 1. Escrow Agreement Section 4.3 Overrides SPA Section 9.6: All Indemnification Capped at $13.95M (Including Fraud)

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.3; SPA § 9.6, § 9.5(b), § 11.9

**Issue.** SPA § 9.6 provides that Losses are satisfied first from the Indemnity Escrow Fund and *thereafter* directly by Seller by wire transfer "to the extent the Indemnity Escrow Fund is insufficient or has been released." SPA § 9.5(b) sets the cap for Fundamental Representations, Taxes, and covenant breaches at the full Purchase Price ($186M) and makes Fraud uncapped.

Escrow Agreement § 4.3, however, states:

> "The Indemnity Escrow Fund shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** of the Buyer Indemnified Parties for any Losses under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement. **No Buyer Indemnified Party shall have recourse against Seller, any Affiliate of Seller or any of their respective assets** for any such Losses in excess of the funds then remaining in the Indemnity Escrow Fund."

SPA § 11.9 resolves the conflict in favor of the Escrow Agreement: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and the Escrow Agreement, the Escrow Agreement controls as to the Indemnity Escrow Fund and the matters it addresses."

The practical effect is that Seller's total indemnification exposure for *every* category of claim under Article IX—general reps, Fundamental Reps, Taxes, covenant breaches, and even Fraud—is limited to the $13,950,000 escrow. Once the escrow is exhausted or released (18 months post-closing), Buyer has no further recourse against Seller.

**Why it matters.** Gourain Industrial Partners is a Delaware LLC (likely a PE sponsor vehicle). If it distributes the purchase price to its investors post-closing, it may have no assets to reach anyway. The sole-recourse provision makes that a non-issue for Seller but a catastrophic gap for Buyer: a single material misrepresentation (e.g., undisclosed liabilities, IP infringement, or a tax exposure) that exceeds $13.95M leaves Seipal wholly uncompensated. Fraud—typically the one category parties agree should be uncapped—is also swallowed by the escrow ceiling.

**Recommended fix.**

- Delete or revise Escrow Agreement § 4.3 to conform to SPA § 9.6. The escrow should be the *first* source of recovery, not the *sole* source. Suggested language: "The Indemnity Escrow Fund shall be the first source of recovery for Losses under Article IX. To the extent the Indemnity Escrow Fund is insufficient or has been released, the indemnified parties may recover directly from Seller in accordance with Section 9.6 of the Purchase Agreement."
- If Seller insists on a sole-recourse structure (common in PE deals), negotiate the following carve-outs from § 4.3: (a) Fraud, (b) breaches of Fundamental Representations, and (c) Section 4.13 (Taxes). At minimum, Fraud must be carved out.
- Alternatively, if sole recourse is non-negotiable for general reps, increase the escrow amount to match the 10% cap ($18.6M) so that the escrow and the cap are aligned, and ensure the escrow release date is extended to match the longest survival period.

## 2. Ashgrove Supply Agreement (38% of Revenue): No Pre-Closing Consent, No Closing Condition, No Post-Closing Covenant

**Documents:** Board Consent §§ 1.5, 4.2; SPA Schedule 4.4, § 7.2(d), § 4.10, § 6.3; SPA § 9.5(a) (cap), Escrow Agreement § 4.3 (sole recourse), § 1.1 (Losses definition)

**Issue.** The Board Consent discloses that the Ashgrove Supply Agreement (Master Cooling Systems Supply Agreement dated March 14, 2024) accounted for approximately **38% of Company revenue** for FY2025 and H1 2026. Section 14.3 of that agreement permits Ashgrove to terminate on written notice if a change of control occurs without Ashgrove's prior written consent.

Despite this, the Ashgrove consent is **not** listed on Schedule 4.4 as a Required Consent. The Board Consent (§ 4.2) expressly directs that the Company's officers **shall not contact Ashgrove before the Closing** and will only approach them *after* closing to "seek Ashgrove's written confirmation that the Ashgrove Supply Agreement will continue in effect."

**Why it matters.** At closing, Seipal acquires a company whose single largest customer can terminate its contract the next day. There is:

- No closing condition protecting Buyer (SPA § 7.2(d) only requires the Dunmore landlord consent);
- No covenant requiring Seller or the Company to obtain Ashgrove's consent pre-closing (SPA § 6.3 only covers "Required Consents");
- No post-closing covenant requiring Seller to use efforts to secure Ashgrove's continued participation;
- Limited indemnification protection even if Ashgrove terminates: the loss would be a breach of the § 4.10 representation only if the rep was inaccurate *at closing* (and it technically wasn't—the contract was in force at closing). More likely, the claim would be framed as a failure of the § 4.7 "Absence of Certain Changes" rep or as a general business risk. In any event, recovery is subject to the $13.95M sole-recourse cap (see Issue 1) and the Escrow Agreement's exclusion of "diminution in value" and "lost profits" from the definition of Losses (see Issue 3).

The loss of 38% of revenue could easily exceed $13.95M in enterprise value terms, and the lost-profits exclusion means Seipal may not be able to quantify the claim within the escrow definition at all.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Add Ashgrove's written consent (or a no-termination waiver) to Schedule 4.4** as a Required Consent. This makes it a closing condition under SPA § 7.2(d) and gives Buyer the right to walk if Ashgrove doesn't consent.
- **Amend Board Consent § 4.2** to direct the officers to seek Ashgrove's consent *before* closing, not after.
- **Add a specific post-closing covenant** (new SPA § 6.8 or similar) requiring Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts for 12 months post-closing to secure Ashgrove's continued performance under the Supply Agreement, with indemnification for losses arising from Ashgrove's termination within that period treated as a breach of covenant (capped at Purchase Price under § 9.5(b), not the general 10% cap).
- **Add a specific indemnity** in SPA § 9.2 for Losses arising from termination of the Ashgrove Supply Agreement by reason of the change of control, carved out from the general cap and basket.
- If Seller resists making Ashgrove consent a closing condition (citing the confidentiality provisions), consider a **specific condition** that Ashgrove has not delivered a termination notice as of the Closing Date, combined with a **post-closing covenant** and **enhanced indemnity** as above.

## 3. Escrow Agreement "Losses" Definition Excludes Diminution in Value and Lost Profits

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Losses"); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Losses")

**Issue.** The two documents use different definitions:

| | SPA § 1.1 | Escrow Agreement § 1.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Diminution in value | **Included** | **Excluded** |
| Lost profits | **Included** | **Excluded** |
| Consequential damages | Not expressly excluded (only punitive damages not paid to third party are excluded) | **Excluded** |
| Experts' fees | **Included** | Not mentioned |

Escrow Agreement § 4.3 states: "Claims against the Indemnity Escrow Fund shall be made and determined by reference to Losses as defined in **this Agreement** [i.e., the Escrow Agreement]."

**Why it matters.** Because the Escrow Agreement controls (SPA § 11.9) and is the sole source of recovery (Escrow Agreement § 4.3, see Issue 1), the operative definition of Losses for all indemnification claims is the narrower one. Seipal cannot recover diminution in value or lost profits—precisely the types of damages most likely to arise from the loss of a major customer (Issue 2) or from a material misrepresentation about the business.

**Recommended fix.**

- Conform the Escrow Agreement's definition of "Losses" to the SPA definition, or at minimum add "diminution in value" and "lost profits" to the included items.
- If Seller resists, negotiate a **separate, broader definition** for claims arising from (a) Fraud, (b) Fundamental Rep breaches, and (c) the Ashgrove-specific indemnity (if added per Issue 2).

## 4. Insurance Coverage Gap: Policies Expire January 1, 2027; Outside Date Is January 29, 2027

**Documents:** Pellford Letter §§ 2.1, 3.2; SPA § 8.1(d) (outside date), § 4.15 (insurance rep), § 6.1 (conduct of business)

**Issue.** All of the Company's insurance policies (CGL, auto, workers' comp, umbrella, professional/E&O, property, cyber) have a policy period of **January 1, 2026 to January 1, 2027**. The SPA outside date is **January 29, 2027**. If the Closing occurs on or after January 1, 2027, the Company will have **no insurance coverage** at closing.

The Pellford Letter (§ 3.2) states only that the broker "has begun marketing the January 1, 2027 renewal in the ordinary course" and will "share renewal terms with the Company as they become available." There is no commitment that renewal will occur, no evidence of bound coverage, and no requirement in the SPA that the Company deliver renewal certificates or maintain insurance through the Closing Date.

SPA § 6.1 (Conduct of Business) requires the Company to conduct business in the ordinary course but does not specifically address insurance maintenance. SPA § 4.15 is a representation as of signing and closing that policies are "in full force and effect"—but if they've expired, the rep is breached, and the only remedy is indemnification (subject to the caps and sole-recourse issues above).

**Why it matters.** A data-center cooling company with manufacturing operations, employee exposure, cyber risk, and professional liability needs continuous coverage. A gap at closing means Seipal inherits an uninsured company for potentially 29 days. If a loss occurs during that gap (e.g., a cyber incident, a product failure at a customer site, or a workplace injury), Seipal bears the full cost with no insurance recovery and limited indemnification recourse.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Add a closing deliverable** (new SPA § 3.2(i)): the Company shall deliver certificates of insurance for the renewal policies (effective on or before the Closing Date) for all coverages listed in the Disclosure Schedule, with limits no less favorable than the expiring policies.
- **Add a specific covenant** (amend SPA § 6.1 or new § 6.8): the Company shall maintain its existing insurance program in full force and effect through the Closing Date and shall use commercially reasonable efforts to bind renewal coverage before the current policies expire.
- **Tie the § 4.15 representation** to the Closing Date explicitly (it already does, but add "including that no coverage gap shall exist as of the Closing Date").
- If the parties anticipate closing after January 1, 2027, request that Pellford provide a **bind quote or binder** for the renewal as a condition to closing.

## 5. SPA § 2.5(c): Working Capital Adjustment Depletes Escrow, but Seller's "Restoration" Obligation Is Unsecured

**Documents:** SPA § 2.5(c), § 2.4

**Issue.** SPA § 2.5(c) allows Buyer to recover a downward purchase price adjustment from the Indemnity Escrow Fund. In that case, "Seller shall promptly restore the Indemnity Escrow Fund by the amount so recovered." There is no security for this restoration obligation—no guarantee, no additional escrow, no acceleration right. If Seller (a PE fund) has already distributed the purchase price to its investors, the "promptly restore" obligation is an unsecured promise.

**Why it matters.** If a working capital true-up of, say, $3M is recovered from the escrow and Seller fails to restore it, the indemnity escrow is permanently reduced to $10.95M. Combined with the sole-recourse provision (Issue 1), Buyer's total indemnification protection drops from $13.95M to $10.95M with no further recourse.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add a **time deadline** for restoration (e.g., "within 10 Business Days") and a **default interest** charge for late restoration.
- Require Seller to **pre-fund the restoration** at closing by increasing the initial escrow deposit by the estimated working capital adjustment range, or by providing a **letter of credit** or **parent guarantee** for the restoration obligation.
- Alternatively, structure the working capital adjustment so it is paid **outside** the escrow (i.e., as a separate wire from Seller), leaving the escrow intact for indemnification claims only. This is cleaner and avoids the restoration issue entirely.

## 6. Board Consent § 2.2: Overly Broad Authorization for Company Officers to Accept SPA Changes

**Documents:** Board Consent § 2.2

**Issue.** The Board Consent authorizes the CEO or CFO (each acting alone) to approve "any changes that the [Authorized Officer] executing it approves as necessary or appropriate and **not materially adverse to the Company**." The standard is whether the change is adverse to the *Company*, not to *Buyer*.

**Why it matters.** The Company is jointly and severally liable with Seller for all representations in Article IV (SPA preamble to Article IV). A change that is "not materially adverse to the Company" could still be materially adverse to Buyer—for example, narrowing a representation, adding a disclosure schedule qualification, or modifying a covenant. Because the Company's liability is joint and several with Seller's, any weakening of the Company's obligations also weakens Buyer's indemnification position. The Board Consent gives Company officers unilateral authority to make such changes without Buyer's consent.

**Recommended fix.**

- Request that the Board Consent be amended to require that any changes be "not materially adverse to the Company **or to Buyer's rights under the Purchase Agreement**," or alternatively, that any changes to the Purchase Agreement require **Buyer's prior written consent**.
- At minimum, require that the Company deliver to Buyer a copy of any amended SPA before the Closing so Buyer can assess the changes.

## 7. No Covenant Requiring Insurance Notice of Change of Control

**Documents:** Pellford Letter § 5.1; SPA Article VI (no insurance-specific covenant)

**Issue.** The Pellford Letter flags that "a change in ownership of the Company may require notice to insurers or endorsements to certain policies. Examples are the named-insured and loss-payee provisions." The SPA contains no covenant requiring Seller or the Company to provide such notices or obtain such endorsements at or before closing.

**Why it matters.** If the insurance policies require notice of change of control or an endorsement to add the new parent (Seipal) as named insured or loss payee, and this isn't done, the insurers could argue that coverage is void or that Seipal is not a proper insured. This is particularly relevant for the property policy (loss payee) and the umbrella/excess policies (which often have change-of-control provisions).

**Recommended fix.**

- Add a **closing deliverable** (new SPA § 3.2(j)): evidence that all required notices of change of control have been given to insurers and all required endorsements (named insured, loss payee, additional insured) have been obtained, or that the insurers have confirmed in writing that no such notice or endorsement is required.
- Add a **post-closing covenant** requiring Seller to cooperate in obtaining any endorsements within 30 days after closing, at Seller's expense for pre-closing periods.

## 8. Escrow Agreement § 3.3: Default Investment Is Non-Interest-Bearing

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 3.3

**Issue.** If no Joint Written Instruction designating an investment vehicle is delivered, the Escrow Agent holds the $13.95M in a "non-interest-bearing deposit account insured to the extent permitted by the FDIC." FDIC insurance caps at $250,000 per depositor, so the vast majority of the fund would be unsecured and earning zero interest for up to 18 months.

**Why it matters.** While the economic impact of lost interest is modest (roughly $200K–$400K over 18 months at current rates), the lack of FDIC insurance on the excess is a credit risk on the Escrow Agent. More importantly, the interest is supposed to accrue to the Indemnity Escrow Fund (Escrow Agreement § 1.1, § 3.3), so a non-interest-bearing default reduces the fund available for claims.

**Recommended fix.**

- Ensure that a **Joint Written Instruction** designating a U.S. government money market fund is delivered at or before closing. Add this as a **closing deliverable** or a condition to the effectiveness of the Escrow Agreement.
- Confirm that the money market fund selected is one in which the Escrow Agent is authorized to invest under its own policies.

## 9. 18-Month Survival Period May Be Inadequate for Certain Claims

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1

**Issue.** General representations and warranties survive only 18 months post-closing. For a company in the data-center cooling industry with multi-year customer contracts, complex IP (dielectric fluid management systems, immersion tank designs), and potential environmental or product liability exposure, 18 months may not be sufficient to discover all issues.

**Why it matters.** If a latent IP infringement claim, a product defect causing customer damage, or an undisclosed environmental condition surfaces in month 19, Buyer has no indemnification recourse (subject to the Fraud carve-out, which requires proof of actual intentional fraud—a high bar).

**Recommended fix.**

- Negotiate to extend general survival to **24 months** (a common midpoint in middle-market deals).
- If 18 months is non-negotiable for general reps, ensure that the **IP representation (§ 4.9)** and the **undisclosed liabilities representation (§ 4.6)** have a longer survival (e.g., 24–36 months), given the nature of the Business.
- Confirm that the **outside date** (January 29, 2027) and the **escrow release date** (18 months post-closing) are aligned so that the escrow doesn't release before the survival period ends.

## 10. Pellford Letter: Buyer Has No Rights Under the Insurance Certificates and Has Not Received Policy Copies

**Documents:** Pellford Letter §§ 4.1, 4.2

**Issue.** The certificates are issued "for information only" and "conferr[e] no rights on the certificate holder." Actual policy copies are available only "on the Company's written authorization." Seipal has not received the policies themselves.

**Why it matters.** Without the actual policies, Seipal cannot assess exclusions, sub-limits, retention structures, or change-of-control provisions that may materially affect coverage. For a company with cyber liability, professional/E&O, and product liability exposure in the data-center space, the specific policy terms are critical to the risk profile Seipal is acquiring.

**Recommended fix.**

- Request **full policy copies** (not just certificates) for all seven coverages as a **due diligence item before signing** or, if already signed, as a **condition to closing**.
- Add a **closing deliverable** requiring the Company to deliver evidence that Seipal (or its designee) has been added as an additional insured or loss payee where appropriate, or that the policies contain no change-of-control termination or consent requirement.
