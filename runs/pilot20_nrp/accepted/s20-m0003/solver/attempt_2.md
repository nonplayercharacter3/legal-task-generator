## Summary

The four documents in the matter folder reveal several significant exposures for Seipal as buyer. The most critical is that the Company's largest customer (Ashgrove, ~38% of revenue) holds a change-of-control termination right that the seller is contractually barred from exercising pre-closing, and the SPA does not make that consent a closing condition. Second, the Escrow Agreement as drafted effectively caps Seipal's post-closing indemnification recovery at the $13.95M escrow and strips out diminution-in-value and lost-profits damages, in direct conflict with the SPA's broader indemnification provisions. Third, there is no covenant requiring the Company to maintain its insurance program through closing, and all policies expire January 1, 2027—before the SPA's outside date.

Issues are listed in order of importance to the client.

## Issue 1 – Ashgrove Change-of-Control Termination Right Not Addressed as a Closing Condition

**Documents and Sections:**
- Kestrelle Board Consent, §§ 1.5, 4.2
- Stock Purchase Agreement, Schedule 4.4 (Required Consents); § 7.2(d); § 4.10

**The Problem:**

The Board Consent discloses that Section 14.3 of the Master Cooling Systems Supply Agreement with Ashgrove Cloud Infrastructure LLC permits Ashgrove to terminate on written notice if a change of control occurs *without Ashgrove's prior written consent*. The Board further resolved (§ 4.2) that the Authorized Officers "shall not contact Ashgrove about the Transaction before the Closing" and will only approach Ashgrove *after* the Closing to "seek Ashgrove's written confirmation that the Ashgrove Supply Agreement will continue in effect."

Meanwhile, the SPA's Schedule 4.4 lists only one Required Consent: the Dunmore Realty Partners landlord consent under the Facility Lease. Ashgrove's consent is not a Required Consent. Section 7.2(d) makes the obtaining of all Required Consents a condition to Buyer's closing obligation, but because Ashgrove is not on the schedule, Seipal has no contractual right to refuse to close if Ashgrove's consent has not been obtained.

Management has told the Board that Ashgrove accounted for approximately 38% of the Company's revenue for fiscal year 2025 and for the six months ended June 30, 2026. If Ashgrove exercises its termination right after closing, Seipal would immediately lose more than a third of the target's revenue with no indemnification recourse (because the representation in § 4.10 that Material Contracts are "in full force and effect" would likely be qualified by the Disclosure Schedule, and in any event the Board's own consent shows the issue was known).

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

This is the single largest deal risk in the current document set. Seipal would be paying $186M for a business whose largest customer can walk away the day after closing, and the seller has affirmatively decided not to seek that customer's consent before closing. The SPA provides no protection: no closing condition, no specific representation, no covenant requiring pre-closing consent.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Add Ashgrove's consent to Schedule 4.4** as a Required Consent, making it a condition to Buyer's obligation to close under § 7.2(d). This is the cleanest fix and should be the first ask.
2. If the seller resists (likely, given the Board has already directed that Ashgrove not be contacted pre-closing), negotiate an alternative:
   - A specific representation that the Ashgrove Supply Agreement does not contain a change-of-control termination provision that has been triggered or is likely to be triggered, *or*
   - A covenant requiring the seller to use best efforts (not merely "commercially reasonable efforts") to obtain Ashgrove's written consent or waiver before closing, with a specific closing condition tied to that consent, *or*
   - A purchase price adjustment or specific indemnity (carved out of the cap and basket) if Ashgrove terminates within 12 months post-closing by reason of the change of control.
3. **Raise this immediately with the seller's counsel.** The Board Consent is a document the seller's own client approved; the seller cannot credibly claim it was unaware of the Ashgrove issue. Frame it as: the Board has already decided not to seek consent pre-closing, which means the risk falls entirely on Seipal unless the SPA is amended.

**Flag:** We do not have the text of the Ashgrove Supply Agreement or the Disclosure Schedule in the folder. We need to confirm (a) the exact scope of the § 14.3 termination right (does it require notice? a cure period?), and (b) whether the Ashgrove contract is listed on the Disclosure Schedule and, if so, how the change-of-control provision is disclosed. If it is disclosed, the § 4.10 representation is qualified and Seipal has no breach-of-rep claim. This makes the closing-condition fix even more essential.

## Issue 2 – Escrow Agreement Limits Recovery to the Escrow Fund, Overriding the SPA's Broader Indemnification

**Documents and Sections:**
- Indemnity Escrow Agreement Form, § 4.3
- Stock Purchase Agreement, § 9.6(b); § 11.9

**The Problem:**

SPA § 9.6(b) provides that if the Indemnity Escrow Fund is insufficient or has been released, Seller shall pay the shortfall "directly by wire transfer of immediately available funds within ten Business Days after final determination." This gives Seipal a direct claim against Seller beyond the escrow.

Escrow Agreement § 4.3 states:

> "The Indemnity Escrow Fund shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** of the Buyer Indemnified Parties for any Losses under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement. **No Buyer Indemnified Party shall have recourse against Seller, any Affiliate of Seller or any of their respective assets** for any such Losses in excess of the funds then remaining in the Indemnity Escrow Fund."

SPA § 11.9 resolves the conflict: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and the Escrow Agreement, **the Escrow Agreement controls** as to the Indemnity Escrow Fund and the matters it addresses."

Because § 4.3 addresses the scope of recovery from the Indemnity Escrow Fund and the relationship between that fund and Seller's broader liability, it likely controls under § 11.9. The practical effect is that Seipal's maximum recovery for any indemnification claim (other than Fraud, which is carved out of the exclusive-remedy provision in § 9.8) is $13,950,000—the escrow amount—regardless of the higher caps in SPA § 9.5(b) (which allow recovery up to the full $186M Purchase Price for Fundamental Reps, Taxes, and covenant breaches).

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

- The general indemnification cap in SPA § 9.5(a) is $18.6M (10% of Purchase Price). The escrow is only $13.95M (7.5%). So even for ordinary rep breaches, Seipal loses $4.65M of potential recovery.
- For Fundamental Representations, Taxes, and covenant breaches, SPA § 9.5(b) allows recovery up to $186M. Under the Escrow Agreement, Seipal is limited to $13.95M.
- After 18 months, the escrow is released to Seller (§ 2.4 of the SPA; § 4.4 of the Escrow Agreement). At that point, Seipal's recovery for any non-fraud claim is zero, even if a claim is pending (the pending-claim retention in § 4.4 helps, but only for claims noticed before the Release Date).

**Recommended Fix:**

Delete or revise Escrow Agreement § 4.3 to provide that the Indemnity Escrow Fund is the *first* source of recovery (consistent with SPA § 9.6(a)) but not the *exclusive* source, and that Buyer Indemnified Parties retain the right to pursue Seller directly for amounts in excess of the escrow, subject to the caps in SPA § 9.5. Alternatively, if the seller insists on the escrow as the exclusive source, negotiate:
- An increase in the Indemnity Escrow Amount to at least $18.6M (matching the general cap), or
- A longer escrow holdback period (e.g., 24 months), or
- A carve-out from § 4.3 for Fundamental Representations, Taxes, and Fraud (aligning with SPA § 9.5(b)).

This is a non-starter as drafted. Seipal should not agree to close with the escrow as the exclusive source of recovery when the SPA's indemnification architecture contemplates direct recourse against Seller.

## Issue 3 – Escrow Agreement's "Losses" Definition Excludes Diminution in Value and Lost Profits

**Documents and Sections:**
- Indemnity Escrow Agreement Form, § 1.1 (definition of "Losses"); § 4.3 (last sentence)
- Stock Purchase Agreement, § 1.1 (definition of "Losses")

**The Problem:**

The SPA defines "Losses" broadly to include "diminution in value and lost profits." The Escrow Agreement defines "Losses" narrowly as "out-of-pocket losses, damages, liabilities, judgments, settlements, Taxes, interest, penalties, fines, costs and expenses actually incurred," and expressly **excludes** "diminution in value, lost profits and consequential damages."

Escrow Agreement § 4.3 provides: "Claims against the Indemnity Escrow Fund shall be made and determined by reference to Losses as defined in this Agreement."

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

The most common post-closing indemnification claim in an M&A transaction is that a representation was inaccurate and the company is worth less than represented—a diminution-in-value claim. Under the Escrow Agreement as drafted, Seipal cannot recover diminution in value from the escrow. It can only recover out-of-pocket losses (e.g., a specific tax liability, a specific lawsuit judgment). This dramatically narrows the practical utility of the escrow.

Combined with Issue 2 (escrow as exclusive source), this means Seipal's recovery for a typical "the company isn't what you said it was" claim is limited to out-of-pocket losses actually incurred, up to $13.95M.

**Recommended Fix:**

Conform the Escrow Agreement's definition of "Losses" to the SPA's definition, or at minimum delete the express exclusions of "diminution in value" and "lost profits." If the seller resists, argue that the SPA's definition was negotiated and agreed, and the Escrow Agreement (which is an exhibit to the SPA) should not silently override it. The last sentence of § 4.3 should be deleted or revised to state that claims are determined by reference to Losses as defined in the Purchase Agreement.

## Issue 4 – No Covenant Requiring the Company to Maintain Insurance Through Closing; All Policies Expire Before the Outside Date

**Documents and Sections:**
- Pellford Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, §§ 2.1, 3.2, 5.1
- Stock Purchase Agreement, § 6.1 (Conduct of Business); § 4.10 (Material Contracts); § 8.1(d) (Outside Date)

**The Problem:**

All of the Company's insurance policies (CGL, auto, workers' comp, umbrella, professional/E&O, property, cyber) expire on January 1, 2027. The SPA's outside date is January 29, 2027 (§ 8.1(d)). Pellford has "begun marketing the January 1, 2027 renewal in the ordinary course" and will "share renewal terms with the Company as they become available" (§ 3.2).

The SPA contains no covenant requiring the Company to (a) maintain its existing insurance program through closing, (b) renew the policies on comparable terms if the renewal date falls before closing, or (c) provide evidence of renewed coverage as a closing deliverable. Insurance policies are not listed as Material Contracts under § 4.10 (which covers customer contracts >$2M, supplier contracts >$1M, the Facility Lease, Indebtedness contracts, non-competes, and JVs). Therefore, § 6.1(c)'s prohibition on amending or terminating Material Contracts without Buyer's consent does not apply to insurance.

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

If closing occurs after January 1, 2027 (which is possible given the outside date is January 29, 2027), the Company may be uninsured or may face materially different renewal terms (higher premiums, lower limits, new exclusions). For a company that manufactures and services immersion cooling systems for hyperscale data centers, the absence of professional/E&O, cyber, and property coverage would be a significant operational and financial risk. Seipal would be closing into a coverage gap with no contractual recourse.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. Add a covenant in Article VI (e.g., new § 6.8) requiring the Company to maintain its existing insurance program in force through the Closing, and if any policy renewal date falls before the Closing, to renew on terms no less favorable than the expiring policy (or as reasonably satisfactory to Buyer).
2. Add a closing deliverable: certificates of insurance (or binders) evidencing coverage in force as of the Closing Date, delivered under § 3.2.
3. Alternatively, add the insurance policies to the definition of Material Contracts in § 4.10 so that § 6.1(c) protects them.

## Issue 5 – Buyer Not Named as Additional Insured; Post-Closing Transition Uncertain

**Documents and Sections:**
- Pellford Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, §§ 2.3, 4.1, 5.1–5.2

**The Problem:**

The certificates name Dunmore Realty Partners as additional insured (required by the Facility Lease) but name Seipal only as "certificate holder for information purposes only" (§ 2.3). Section 4.1 confirms that a certificate "confers no rights on the certificate holder." Section 5.1 acknowledges that a change in ownership "may require notice to insurers or endorsements to certain policies," and Section 5.2 offers to coordinate "at or after the closing."

There is no SPA provision requiring the Company or Seller to (a) add Buyer or its affiliates as additional insureds, (b) provide notice to insurers of the change of control, or (c) obtain endorsements before or at closing.

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

After closing, Seipal will own the Company. If the insurance policies contain change-of-control or assignment provisions that require insurer consent (common in commercial policies), coverage could be voidable or terminable upon the transaction. The broker's offer to coordinate "at or after the closing" is voluntary and creates no obligation. Seipal could discover post-closing that coverage has lapsed or is disputed.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. Add a closing condition or covenant requiring the Company to deliver evidence that all insurers have been notified of the change of control and that no policy has been or will be cancelled, voided, or materially modified as a result.
2. Require the Company to obtain endorsements naming Buyer (and/or its affiliates) as additional insureds under the CGL, umbrella, and professional/E&O policies, effective as of the Closing Date.
3. Make delivery of updated certificates of insurance (reflecting the endorsements) a closing deliverable under § 3.2.

## Issue 6 – Board Consent Grants Broad Delegation to Sign Without Further Board Approval

**Documents and Sections:**
- Kestrelle Board Consent, § 2.2

**The Problem:**

The Board authorized the CEO and CFO to execute the SPA "with any changes that the Authorized Officer executing it approves as necessary or appropriate and not materially adverse to the Company." This is an extremely broad delegation. It means the Company's signatory can agree to material changes in the SPA during the negotiation period (between signing and closing) without returning to the Board, so long as the officer subjectively determines the change is "not materially adverse."

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

While this is primarily a governance issue for the Company, it creates a practical risk for Seipal: the Company could agree to changes that are adverse to Seipal's position (e.g., weakening a representation, adding a disclosure schedule item, modifying a covenant) without Seipal having the ability to verify that the Board would have approved such a change. In a dispute, the seller could argue the Company's officer had authority to make the concession, even if the Board would not have.

**Recommended Fix:**

This is difficult to fix contractually (it is the Company's internal governance). However, Seipal should:
- Require that any amendment to the SPA after signing be in writing and signed by all parties (already provided in § 11.3).
- Be alert in negotiations to whether the Company's signatory is making concessions that seem beyond what the Board would have approved, and if so, request evidence of further Board authorization.
- Consider requesting that the secretary's certificate delivered at closing (§ 3.2(h)) confirm that no amendments were made to the SPA after the Board Consent date without further Board approval.

## Issue 7 – Escrow Funds Held in Non-Interest-Bearing Account by Default

**Documents and Sections:**
- Indemnity Escrow Agreement Form, § 3.3

**The Problem:**

If Buyer and Seller do not deliver a Joint Written Instruction designating an investment, the Escrow Agent holds the $13.95M "in a non-interest-bearing deposit account insured to the extent permitted by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation." FDIC insurance is limited to $250,000 per depositor, so the vast majority of the fund would be unsecured.

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

Over an 18-month holdback period, forgoing interest on $13.95M is a real cost (at current money-market rates, roughly $1.2–$1.5M). Additionally, the lack of FDIC coverage for amounts above $250K is a credit risk, though Larchmont Trust Company, N.A. is a national banking association and the risk is low.

**Recommended Fix:**

At or before closing, deliver a Joint Written Instruction (or include it in the escrow agreement itself) directing the Escrow Agent to invest the fund in a U.S. government money market fund. This is a low-friction fix that the seller should not resist.

## Issue 8 – Narrow "Knowledge of the Company" Qualifier

**Documents and Sections:**
- Stock Purchase Agreement, § 1.1 (definition of "Knowledge of the Company")

**The Problem:**

"Knowledge of the Company" is defined as the actual knowledge, after reasonable inquiry of direct reports, of only three individuals: the CEO, CFO, and VP of Operations. This excludes the general counsel, head of business development, controller, and any other officer or employee who might have relevant knowledge.

**Why It Matters to Seipal:**

Several representations are knowledge-qualified (§§ 4.9, 4.10, 4.12). If, for example, the general counsel knew of a threatened IP infringement claim or a customer dispute that the CEO did not know about, the representation would not be breached. The narrow qualifier makes it harder for Seipal to establish a breach of a knowledge-qualified rep.

**Recommended Fix:**

Broaden the definition to include "the chief legal officer" or "general counsel" and, if possible, "the controller." At minimum, add "after reasonable inquiry of such officers and employees as would reasonably be expected to have knowledge of the matter in question." This is a standard ask and should not be difficult to negotiate.
