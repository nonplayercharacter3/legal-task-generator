## Issue 1 – The Note's Definition of "Business" Restrains Zaibeind's Entire Portfolio

**Where:** Note § 1.2 (definition of "Business"); Note § 6.2 (Restrictions on Transfer and Encumbrance); Note § 7.1(d) (Event of Default for breach of § 6.2); APA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); APA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence).

**The problem.** The APA defines "Business" as "the ownership and operation of the Hotel by Seller" (APA § 1.1). The Note redefines "Business" as "all hotels and hospitality operations owned, leased or managed by Maker or any of its Affiliates" (Note § 1.2). Section 6.2 of the Note then prohibits Maker from selling, transferring, leasing, refinancing or encumbering "any material portion of the assets of the Business" without Holder's prior written consent. Because APA § 11.9 provides that the Note "shall control as to payment terms, Maker covenants and Events of Default," the Note's broader definition governs the § 6.2 covenant.

The practical effect: Zaibeind cannot sell, refinance, or encumber a material portion of the assets of *any* hotel in its portfolio—or in the portfolios of its Affiliates—without Stavain's consent. Breach of § 6.2 is an Event of Default with **no cure period** (Note § 7.1(d)), unlike the 30-day cure available for breaches of §§ 6.1, 6.3, 6.4 and 6.5 (Note § 7.1(c)). Upon an Event of Default, Holder may accelerate the entire $3.2 million balance (Note § 7.2).

**Why it matters.** Zaibeind is a hospitality company. It presumably owns, operates, or will acquire other hotels. As drafted, a routine portfolio transaction at an unrelated property could trigger acceleration of the Larkspur House Seller Note. This is an existential constraint on the client's business flexibility that goes far beyond what is typical for seller financing in a single-asset hotel acquisition.

**Recommendation.** Amend Note § 1.2 to define "Business" as "the ownership and operation of the Hotel" (conforming to the APA), or, if Seller insists on a broader definition, narrow § 6.2 to restrict only transfers and encumbrances of the Hotel's assets (the Real Property and the tangible and intangible assets used in the Hotel). Alternatively, add exceptions to § 6.2 for (i) transactions among Maker's Affiliates, (ii) dispositions of individual properties that do not include the Hotel, and (iii) refinancings of the Senior Indebtedness in a principal amount up to a stated threshold. Also negotiate a cure period (e.g., 30 days) for § 6.2 breaches, consistent with the other covenants.

**Facts to confirm with client:** What other hotels or hospitality operations does Zaibeind or its Affiliates currently own, lease, or manage? Are any portfolio transactions (sales, refinancings, intercompany asset transfers) anticipated within the 36-month note term? This will help us gauge the practical risk and frame the negotiation with Seller's counsel.

## Issue 2 – The $410,000 Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Claim Is an Assumed Liability With No Indemnity Backstop

**Where:** APA § 2.3(a) (Assumed Liabilities); APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities); APA § 4.9 (Assigned Contracts rep); APA § 9.2 (Indemnification by Seller); APA § 9.5 (Limitations); Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 2.1(c) item 1; Sched. 4.4 item 1; Sched. 4.9 item 1; Sched. 4.10 item 1; Integration Notes § 3.1.

**The problem.** The Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement is an Assigned Contract (Sched. 2.1(c), item 1). Seller cancelled the contracted room block and garden pavilion event for June 12–14, 2026, and the counterparty has demanded $410,000 (Sched. 4.10, item 1). Under APA § 2.3(a), Buyer assumes "all liabilities and obligations arising under the Assigned Contracts, **whether arising before or after the Closing**." The $410,000 claim arises under the Assigned Contract and therefore is an Assumed Liability.

Because the matter is disclosed on Schedule 4.9, the representation in APA § 4.9 is qualified, and Buyer cannot claim indemnity under § 9.2(a) for a breach of that representation. Because the claim is an Assumed Liability (not an Excluded Liability), the indemnity in § 9.2(b) does not apply. The result: **Zaibeind bears the full $410,000 exposure with no recourse against Seller.**

Compounding the issue, the Integration Notes (§ 3.1) indicate that a "consent request" to assign the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement is still outstanding. It is unclear what consent is being sought if Seller has already cancelled the contract. If the contract is still in force (because the cancellation is disputed), Buyer is assuming a contract with an active $410,000 claim. If the contract is terminated, there is nothing to assign, and the "consent" is moot—but the liability for the cancellation still transfers to Buyer as an Assumed Liability.

**Why it matters.** This is a known, quantified $410,000 exposure (approximately 2.2% of the Purchase Price) that the client is absorbing without indemnity protection. The 45-day response deadline in the demand letter (dated March 2, 2026) expires on or about April 16, 2026—the day after Closing—meaning litigation will likely be filed while Zaibeind is the owner of record.

**Recommendation.** Before Closing:

- (a) Seek a purchase price reduction or an escrow holdback of $410,000 (or a negotiated lesser amount reflecting Seller's stated dispute of the claim) to cover the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective exposure.
- (b) Alternatively, negotiate a specific indemnity from Seller for this claim, carved out from the "disclosed matters" qualification, or reclassify the claim as an Excluded Liability by amending § 2.3(a) to exclude liabilities arising from Seller's pre-Closing breach of an Assigned Contract.
- (c) Clarify the legal status of the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement (in force vs. terminated) and the nature of the outstanding "consent."
- (d) If the claim is resolved between now and Closing, require a corresponding purchase price adjustment.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Does the client have a business reason to want the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective relationship (e.g., the June 2026 event block represents significant revenue)? Is the client willing to accept the $410,000 as a cost of the deal, or should we push for a price adjustment? What is the client's assessment of the merits of Seller's stated dispute of the claim amount?

## Issue 3 – Setoff Against the Note Requires "Final Determination," Creating Cash-Flow Risk

**Where:** Note § 4.1 (Right of Setoff); Note § 4.2 (Final Determination); APA § 9.6 (Setoff); APA § 9.1 (Survival Period).

**The problem.** APA § 9.6 permits Buyer to set off indemnity amounts "finally determined" against payments under the Seller Note. Note § 4.2 defines "finally determined" as either (a) written agreement of both parties, or (b) a final, non-appealable court judgment. There is no mechanism for unilateral setoff of a good-faith disputed claim. Note § 4.1 further limits the setoff to claims "asserted in writing within the survival period" (9 months for most reps).

**Why it matters.** If Zaibeind discovers a post-Closing issue (e.g., a latent building defect, a tax exposure, or a contract breach) and asserts an indemnity claim, but Seller disputes it, Zaibeind must continue making note payments (principal and interest) in full while the dispute is resolved. With quarterly interest payments of approximately $48,000 and a $3.2 million balloon at maturity, the cash-flow impact of being unable to offset a disputed claim is material. The 9-month survival period also means the claim must be asserted in writing by approximately January 15, 2027, or the setoff right is lost entirely.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate an amendment to Note § 4.2 to permit setoff for claims that are "determined" by a binding arbitration award (not only a non-appealable court judgment), or to allow setoff for claims that are "reasonably asserted in good faith" subject to Maker's obligation to restore the setoff amount if the claim is ultimately resolved in Seller's favor. Alternatively, add an arbitration clause to the APA to provide a faster resolution mechanism than litigation.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Does the client anticipate any post-Closing indemnity claims? Is the client comfortable with the cash-flow risk of paying the note in full while a dispute is pending?

## Issue 4 – Nine-Month Survival Period Is Short for a Hotel Acquisition

**Where:** APA § 9.1 (Survival); APA § 1.1 (Fundamental Representations).

**The problem.** Only the Fundamental Representations (Organization, Authority, and Title to Purchased Assets) survive for 6 years. All other representations and warranties—including Condition of Assets (§ 4.8), Compliance and Permits (§ 4.11), Environmental Matters (§ 4.14), Financial Statements (§ 4.5), and Absence of Changes (§ 4.6)—survive for only 9 months post-Closing (i.e., until approximately January 15, 2027).

**Why it matters.** A 42-room hotel with a carriage house, restaurant, bar, kitchen, garden pavilion, and elevator presents numerous systems that may reveal defects only after extended use. Seasonal issues (e.g., roof leaks in winter, HVAC failures in summer, foundation settlement) may not manifest within 9 months. Environmental conditions (e.g., soil contamination, asbestos in the older carriage house) may not be discovered without invasive testing. The 9-month window may expire before the client has operated through a full seasonal cycle.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate an extension of the general survival period to at least 18 months (covering one full seasonal cycle plus a buffer), or carve out specific representations (Condition of Assets, Environmental Matters, Compliance) for a longer survival period (e.g., 24 months). At minimum, ensure the client's post-Closing inspection and testing program is designed to identify and assert claims well within the 9-month window.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Has the client conducted or commissioned any environmental assessment (Phase I/II), engineering survey, or building systems inspection? If so, what were the results and when were they completed? This will inform both the survival period negotiation and the client's post-Closing diligence plan.

## Issue 5 – The Deed Is a Bargain and Sale Deed, Weaker Than the Title Representation

**Where:** APA § 1.1 (definition of "Deed"); APA § 4.7 (Title to Purchased Assets); APA § 7.1(d) (title insurance condition).

**The problem.** The "Deed" is defined as "a bargain and sale deed with covenant against grantor's acts" (APA § 1.1). A bargain and sale deed covenants only that the grantor has not done any act to encumber the title during its ownership. It does **not** warrant that title is free of defects arising from prior owners. Yet § 4.7 represents that Seller has "good and marketable fee simple title." If a title defect predates Seller's ownership, the deed provides no contractual remedy against Seller, and the representation in § 4.7 (while technically breached) would be subject to the 9-month survival period and the $92,000 basket / $1,840,000 cap.

**Why it matters.** For an $18.4 million acquisition, the deed form is a meaningful gap. The title insurance policy required by § 7.1(d) should cover most title defects, but policies have exclusions and limitations (e.g., matters revealed by survey, certain zoning issues, unrecorded easements). If a title problem falls within a policy exclusion, Buyer's recourse against Seller under the deed is limited to Seller's own acts.

**Recommendation.** Request a special warranty deed (covenanting against acts of Seller and those claiming under Seller) in lieu of a bargain and sale deed. If Seller resists, confirm with the title insurer that the policy will cover the specific risks identified in the title commitment, and ensure the policy is in an amount not less than the Purchase Price. The Integration Notes (§ 8.3) indicate the title commitment has been received and exceptions match Schedule 1.1(a), which is reassuring, but the deed form should still be upgraded.

**Facts to confirm with client:** What is the title insurance policy limit? Are there any exceptions in the title commitment beyond those listed on Schedule 1.1(a) that the client finds concerning?

## Issue 6 – No Portfolio-Level Exceptions in Note § 6.2

**Where:** Note § 6.2 (Restrictions on Transfer and Encumbrance); Note § 1.2 (definition of "Senior Indebtedness").

**The problem.** The exceptions to the § 6.2 restriction are limited to (a) ordinary-course inventory and FF&E replacement, (b) the lien securing the Senior Indebtedness, and (c) the Mortgage itself. There is no exception for:

- Intercompany transfers or encumbrances among Zaibeind's Affiliates;
- Refinancings of the Senior Indebtedness in a principal amount *exceeding* the outstanding balance (the "Senior Indebtedness" definition in Note § 1.2 is limited to refinancings "in a principal amount not exceeding the outstanding principal amount being refinanced");
- Sales of individual properties in Zaibeind's portfolio;
- Asset-backed securitizations or similar financing structures.

**Why it matters.** Even if Issue 1 is resolved by narrowing the "Business" definition, the lack of these exceptions could still constrain Zaibeind's financing and portfolio management strategies. For example, if Zaibeind wishes to upsize its senior loan at Larkspur House to refinance and pull out equity, the new (larger) lien may not qualify as "Senior Indebtedness" under the Note's definition, potentially violating § 6.2.

**Recommendation.** Add exceptions to § 6.2 for (i) intercompany transactions, (ii) refinancings of the Senior Indebtedness in any principal amount (or up to a stated cap), and (iii) dispositions of properties other than the Hotel. If the "Business" definition is narrowed per Issue 1, this issue becomes less acute but the refinancing exception remains important.

**Facts to confirm with client:** What is the structure and amount of the senior acquisition financing? Is a refinancing or upsizing of the senior loan anticipated within the 36-month note term? Does Zaibeind engage in intercompany asset transfers or portfolio-level financing arrangements?

## Issue 7 – Ambiguity in the Status of the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 4.9 item 1; Sched. 4.4 item 1; Integration Notes § 3.1.

**The problem.** Schedule 4.9 states that Seller "cancelled" the contracted room block and event for June 12–14, 2026, and that the counterparty "has asserted that the cancellation breaches the agreement." Schedule 4.4 lists a "consent" requirement to assign the agreement to Buyer. The Integration Notes (§ 3.1) state the consent request is "outstanding."

It is unclear whether the agreement is (a) still in force (with the cancellation disputed), (b) terminated by Seller's act (with the counterparty's claim being for damages), or (c) in some intermediate state. If the agreement is still in force, Buyer is assuming a contract with an active dispute and the June 2026 event obligation. If it is terminated, the "consent to assign" is meaningless, and the only issue is the $410,000 damages claim.

**Why it matters.** This ambiguity affects (a) whether Buyer needs to perform under the contract for the June 2026 event (38 rooms × 2 nights + exclusive pavilion use), (b) the scope of the Assumed Liability, and (c) the practical integration plan (the Integration Notes do not address whether the June event will be honored).

**Recommendation.** Before Closing, require Seller to clarify in writing the status of the agreement and either (a) confirm the agreement is terminated and the only exposure is the $410,000 claim, or (b) confirm the agreement remains in force and specify whether Buyer will perform the June 2026 event. If the agreement remains in force and Buyer will perform, the revenue from the event should be reflected in the proration or purchase price adjustment.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Does the client intend to honor the June 12–14, 2026 event block if the contract is still in force? What is the estimated revenue from that block (38 rooms × 2 nights + pavilion)? This affects the economic analysis of the $410,000 claim.

## Issue 8 – Proration Language for Closing-Night Room Revenue Is Imprecise

**Where:** APA § 2.7 (Prorations); Bill of Sale § 1.2 (effective time).

**The problem.** APA § 2.7 provides that prorations are "as of 11:59 p.m. on the day before the Closing Date" and that "[r]oom revenue for the night that includes the Closing shall be allocated to Seller." The Bill of Sale takes effect at "12:01 a.m. on the Closing Date" (Bill of Sale § 1.2). There is a one-minute gap (12:00 a.m.) between the proration cut-off and the effective transfer. More substantively, "the night that includes the Closing" is ambiguous: in hotel terminology, a "night" is defined by check-in date. A guest who checks in on April 14 and checks out on April 15 occupies the room during the Closing (April 15). Is that the "night that includes the Closing"? The apparent intent is that Seller keeps the revenue for the last night before the transfer (the night of April 14), but the language could be read differently.

**Why it matters.** This is a minor issue in isolation (one night's room revenue for 42 rooms), but imprecise proration language can create disputes, particularly if the Closing is delayed or moved to a different time of day.

**Recommendation.** Amend § 2.7 to state explicitly: "Room revenue for guest nights with a check-in date on or before the day preceding the Closing Date shall be allocated to Seller. Room revenue for guest nights with a check-in date on or after the Closing Date shall be allocated to Buyer." Align the Bill of Sale effective time with the proration cut-off (e.g., both at 12:00 a.m. or both at 11:59 p.m.).

## Issue 9 – Employee Transition Creates a Technical Employment Gap

**Where:** APA § 5.4 (Employees); APA § 2.4(d) (Excluded Liabilities – employment); Integration Notes § 6.

**The problem.** Section 5.4 provides that "Seller shall terminate the employment of all Hotel employees effective as of the Closing" and that "Buyer may, but is not obligated to, offer employment to any employee of the Hotel effective as of the Closing." The Integration Notes (§ 6.1) confirm Buyer intends to offer employment to "substantially all" employees. However, the structure creates a moment where all employees are terminated by Seller and then re-hired by Buyer. Even if the offers are effective "as of the Closing," there is a technical break in employment.

**Why it matters.** The break could affect (a) continuity of service for benefits vesting purposes, (b) accrued but unused PTO (which is an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(d), so Seller must pay it—but if the employee is re-hired by Buyer, the employee might expect continuity), (c) workers' compensation history, and (d) morale and retention (the General Manager has "agreed in principle to stay on" per Integration Notes § 6.3, but terms are not finalized). Additionally, if any employee declines Buyer's offer, there is no obligation on Buyer to hire, but the hotel may be short-staffed on day one.

**Recommendation.** Coordinate with HR to ensure offer letters are unconditional and effective at 12:01 a.m. on the Closing Date, with no gap in payroll. Confirm that Seller will pay all accrued PTO and other termination amounts before the Closing (as required by § 5.4 and § 2.4(d)). Finalize the General Manager's employment terms before Closing to avoid a leadership vacuum. Consider whether a "hire of record" date continuity provision should be included in the offer letters.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Have all offer letters been finalized and approved? What are the terms for the General Manager? Has the client confirmed with its benefits broker that there will be no gap in health and other benefits coverage (Integration Notes § 6.4, action item 4)?

## Issue 10 – Ten-Day Grace Period for Interest Payments Is Tight

**Where:** Note § 7.1(b) (Event of Default – payment); Note § 2.5 (Default Interest; Late Charge); Note § 2.1 (Interest payment dates).

**The problem.** Interest is payable quarterly on the 15th of July, October, January, and April (Note § 2.1). If any interest payment is not made within 10 days after its due date, it is an Event of Default (Note § 7.1(b)). A 3% late charge also applies to any interest not paid within 10 days (Note § 2.5). While § 7.2 requires Holder to give written notice before accelerating for non-insolvency defaults, the default itself is triggered after only 10 days.

**Why it matters.** The first interest payment is due July 15, 2026—only 3 months after Closing. If there is any wire delay, accounting error, or treasury misallocation, Zaibeind could be in default within 10 days. Given the Note's broad covenants and the cross-default to the Senior Indebtedness (Note § 7.1(e)), a technical payment default could cascade.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate an extension of the grace period to 15 or 20 Business Days. Alternatively, add a provision that a payment made within the grace period (with the late charge) does not constitute an Event of Default—only a failure to pay within the grace period triggers default. Ensure the client's treasury function has the payment calendar and wire instructions well before the first payment date.

**Facts to confirm with client:** What is the client's standard treasury process for making scheduled debt service payments? Is there any risk of the first quarterly payment (July 15, 2026) being delayed due to integration-related accounting transitions?
