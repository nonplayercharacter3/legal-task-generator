## Issue 1 – $410,000 Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Claim Is Likely an Assumed Liability

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 2.3(a) (Assumed Liabilities)
- APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities)
- APA § 4.6 (Absence of Changes representation)
- APA § 9.2(a), 9.5 (Indemnity; limitations)
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 2.1(c) item 1 (Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement listed as an Assigned Contract)
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 4.9 item 1 (Seller's March 1, 2026 cancellation disclosed)
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 4.10 item 1 ($410,000 demand letter dated March 2, 2026)

**Explanation:**
APA § 2.3(a) assumes "all liabilities and obligations arising under the Assigned Contracts, **whether arising before or after the Closing**." The Hudson Valley Wedding Collective Agreement is an Assigned Contract (Sched. 2.1(c), item 1). Seller cancelled the June 12–14, 2026 event block (38 rooms, two nights, exclusive garden pavilion) by notice dated March 1, 2026, and the counterparty demanded $410,000 on March 2, 2026. The claim arises directly under an Assigned Contract and was disclosed as a pre-closing breach. Under the plain language of § 2.3(a), this is an Assumed Liability, not an Excluded Liability. The Excluded Liabilities in § 2.4 do not carve out pre-closing breaches of Assigned Contracts.

Zaibeind's only potential recourse is an indemnity claim under § 9.2(a) for inaccuracy of the § 4.6 representation (no material adverse change since December 31, 2025). The § 4.9 representation (no material breach of Assigned Contracts) is expressly qualified by the Disclosure Schedule, so it cannot support an indemnity claim. Even if the § 4.6 claim succeeds, it is subject to the $92,000 deductible and the $1,840,000 cap in § 9.5. And recovery via setoff against the Note is constrained by Issue 3 below.

**Why it matters:**
Zaibeind pays $18.4 million for the hotel and then inherits a $410,000 claim caused by Seller's own pre-closing decision to cancel. The net economic cost to Zaibeind could be up to $410,000 (less any successful indemnity recovery, which is uncertain and procedurally difficult).

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing (if still possible):** Negotiate an amendment to APA § 2.3(a) to exclude pre-closing liabilities arising from Seller's breach of an Assigned Contract, or add a specific indemnity in § 9.2 for the Hudson Valley Wedding Collective claim. Alternatively, negotiate a purchase price reduction of $410,000.
- **If the APA is already signed and the deal is proceeding to Closing on schedule:** Confirm with the client whether the $410,000 was factored into the purchase price negotiations. If not, raise the issue with Seller's counsel immediately and seek either (a) a specific side-letter indemnity from Seller for this claim, or (b) a reduction in the cash portion of the Purchase Price at Closing.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Was the Wedding Collective claim known to or considered by the client during pricing? (2) Does the client believe the claim is meritorious or overvalued? (3) Is the client willing to accept the risk in exchange for other concessions?

## Issue 2 – Note Negative Covenant Restricts Zaibeind's Entire Hospitality Portfolio

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 1.2 (definition of "Business")
- Note § 6.2 (Restrictions on Transfer and Encumbrance)
- Note § 7.1(d) (breach of § 6.2 is an Event of Default)
- APA § 1.1 (definition of "Business" – limited to the Hotel)

**Explanation:**
The Note defines "Business" as "all hotels and hospitality operations owned, leased or managed by Maker or any of its Affiliates" (Note § 1.2). Section 6.2 then prohibits Maker, without Holder's prior written consent, from selling, transferring, leasing, refinancing or encumbering "any material portion of the assets of the Business." The exceptions cover only ordinary-course inventory/FF&E sales, the Senior Indebtedness lien, and the Mortgage itself.

This is dramatically broader than the APA's definition of "Business" (the ownership and operation of the Larkspur House by Seller). If Zaibeind owns or manages other hotels, the Note gives Seller a consent right over Zaibeind's portfolio-level transactions. A breach is an Event of Default under § 7.1(d) with no cure period.

**Why it matters:**
For a hospitality platform like Zaibeind, the ability to sell, refinance or restructure properties across the portfolio is core to the business model. A seller's consent right over "any material portion" of all hospitality assets, backed by an uncured Event of Default, is an extraordinary restriction that could paralyze portfolio decisions for the three-year term of the Note. Even if Zaibeind currently owns only the Larkspur House, the restriction will apply to any future acquisitions made by the entity or its Affiliates during the Note's term.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend Note § 1.2 to define "Business" as the Larkspur House only (consistent with the APA), or narrow § 6.2 to restrict only dispositions or encumbrances of the Real Property or the Larkspur House assets. If the client anticipates growing the portfolio through the same entity or Affiliates, this is essential.
- **If the Note is already in final form:** Assess whether the client's current portfolio and near-term plans would actually trigger the covenant. If so, seek a consent or amendment.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Does Zaibeind or any Affiliate currently own, lease or manage any other hotel or hospitality property? (2) Are there any planned dispositions, refinancings or new acquisitions in the next 36 months that could be characterized as a "material portion" of the Business? (3) Is the entity structure such that future acquisitions would be held by Zaibeind or its Affiliates?

## Issue 3 – "Final Determination" Standard Eliminates Practical Setoff

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 4.1 (Right of Setoff)
- Note § 4.2 (Final Determination defined)
- APA § 9.6 (Setoff cross-reference)
- APA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence – Note controls on payment terms)

**Explanation:**
APA § 9.6 permits Buyer to set off indemnity amounts against Note payments "on the terms set forth in the Seller Note." APA § 11.9 provides that the Note controls over the APA as to payment terms. Note § 4.2 defines "finally determined" as either (a) written agreement of both parties or (b) a final, non-appealable court judgment. There is no provision for setoff based on a good-faith disputed claim, an arbitration award pending appeal, or a settlement in principle.

**Why it matters:**
In practice, Seller is unlikely to agree in writing to pay a disputed indemnity claim. Litigation to a final non-appealable judgment in New York state or federal court could take three to five years. During that time, Zaibeind must continue making quarterly interest payments and the balloon principal payment on the Note without the ability to offset known or probable indemnity recoveries. This converts the setoff right from a practical credit enhancement into a theoretical one.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend Note § 4.2 to add a third prong: an amount is "finally determined" when a tribunal (court or arbitration panel) has rendered an award or judgment that is not subject to further appeal as of right, even if the losing party has filed a discretionary appeal. Alternatively, permit setoff for claims that are the subject of a pending arbitration or litigation that has passed the summary judgment or dispositive motion stage.
- **If the Note is in final form:** Evaluate whether the client's risk tolerance accommodates this limitation. The client should understand that its primary remedy for Seller's post-closing breaches is to continue making Note payments and then pursue indemnity recovery separately.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Does the client have any existing disputes with Seller that it expects to assert as indemnity claims? (2) Is the client amenable to a multi-year litigation timeline as the practical cost of its remedy?

## Issue 4 – Cross-Default to Senior Indebtedness Creates Cascading Risk

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 7.1(e) (Event of Default: acceleration of Senior Indebtedness)
- Note § 7.2 (Acceleration remedies)
- Note § 1.2 (definition of "Senior Indebtedness")

**Explanation:**
If a default occurs under the Senior Indebtedness that results in its acceleration, the Seller Note is automatically in default. Note § 7.2 provides that for insolvency events (§ 7.1(g)), acceleration is automatic; for other events (including § 7.1(e)), Holder may accelerate by written notice. Once accelerated, Seller can foreclose on the Mortgage (subject to the subordination in § 8) and seek collection costs under § 7.3.

**Why it matters:**
A technical or curable default under the senior loan (e.g., a late financial delivery, a minor covenant breach) that triggers acceleration would simultaneously put the Seller Note in default. This creates a scenario where a single senior-lender dispute cascades into a Seller Note default, potentially giving Seller leverage to demand concessions or accelerate the $3.2 million balance. The subordination in Note § 8 protects the Senior Lender's priority but does not prevent Seller from exercising remedies against other assets or seeking a judgment.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend Note § 7.1(e) to require that the senior default be (a) uncured after the applicable cure period under the senior loan documents, and (b) material (e.g., involving a payment default or a default that has actually resulted in acceleration by the Senior Lender, not merely a cross-default trigger in the senior loan).
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) What are the specific cross-default and acceleration provisions in the senior loan documents? (2) Is there a cure period under the senior loan before acceleration becomes effective?

## Issue 6 – Self-Reporting Covenant Creates a Default Trap

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 6.5 (Notice of Default within 5 Business Days)
- Note § 7.1(c) (breach of § 6.5 is an Event of Default after 30-day cure)

**Explanation:**
Note § 6.5 requires Maker to give Holder written notice of any Event of Default, "or of any event that with notice or lapse of time would become an Event of Default," within five (5) Business Days after an officer becomes aware of it. Failure to comply is an Event of Default under § 7.1(c) (subject to a 30-day cure period after Holder's notice).

**Why it matters:**
This is a self-reporting obligation that covers not just actual defaults but *potential* defaults. If, for example, a quarterly financial delivery is delayed by six days (within the 45-day window but the officer realizes it will be late), the officer may have a reporting obligation. The five-business-day window is short, and the scope ("any event that with notice or lapse of time would become an Event of Default") is vague. A failure to report is itself a default, creating a compounding problem: the original issue plus a new default for failing to disclose it.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend Note § 6.5 to (a) limit the reporting obligation to actual Events of Default (not potential ones), (b) extend the deadline to ten (10) Business Days, and (c) add a materiality qualifier (e.g., only events that a reasonable officer would expect to result in an Event of Default).
- **Internally:** If the Note is not amended, implement an internal protocol requiring the Controller or General Counsel to review potential covenant compliance issues weekly during the Note term and escalate to an officer within two business days of identification.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Who will be the designated officer responsible for monitoring Note covenants? (2) Does the client have existing compliance monitoring infrastructure for debt covenants?

## Issue 7 – Senior Indebtedness Definition Constrains Refinancing Flexibility

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 1.2 (definition of "Senior Indebtedness")
- Note § 6.2 (negative covenant)
- Note § 8 (subordination)

**Explanation:**
"Senior Indebtedness" is defined as the senior acquisition financing "together with any refinancing of that financing in a principal amount not exceeding the outstanding principal amount being refinanced." If Zaibeind refinances the senior loan with a larger amount (e.g., a cash-out refi to fund capital improvements or another acquisition), the excess principal does not qualify as "Senior Indebtedness." The new, larger lien would not be recognized under the Note's subordination structure, and the additional encumbrance on the Real Property could constitute a breach of § 6.2 (encumbering assets without Holder's consent).

**Why it matters:**
Hotel acquisitions often require follow-on capital for renovations, or the owner may want to refinance at a better rate with additional proceeds. This definition could prevent a cash-out refi without Seller's consent, or force Zaibeind to structure any refinancing to stay within the original principal amount.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend the "Senior Indebtedness" definition to permit refinancings in a principal amount up to a specified percentage above the original (e.g., 125%) or to permit any refinancing so long as the new lender's lien is senior to the Mortgage.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) What is the original principal amount of the senior acquisition financing? (2) Are there plans to refinance or increase senior debt during the 36-month Note term? (3) Does the client anticipate any capital expenditure program that might require additional leverage?

## Issue 8 – Nine-Month Survival Period Is Short for a Hotel Acquisition

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 9.1 (Survival Period)
- APA § 1.1 (Fundamental Representations – only Organization, Authority, Title survive 6 years)

**Explanation:**
All representations and warranties other than the Fundamental Representations (Organization, Authority, Title to Purchased Assets) survive for only nine (9) months after Closing. This includes the representation regarding condition of assets (§ 4.8), environmental matters (§ 4.14), taxes (§ 4.12), assigned contracts (§ 4.9), and absence of changes (§ 4.6).

**Why it matters:**
For a 42-room hotel with three buildings, mechanical systems, and environmental exposure, nine months may be insufficient to discover latent defects (e.g., roof leaks that manifest in winter, HVAC failures, environmental contamination). The tax representation is particularly time-sensitive: a pre-closing tax issue (e.g., uncollected occupancy tax) may not surface until an audit that occurs after nine months.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Seek to extend the general survival period to 18–24 months, or to add specific longer survival periods for the tax (§ 4.12) and environmental (§ 4.14) representations (e.g., until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations).
- **If the APA is in final form:** Ensure that all pre-closing tax and environmental due diligence is completed before Closing so that issues are identified while the survival period is still running. Consider purchasing a pre-closing environmental assessment if one has not been done.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Has a Phase I (and if warranted, Phase II) environmental site assessment been completed? (2) Has a tax due diligence review of pre-closing occupancy tax, sales tax, and payroll tax compliance been performed? (3) Is the client comfortable with the nine-month window given the asset type?

## Issue 9 – Broad "Before or After Closing" Assumption Language in § 2.3(a)

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 2.3(a)
- APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities – no specific carve-out for pre-closing contract breaches)

**Explanation:**
As noted in Issue 1, § 2.3(a) assumes all liabilities under Assigned Contracts "whether arising before or after the Closing." This is broader than market standard, where the assumption is typically limited to post-closing performance obligations, with pre-closing breaches remaining with Seller. The Excluded Liabilities in § 2.4 do not specifically carve out pre-closing breaches of Assigned Contracts.

**Why it matters:**
This structural issue is the root cause of Issue 1 and could create additional exposure for any other pre-closing breaches of Assigned Contracts that are not specifically disclosed. For example, the Valley Linen Supply late payment (Sched. 4.9, item 2) is disclosed, but if there were an undisclosed pre-closing breach of another Assigned Contract, it would still be an Assumed Liability.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Amend § 2.3(a) to limit the assumption to liabilities arising from Buyer's post-closing performance under the Assigned Contracts, with pre-closing breaches remaining as Excluded Liabilities.
- **If the APA is in final form:** Conduct a thorough review of all Assigned Contracts for any pre-closing breaches or disputes beyond those disclosed in Schedule 4.9. Request that Seller confirm in writing that no other pre-closing breaches exist.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Has the client's deal team reviewed all 13 Assigned Contracts for pre-closing issues? (2) Are there any vendor relationships with known disputes not reflected in the Disclosure Schedule?

## Issue 10 – Ambiguity in Assigning the Note to an Affiliate

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 9.3 (Assignment by Maker)
- APA § 11.3 (Assignment by Buyer)

**Explanation:**
APA § 11.3 permits Buyer to assign "its rights" to Affiliates or "collaterally to its lenders" without consent. Note § 9.3 permits Maker to assign "its obligations" to "a permitted assignee of the Purchase Agreement under Section 11.3 of the Purchase Agreement that assumes this Note in writing." The cross-reference is awkward: § 11.3 addresses assignment of *rights*, while the Note addresses assignment of *obligations*. It is unclear whether an Affiliate that is a "permitted assignee" of Buyer's rights under § 11.3 is automatically a permitted assignee of the Note's obligations, or whether Holder's separate consent is required.

**Why it matters:**
If Zaibeind wishes to restructure by transferring the Note (and the underlying assets) to an Affiliate (e.g., a new operating LLC), ambiguity in the assignment provision could give Seller a basis to claim consent is required, creating friction or a potential default.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Clarify Note § 9.3 to expressly permit assignment of the Note to any Affiliate of Maker that assumes the Note in writing, without Holder's consent, consistent with APA § 11.3.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Does the client anticipate any corporate restructuring during the Note term that would involve transferring the Note or the Hotel assets to an Affiliate? (2) Is the current entity structure final, or are there plans to create a new operating entity?

## Issue 11 – Broad Survey/Inspection Carve-Out in Permitted Encumbrances

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 1.1(a) item 4
- APA § 1.1 (definition of "Permitted Encumbrances")
- APA § 4.7 (Title representation)

**Explanation:**
Schedule 1.1(a), item 4 lists as a Permitted Encumbrance: "Any state of facts that an accurate survey or personal inspection of the Real Property would show, provided it does not render title unmarketable or materially impair the use or operation of the Hotel." This is a broad catch-all that effectively discloses any physical condition of the property, subject only to the qualifiers of marketability and material impairment.

**Why it matters:**
This carve-out could encompass encroachments, boundary discrepancies, unpermitted structures, or other physical issues that a survey would reveal. While the qualifiers provide some protection, "materially impair" is a high threshold. A minor encroachment or a set-back issue that does not "materially impair" use would be a Permitted Encumbrance, meaning Seller's title representation (§ 4.7) is not breached and Zaibeind has no indemnity claim.

**Recommendation:**
- **Before Closing:** Review the title commitment and any survey results. Confirm with the title insurer that the exceptions in the commitment match Schedule 1.1(a) (the Integration Notes § 8.3 indicate they do). If a survey has been ordered, review it for any issues before Closing.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Has a current survey been commissioned? (2) Are there any known physical issues with the property (encroachments, boundary disputes, unpermitted additions)?

## Issue 12 – Financial Reporting Covenants Are an Ongoing Compliance Burden

**Documents and Sections:**
- Note § 6.1 (Financial Reporting)
- Note § 7.1(c) (breach is Event of Default after 30-day cure)

**Explanation:**
Maker must deliver quarterly unaudited statements within 45 days of quarter-end and annual statements within 120 days of year-end, certified by an officer as fairly presenting results. Breach is an Event of Default under § 7.1(c) if not cured within 30 days after Holder's notice.

**Why it matters:**
The 45-day quarterly deadline is tight for a hotel with complex revenue (rooms, F&B, events, OTA commissions). If the Controller's team is stretched during integration, a late delivery could trigger a default (subject to the 30-day cure). The certification requirement means an officer must personally sign off on the accuracy of the financials.

**Recommendation:**
- **Internally:** Calendar the delivery deadlines (July 30, October 29, January 28, April 27 for quarterly; April 14 for annual) and assign ownership to the Controller. Build in internal review time so the officer certification is not rushed.
- **Before Closing (if possible):** Seek to extend the quarterly deadline to 60 days.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Does the client's accounting team have the capacity to produce hotel-specific quarterly financials within 45 days? (2) Who will be the certifying officer?

## Issue 13 – Guest Slip-and-Fall Claim: Confirm Seller Retains Responsibility

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 4.10 item 2
- APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities – catch-all)
- APA § 2.2 / Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 2.2 item 4 (Seller's insurance policies are Excluded Assets)
- APA § 9.2(b) (indemnity for Excluded Liabilities)

**Explanation:**
A guest reported a slip-and-fall injury in October 2025 on the carriage house exterior stairs. The claim was tendered to Seller's GL insurer, which acknowledged coverage subject to a $5,000 deductible. No action has been filed. Seller's insurance policies are Excluded Assets (Sched. 2.2, item 4), so the rights to proceeds do not transfer to Buyer.

The claim is not an Assumed Liability (it does not arise under an Assigned Contract, a reservation obligation, or post-closing operation). It is therefore an Excluded Liability, and Seller must indemnify Buyer under § 9.2(b). This indemnity is not subject to the basket or cap in § 9.5.

**Why it matters:**
The financial exposure is limited (potentially $5,000 deductible plus any amount above the policy limit), but the practical issue is ensuring Seller actually handles the claim. If the claim escalates or the insurer denies coverage, Seller is responsible, but Zaibeind may initially face the injured guest.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with Seller (through counsel) that Seller will handle the claim through its insurer and will indemnify Buyer for any amounts paid. Request written confirmation at or before Closing.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Has the injured guest made any direct contact with the property or Buyer's team? (2) What is the status of the insurance claim?

## Issue 14 – Employee Transition and Potential NY WARN Act Exposure

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 5.4 (Employees)
- APA § 2.4(d) (WARN Act obligations are Excluded Liabilities)
- Disclosure Schedule, Sched. 4.13 (30 employees listed)
- Integration Notes § 6.1–6.2

**Explanation:**
Seller will terminate all 30 Hotel employees effective as of the Closing (April 15, 2026). Buyer intends to offer employment to "substantially all" of them, with offer letters to be issued by April 1 and onboarding on April 9–10. WARN Act obligations are Excluded Liabilities (§ 2.4(d)), so Seller bears the legal risk. However, New York's WARN Act (NY Labor Law § 861) applies to employers with 25 or more employees in New York and requires 90 days' notice for the termination of 25 or more employees at a single site.

**Why it matters:**
If Seller has not provided the required NY WARN notice before terminating 30 employees on April 15, Seller may be in violation. While this is legally Seller's exposure (Excluded Liability), the practical consequences could affect the transition: employees may be uncertain about their re-employment, morale could be low, and key staff (e.g., the General Manager) might hesitate to accept Buyer's offer if they perceive the transition as chaotic. The Integration Notes indicate the GM has "agreed in principle to stay on" but terms are not finalized.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with Seller's counsel that NY WARN notice has been given or that an exception applies (e.g., the "unforeseeable business circumstances" exception, though this is narrow).
- Ensure offer letters are delivered by April 1 as planned, with clear start dates of April 15, to minimize uncertainty for employees.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** (1) Has Seller provided any WARN notice to employees? (2) Are all 30 employees expected to accept Buyer's offers? (3) What are the terms for the General Manager's continued employment?
