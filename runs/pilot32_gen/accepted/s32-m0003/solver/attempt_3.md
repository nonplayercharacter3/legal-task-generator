## Executive Summary

The current document set leaves Vaikiv materially exposed in three interrelated areas: (1) the 2025 Class II recall and the FDA's VAI classification of the CMO are both live, unresolved regulatory events, yet the indemnity structure caps Vaikiv's recovery at $18.6 million (after a $1.395 million deductible) and expires 90 days post-closing; (2) the Transition Services Agreement is governed by New York law and caps Seller's liability at roughly $1.77 million despite the fact that Seller will be performing FDA-regulated quality and pharmacovigilance functions on Vaikiv's behalf; and (3) the draft press release characterizes the product as "well-established" with a "durable patent position" while omitting any reference to the open recall or the VAI, creating securities and regulatory risk for Vaikiv at closing.

The issues below are ordered by significance.

## Issue 1: 2025 Recall Indemnification Subjected to Cap and Deductible

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.4(c), paragraph 3; read against APA § 9.5(b)(i).

**The problem.** APA § 9.5(b)(i) provides that the Cap and Deductible "shall not apply to Losses arising out of or relating to (i) any Excluded Liability under Section 9.2(c)." The 2025 Recall is expressly an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(c). On its face, therefore, Vaikiv's indemnification recovery for recall-related losses should be uncapped and deductible-free.

However, Schedule 2.4(c)(3) of the Disclosure Schedule overrides this:

> "Seller's indemnification of the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(c) for Losses arising out of or relating to the 2025 Recall shall be subject to the Cap and the Deductible under Section 9.5 **notwithstanding Section 9.5(b).**"

This is a direct, express carve-back that neutralizes the APA's own protection. The practical effect: Vaikiv bears the first $1,395,000 of recall-related losses and has no recovery above $18,600,000 in the aggregate.

**Why it matters.** The recall is not over. As of the Signing Date:
- Only 81% of distributed units have been returned (Schedule 2.4(c)(2)). Approximately 19% remain in the hands of patients, pharmacies, or wholesalers.
- The FDA has not issued a termination notice (Schedule 2.4(c)(2)).
- The FDA classified the underlying CMO inspection as VAI (Schedule 4.5(1)), which is one enforcement step below a warning letter.
- The recall was triggered by a granulation moisture deviation that affected all nine lots; if the deviation was not fully contained, additional lots or even future lots could be implicated.

A Class II recall of a commercial oral solid dosage product, combined with potential product liability claims from the unreturned 19% of units, could easily generate costs well in excess of $18.6 million—particularly if the FDA escalates to a warning letter or if an adverse event trend emerges from the unreturned inventory.

**Recommended fix.** Delete the last sentence of Schedule 2.4(c)(3) in its entirety. If Seller resists, propose the following compromise:

> "Seller's indemnification of the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(c) for Losses arising out of or relating to the 2025 Recall shall be subject to the Cap under Section 9.5(a) but **not** to the Deductible. The Cap for purposes of this sentence shall be $[30,000,000]."

This preserves a cap (addressing Seller's concern about unlimited exposure) while removing the deductible (so Vaikiv is not out-of-pocket for the first $1.4 million) and raising the cap to a level more commensurate with the risk.

## Issue 2: 90-Day Survival Period and Escrow Release Date

**Where:** APA § 9.1; Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.1.

**The problem.** APA § 9.1 provides that all non-Fundamental Seller representations—including § 4.5 (Regulatory Matters), § 4.6 (Product Quality), and § 4.7 (Recalls)—"shall survive the Closing until the Release Date." The Escrow Agreement defines the Release Date as **90 days after the Closing Date** (§ 4.1). The same 90-day window governs Vaikiv's ability to file a Claim Notice against the escrow (Escrow Agreement § 4.2(a): "At any time before the Release Date").

**Why it matters.** Ninety days is grossly inadequate for a pharmaceutical product with the following open items at closing:

| Open Item | Source | Realistic Resolution Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| FDA VAI classification at CMO | Disclosure Schedule 4.5(1) | 6–18 months (follow-up inspection, possible escalation) |
| 2025 Recall effectiveness check / FDA termination | Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c)(2) | 3–12 months |
| Potential product liability claims from unreturned units (19%) | Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c)(2) | 1–3 years (statute of limitations) |
| Stability of non-recalled lots manufactured Nov 2024–Mar 2025 | Disclosure Schedule 4.6; 2.4(c)(1) | 12–24 months (next stability time point) |

If the FDA issues a warning letter to the CMO at month five, or if a product liability claim is filed at month eight based on a unit from the recalled lots, Vaikiv will have no recourse against the escrow (released) and—because the underlying rep has expired—potentially no recourse under § 9.2(a) at all. The only surviving claim would be under § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities), which is subject to the cap per Issue 1.

**Recommended fix.** Amend APA § 9.1 to provide tiered survival periods:

> "The representations and warranties of Seller in Article IV, other than the Fundamental Representations, shall survive the Closing as follows:
> (a) the representations in Sections 4.5, 4.6 and 4.7 shall survive until the later of (i) twenty-four (24) months after the Closing Date and (ii) the date on which the FDA issues a final determination terminating the 2025 Recall and closing all open inspections of the CMO facility;
> (b) all other non-Fundamental Representations shall survive until eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date; and
> (c) the Fundamental Representations shall survive until sixty (60) days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations."

Conforming changes to Escrow Agreement § 4.1 (Release Date) and § 4.2(a) (Claim Notice window) are required. If Seller insists on a shorter escrow hold, propose a **staged release**: 50% at 12 months, 50% at 24 months, with the recall-related portion held until FDA termination of the recall.

## Issue 3: FDA VAI Classification—Adequacy of Disclosure and Rep Qualification

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.5(1); APA § 4.5(c).

**The problem.** The FDA classified the CMO's Raleigh, NC facility inspection as **Voluntary Action Indicated (VAI)** by letter dated November 4, 2025. A VAI is the FDA's second-most-serious inspection classification (below only "Official Action Indicated"). It signals that the FDA has identified significant cGMP deficiencies that, if not adequately corrected, will result in enforcement action—typically a warning letter.

The three observations are:
1. In-process moisture monitoring during granulation (the same deviation that caused the 2025 Recall);
2. Timeliness of deviation investigations;
3. Equipment cleaning log documentation.

APA § 4.5(c) represents that, except as disclosed, neither Seller nor the CMO has received any FDA Form 483, warning letter, or "other written notice of noncompliance." The VAI letter is disclosed in Schedule 4.5(1), so the rep is technically qualified. But the practical significance is understated:

- A VAI typically precedes a warning letter by 3–12 months if the FDA is not satisfied with the corrective actions.
- Observation (a) is the same root cause as the recall. If the CMO's "revised in-process moisture controls" (implemented July 28, 2025 per Schedule 2.4(c)(2)) are found inadequate at follow-up inspection, the FDA could issue a warning letter and potentially an import alert on the CMO's product.
- An import alert would prevent the CMO from shipping product to the U.S., creating an immediate supply disruption for Vaikiv.

**Why it matters to Vaikiv.** Vaikiv is purchasing a product whose sole manufacturer is under active FDA scrutiny with a classification that predicts enforcement. The 90-day survival period (Issue 2) means Vaikiv will have no contractual recourse if the VAI escalates after month three. The non-compete (§ 6.6) does not help because the risk is to the CMO, not to Seller.

**Recommended fix.**
1. Survival period per Issue 2 (24 months / until FDA closes all open items).
2. Add a specific covenant in APA Article VI:

> "**6.9 CMO Regulatory Matters.** Seller shall use commercially reasonable efforts to (a) monitor the status of all open FDA inspections, Form 483s, VAIs and warning letters relating to the CMO facility, (b) promptly notify Buyer of any FDA communication, follow-up inspection or escalation relating to the CMO, and (c) cooperate with Buyer in any response to the FDA. Seller shall remain responsible for all Excluded Liabilities arising from pre-Closing CMO deficiencies, including any warning letter, import alert or consent decree relating to the CMO facility that results from conditions existing before the Closing Date."

3. Consider requiring Seller to deliver, as a closing condition or post-closing covenant, a written confirmation from the CMO that its corrective actions are complete and that it has received no indication of FDA dissatisfaction.

## Issue 4: Transition Services Agreement Governed by New York Law

**Where:** TSA § 9.3; APA § 11.8.

**The problem.** The APA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (§ 11.8–11.9). The TSA is governed by **New York law** with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts (§ 9.3). APA § 11.4 provides that the APA prevails over ancillary agreements in case of conflict, "except that each Ancillary Agreement shall control with respect to matters arising under such Ancillary Agreement."

This creates a split: a dispute about whether Seller breached a TSA service obligation (e.g., failed to timely report an adverse event under § 5.2) is governed by New York law in New York courts, while a dispute about whether the same failure constitutes a breach of Seller's covenants under the APA (e.g., § 6.4 Notice of Recalls and Regulatory Events) is governed by Delaware law in Delaware courts. The same facts could yield different outcomes depending on which agreement's forum is invoked.

**Why it matters.** In a pharmaceutical context, the distinction between New York and Delaware law on issues like implied covenants, materiality, and the standard of care can be significant. More practically, Vaikiv's legal team would need to maintain counsel in two jurisdictions for what is essentially a single integrated transaction.

**Recommended fix.** Amend TSA § 9.3 to conform to the APA:

> "This Agreement, and any claim, controversy or dispute arising out of or relating to this Agreement, shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the internal laws of the State of Delaware, without giving effect to any choice or conflict of law provision or rule. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of the State of Delaware (or, only if that court lacks subject matter jurisdiction, the Superior Court of the State of Delaware or the United States District Court for the District of Delaware) for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement."

Delete the reference to "the Purchase Agreement" in the jurisdiction clause to avoid circularity.

## Issue 5: TSA Liability Cap Inadequate for Regulatory Services

**Where:** TSA § 8.3.

**The problem.** TSA § 8.3 caps each party's aggregate liability at "the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement." Based on Schedule A:

| Service | Monthly Fee | Duration | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-1 Regulatory Support | $42,000 | 9 months | $378,000 |
| A-2 Pharmacovigilance | $58,000 | 9 months | $522,000 |
| A-3 Quality and Batch Release | $47,500 | 6 months | $285,000 |
| A-4 Supply Chain | $21,000 | 6 months | $126,000 |
| A-5 Distribution | $36,500 | 9 months | $328,500 |
| A-6 Recall Support | T&M $225/hr | 9 months | Variable |
| A-7 Data Extraction | $15,000 | 9 months | ~$135,000 |
| **Total (fixed)** | | | **~$1,774,500** |

So if Seller's pharmacovigilance team fails to file a required adverse event report with the FDA within the regulatory deadline, or if Seller's quality unit releases a non-conforming batch, Vaikiv's maximum recovery under the TSA is approximately $1.77 million (plus any T&M recall fees actually incurred). The resulting FDA sanction, product recall, or patient harm could cost Vaikiv orders of magnitude more.

The carve-outs in § 8.3 (gross negligence, willful misconduct, fraud, breach of § 6 confidentiality/data, third-party indemnification) are helpful but narrow. A simple negligent failure to meet an FDA reporting deadline would fall within the cap.

**Recommended fix.** Amend TSA § 8.3:

> "Except for Losses arising out of a Party's gross negligence, willful misconduct, fraud, breach of Section 6, or indemnification obligations under this Section 8 for third-party claims, neither Party's aggregate liability under this Agreement shall exceed the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement. **Notwithstanding the foregoing, Seller's aggregate liability for Losses arising out of or relating to (a) Seller's breach of Section 5 (Regulatory and Quality Matters), or (b) any recall, market withdrawal, field correction or FDA enforcement action resulting from Seller's performance or non-performance of the Services, shall not be subject to the cap in this Section 8.3.** Neither Party shall be liable for consequential, indirect or punitive damages under this Agreement, except to the extent paid to a third party."

## Issue 6: Press Release Omits Recall and VAI; Overstates Product Position

**Where:** Closing Press Release, §§ 1, 2, 4, 8.

**The problem.** The draft press release:
- Describes Oravexin as "an established, once-daily commercial product" (§ 1) and "a well-established product with a durable patent position" (§ 4, CEO quote);
- States the aim is "an orderly transfer of the franchise with continued supply to patients, pharmacies and wholesalers" (§ 3);
- Lists forward-looking statement risks including "timing of FDA acknowledgment of the NDA transfer" and "manufacturing and supply continuity" (§ 8) but **omits any reference to the open Class II recall, the VAI classification, or the risk of FDA escalation**.

**Why it matters.**
1. **Securities risk.** If Vaikiv is a public company (or if this release is used in investor communications), characterizing a product with an open Class II recall and a VAI-classified manufacturer as "well-established" without disclosure of those facts could constitute a material misstatement or omission. The safe-harbor for forward-looking statements (§ 8) is undermined if the known risks are not identified.
2. **Regulatory risk.** The FDA reviews press releases and public statements. A statement implying "continued supply" and "orderly transfer" while the sole manufacturer is under a VAI could be viewed as inconsistent with the product's actual regulatory status, particularly if the FDA is considering escalation.
3. **Litigation risk.** If a product liability claim arises from the unreturned 19% of recalled units, the press release could be introduced as evidence that Vaikiv represented the product as safe and established at the time of acquisition.

**Recommended fix.**
- Add to § 8 (Forward-Looking Statements) the following risks: "the status and outcome of the voluntary recall of certain Oravexin lots initiated in September 2025; the FDA's classification of the contract manufacturer's inspection as Voluntary Action Indicated in November 2025 and the possibility of further FDA regulatory action; and the timing and scope of any FDA follow-up inspection or enforcement communication."
- Soften the CEO quote in § 4: replace "well-established product with a durable patent position" with "product with a established patent position and a proven once-daily dosing profile."
- In § 3, add: "The parties are aware of certain open regulatory matters relating to the Product, including a voluntary recall initiated by Taigon in 2025, and are working to resolve them in an orderly manner."
- **Flag to client:** Confirm whether Vaikiv is a public company or has any securities disclosure obligations that would require additional review of this release by Vaikiv's disclosure committee or securities counsel before issuance.

## Issue 7: Insurance Proceeds from Recall Retained by Seller

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.2(4); APA § 2.4(c).

**The problem.** Schedule 2.2(4) excludes from the Purchased Assets "all insurance policies of Seller and its Affiliates and all rights to proceeds under them, **including any proceeds relating to the 2025 Recall**." The recall liabilities are Excluded Liabilities (§ 2.4(c)), so Seller retains both the liability and the insurance. This is internally consistent.

However, combined with Issue 1 (recall indemnification capped at $18.6M after $1.395M deductible), the structure creates a misalignment: if actual recall costs are $25 million, Seller's indemnification obligation to Vaikiv is capped at $18.6M (and reduced by the deductible to $17.2M). Seller keeps its insurance proceeds, which might cover $10–$15 million of those costs. Vaikiv bears the excess but has no access to the insurance that was specifically generated by the pre-closing event.

**Recommended fix.** If the cap on recall indemnification is maintained (per Issue 1 compromise), add to Schedule 2.4(c)(3):

> "Notwithstanding the foregoing, if Seller receives any insurance proceeds relating to the 2025 Recall, Seller shall hold such proceeds in trust for the benefit of Buyer and shall apply them to reduce Seller's indemnification obligations under this paragraph dollar-for-dollar before applying the Cap."

Alternatively, negotiate to include recall-related insurance proceeds in the Purchased Assets (delete the "including any proceeds relating to the 2025 Recall" language from Schedule 2.2(4)).

## Issue 8: Inventory Shelf Life and Potential Quality Risk on Non-Recalled Lots

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6(2); Schedule 2.4(c)(1); APA § 4.6.

**The problem.** The approved shelf life is 24 months. The minimum remaining shelf life required at closing is 15 months (Schedule 4.6(2)), meaning some inventory units will be 9 months old at closing. The 2025 Recall was triggered by out-of-specification dissolution at the **12-month stability time point** for Lot OX-2514, caused by a granulation moisture deviation affecting lots manufactured between November 2024 and March 2025.

The recalled lots are excluded from inventory (Schedule 4.6(2): "No unit of the Inventory is from any of Lots OX-2511 through OX-2519"). However, the CMO's revised moisture controls were not implemented until **July 28, 2025** (Schedule 2.4(c)(2)). Any non-recalled lots manufactured between November 2024 and July 28, 2025 were produced under the same deficient controls. If those lots are in the Inventory at closing, they will reach the 12-month stability time point within 3–9 months after closing—during the transition period when Seller is still performing batch release (TSA § 5.3, Service A-3, ending at 6 months).

**Why it matters.** If a non-recalled lot fails dissolution at 12 months, Vaikiv could face a new recall, FDA notification, and product liability exposure. The rep in § 4.6 (that each unit "was manufactured in accordance with the NDA, its specifications and cGMP") is qualified by the disclosure schedule, and in any event expires at the Release Date (90 days per Issue 2).

**Recommended fix.**
1. Require Seller to confirm in writing, as a closing deliverable, the manufacturing dates of all lots in the Inventory and to represent that no lot in the Inventory was manufactured between November 1, 2024 and July 28, 2025 (the period of deficient moisture controls). If any such lots exist, require their exclusion from the Inventory or a specific price adjustment.
2. Extend the survival of § 4.6 to at least 18 months (per Issue 2 fix) to cover the 12-month stability time point for inventory that is 9 months old at closing.
3. Add to TSA § 5.3: "Seller shall notify Buyer immediately upon becoming aware of any out-of-specification or out-of-trend result for any lot in the Inventory, and shall not release any batch for which such a result has occurred without Buyer's prior written consent."

## Issue 9: Non-Assignable Contract Fallback Is Weak

**Where:** APA § 2.5; Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 3.2(d).

**The problem.** If the CMO does not consent to assignment of the Manufacturing Agreement by the Closing, § 2.5 provides that the agreement "shall not constitute an assignment" and Seller shall "use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain that consent." Pending consent, Seller "shall provide Buyer the benefits of that Assigned Contract, and Buyer shall perform Seller's obligations under it to the extent of those benefits."

This creates an informal tripartite arrangement with no privity between Buyer and CMO. Buyer is performing obligations under a contract to which it is not a party, with no direct right to enforce the CMO's manufacturing commitments, quality obligations, or supply guarantees. If the CMO underperforms or the relationship deteriorates, Buyer has no contractual remedy against the CMO—only an indemnification claim against Seller (subject to the cap and survival period).

**Why it matters.** The CMO is the sole manufacturer. If consent is not obtained at closing, Vaikiv is operationally dependent on an arrangement it cannot enforce. Given the VAI (Issue 3), the CMO relationship is already strained.

**Recommended fix.**
1. Make CMO consent a **condition to Closing** under § 7.2 (add as § 7.2(d): "Seller shall have obtained the written consent of the CMO to the assignment of the Manufacturing Agreement and the Quality Agreement, in form and substance reasonably acceptable to Buyer").
2. If Seller resists, at minimum require that the CMO consent be delivered within 30 days post-closing as a covenant, with a specific indemnity (uncapped) for any loss arising from the CMO's refusal or the interim arrangement.
3. Add to § 2.5: "If any Assigned Contract is not assigned by the Closing due to lack of third-party consent, Seller shall remain the party of record under such contract and shall be solely responsible for all obligations thereunder, including payment obligations, for so long as the contract remains unassigned."

## Issue 10: Broad Cross-Disclosure Provision

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, § 1.3.

**The problem.** Section 1.3 provides: "Any information disclosed under one schedule is deemed disclosed for any other section of Article IV of the Agreement **to the extent its relevance to that other section is reasonably apparent on the face of the disclosure.**"

This is broader than typical. For example, the detailed recall narrative in Schedule 2.4(c) could be argued to "reasonably appear" on its face as qualifying § 4.5 (Regulatory Matters), § 4.6 (Product Quality), § 4.9 (Assigned Contracts—regarding the CMO relationship), and § 4.10 (Litigation—regarding potential product liability claims). The more reps a disclosure qualifies, the narrower Vaikiv's indemnification claim under § 9.2(a).

**Why it matters.** In a post-closing dispute, Seller will argue that the recall disclosure in Schedule 2.4(c) qualified every rep that could possibly be implicated by the recall. The "reasonably apparent" standard is subjective and invites litigation.

**Recommended fix.** Narrow § 1.3:

> "Any information disclosed under a schedule shall be deemed disclosed for the section of Article IV to which that schedule specifically corresponds. Information disclosed under one schedule shall not be deemed disclosed for any other section unless the disclosure expressly references that other section by number."

Alternatively, if Seller resists deletion, add: "For the avoidance of doubt, the disclosures in Schedule 2.4(c) do not qualify the representations in Sections 4.5(a), 4.5(b), 4.6, 4.8 or 4.9."

## Issue 11: No Service Level Agreements in TSA

**Where:** TSA §§ 2.2, 3.1; Schedule A.

**The problem.** The TSA's performance standard is "substantially similar in nature, scope, quality and priority" to pre-closing services (§ 2.2) and "professional and workmanlike manner" (§ 3.1(a)). There are no specific SLAs: no maximum turnaround time for batch release, no defined response time for adverse event processing (beyond the forwarding deadlines in § 5.2), no accuracy threshold for data migration, no uptime requirement for systems.

**Why it matters.** Without objective benchmarks, a performance dispute under § 3.4 (Escalation) will turn on subjective arguments about what "substantially similar" means. Given the TSA liability cap (Issue 5), even a successful dispute yields limited recovery.

**Recommended fix.** Add a Schedule B (Service Level Standards) with minimum requirements, for example:

| Service | SLA |
|---|---|
| Batch release (A-3) | Release decision communicated to Buyer within 5 Business Days of CMO batch record submission |
| Adverse event processing (A-2) | Case disposition within 10 Business Days of receipt; expedited reports (serious/unexpected) within 2 Business Days |
| Data migration (A-7) | 99% data completeness as verified by Buyer's IT; errors identified and corrected within 15 Business Days of phase delivery |
| Regulatory correspondence (A-1) | Forwarded to Buyer within 1 Business Day of receipt from FDA or CMO |

Add to § 3.1: "Failure to meet the Service Level Standards in Schedule B on more than two (2) occasions in any consecutive three-month period shall constitute a material breach of this Agreement."

## Issue 12: Escrow Tax Treatment

**Where:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 8.1; APA § 9.8.

**The problem.** Escrow Agreement § 8.1 provides that Buyer is treated as the tax owner of the Escrow Fund until disbursed. Buyer reports all income earned on the escrow. APA § 9.8 treats indemnification payments as a purchase price adjustment for tax purposes.

If the escrow is held for 24 months (per the recommended fix in Issue 2), Buyer will report interest income on $18.6 million for two years. More significantly, if the escrow is released to Seller at month 24, the tax treatment of that release as a "purchase price adjustment" could create timing mismatches or unexpected tax consequences for Buyer.

**Why it matters.** This is a secondary issue but should be flagged to Vaikiv's tax team before the escrow period is extended. The 90-day period in the current draft minimizes this exposure; extending it to 24 months increases it.

**Recommended fix.** No change to the APA language, but:
1. Confirm with Vaikiv's tax advisors that the extended escrow period does not create adverse tax consequences.
2. If needed, add to Escrow Agreement § 8.1: "The Parties shall cooperate in good faith to minimize the tax burden on Buyer resulting from the treatment of the Escrow Fund under this Section 8.1, including by agreeing on investment directions under Section 3.1 that are tax-efficient for Buyer."

## Items Requiring Client / Business Team Input

Before we finalize our markup position on the following, we need input from the Vaikiv team:

1. **Recall exposure quantification (Issue 1).** Does Vaikiv's business team have an estimate of total expected recall costs (including the unreturned 19% of units, effectiveness checks, potential product liability claims, and regulatory response)? This will determine whether the $18.6M cap is even close to adequate or whether we should push for $30M+ or uncapped.

2. **Vaikiv's quality unit readiness (Issues 5, 8, 11).** When does Vaikiv expect its own quality unit to be fully operational for batch release and pharmacovigilance? If the answer is later than 6 months (when Service A-3 ends), we need to negotiate a longer transition period or a bridge arrangement. This also affects the shelf-life issue: if Vaikiv can't take over batch release until month 9, inventory that is 9 months old at closing will be 18 months old—half its shelf life consumed—before Vaikiv is in control.

3. **Distribution infrastructure (TSA Schedule A, A-5).** Service A-5 (Distribution and Order-to-Cash) relies on Seller's master services agreements with 3PLs, wholesalers, and GPOs, which are Excluded Assets (Schedule 2.2(3)). Does Vaikiv have its own distribution contracts in place, or will it need to negotiate new ones? If the latter, the 9-month transition period may be insufficient, and we should consider extending A-5 or adding a distribution transition service.

4. **Public company status (Issue 6).** Is Vaikiv a public company or does it have any securities disclosure obligations (e.g., as a subsidiary of a public parent)? If so, the press release needs review by Vaikiv's disclosure committee and securities counsel before we agree to the final language.

5. **CMO relationship strategy (Issues 3, 9).** Does Vaikiv intend to maintain the Brennholt CMO relationship long-term, or is it evaluating alternative manufacturers? If Vaikiv plans to switch CMOs, the VAI issue is less critical (though still relevant for the transition), and our markup priorities shift toward ensuring a clean exit from the CMO. If Vaikiv plans to stay with Brennholt, the VAI and the non-assignment fallback become existential issues.

6. **Insurance (Issue 7).** Does Vaikiv know what product liability insurance Seller carries, what the policy limits are, and whether the policy would respond to a recall of this nature? If Seller's insurance is substantial (e.g., $50M+ tower), the cap on recall indemnification is less concerning because Seller will be well-insured. If coverage is limited, the cap is more dangerous.
