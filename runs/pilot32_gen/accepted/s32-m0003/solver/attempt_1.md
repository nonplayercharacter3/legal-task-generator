# MEMORANDUM

**Re:** Vaikiv Therapeutics / Taigon Pharma – Buyer-Side Issue Identification and Recommended Markup

---

## Summary

The current document set leaves Vaikiv materially exposed in four areas: (1) the survival period for regulatory, quality, and recall representations is only 90 days post-closing, which is inconsistent with the risk profile of a product under an active Class II recall and a VAI-classified CMO; (2) the Disclosure Schedule overrides the APA to subject recall-related indemnification to the cap and deductible, gutting the protection that Section 9.5(b) was designed to provide for Excluded Liabilities; (3) the Transition Services Agreement contains a governing-law and jurisdiction clause that directly conflicts with the APA and sweeps APA disputes into New York courts; and (4) the TSA caps Seller's liability at the modest Service Fees, leaving Vaikiv under-protected for transition failures. Additional issues of varying significance are addressed below.

---

## Tier 1 – Most Significant Issues

### 1. Survival Period for Regulatory, Quality, and Recall Reps Is Only 90 Days

**Where:** APA § 9.1 (first sentence); Escrow Agreement § 4.1 (definition of "Release Date"); APA § 1.1 (definition of "Release Date" cross-referenced to Escrow Agreement).

**The Problem.** Section 9.1 provides that Seller's non-Fundamental Representations—including § 4.5 (Regulatory Matters), § 4.6 (Product Quality), and § 4.7 (Recalls)—survive only until the Release Date. The Escrow Agreement defines the Release Date as 90 days after the Closing Date (Escrow Agmt. § 4.1). With a March 2, 2026 closing, the Release Date is approximately June 1, 2026.

This is critically short given:

- The 2025 Recall (Class II, FDA Recall Event No. 94417) was initiated September 17, 2025, and **the FDA has not yet issued a termination notice** (Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 2). Effectiveness checks, consumer notifications, and any resulting product liability claims can surface well beyond 90 days.
- The CMO's facility was classified **Voluntary Action Indicated** by the FDA on November 4, 2025 (Disclosure Schedule 4.5(1)). A VAI classification means the FDA may refuse to file new NDAs or supplements from that facility. The practical consequences—whether the CMO can continue to manufacture conforming product, whether a second inspection will occur, whether the FDA will require additional process validation—will not be known within 90 days.
- The Form 483 observations included "timeliness of deviation investigations" and "equipment cleaning log documentation" (Disclosure Schedule 4.5(1)), which are systemic issues that could manifest in future batches.

If a new out-of-specification result, a second Form 483, or a product liability claim from the recalled lots emerges after June 1, 2026, Vaikiv will have no indemnification recourse under § 9.2(a) because the representation will have ceased to survive. The exclusive remedy provision (§ 9.7) forecloses any alternative theory of post-closing contract damages.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** Vaikiv is paying $186 million for a product whose manufacturer is under a VAI cloud and whose recent recall is not yet closed out by the FDA. The 90-day window means Vaikiv effectively bears all regulatory and quality risk from day 91 forward, with no recourse against Seller. The escrow, which is the sole practical source of recovery (see § 9.6), will have been released to Seller by that point.

**Recommended Fix.** Carve the regulatory, product quality, and recall representations out of the 90-day Release Date and give them a longer survival period. A market-standard approach for pharma asset deals with an ongoing recall would be:

> "The representations and warranties in Sections 4.5 (Regulatory Matters), 4.6 (Product Quality) and 4.7 (Recalls) shall survive the Closing until the later of (a) the Release Date and (b) [18] months after the Closing Date; provided that with respect to Section 4.7, survival shall continue until the later of the foregoing dates and the date on which the FDA issues a letter terminating the 2025 Recall (Recall Event No. 94417) or the applicable statute of limitations expires, if earlier."

Correspondingly, the Escrow Agreement should be amended so that the Pending Claim Amount mechanism in § 4.1 continues to hold funds for claims under these extended-survival reps. Alternatively, if Seller resists extending survival for all three reps, extend at minimum § 4.7 (Recalls) until FDA termination of the recall, and § 4.5 (Regulatory) for 18 months.

If Seller insists on the 90-day Release Date for escrow purposes, negotiate a **second-tier escrow or holdback** of $5–$10 million released 18 months post-closing, dedicated to regulatory and recall claims.

### 2. Disclosure Schedule Carves the 2025 Recall Back Into the Cap and Deductible

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 3; APA § 9.5(b); APA § 9.2(c).

**The Problem.** Section 9.5(b) of the APA provides that the Cap and Deductible "shall not apply to Losses arising out of or relating to (i) any Excluded Liability under Section 9.2(c)." The 2025 Recall is expressly an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(c). Therefore, under the four corners of the APA, recall-related indemnification claims should be **uncapped and without a deductible**.

However, Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 3 states:

> "Seller's indemnification of the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(c) for Losses arising out of or relating to the 2025 Recall shall be subject to the Cap and the Deductible under Section 9.5 **notwithstanding Section 9.5(b)**."

Because the Disclosure Schedule is incorporated into the Agreement (APA § 11.12), this language operates as a specific override of § 9.5(b) for the 2025 Recall. The practical effect:

- Vaikiv absorbs the first **$1,395,000** of recall-related Losses (the Deductible).
- Vaikiv's total recovery for all recall-related Losses is capped at **$18,600,000** (the Cap).
- The Cap and Deductible do NOT apply to other Excluded Liabilities (e.g., pre-Closing taxes, pre-Closing employee liabilities), only to the 2025 Recall.

This is a significant deal point buried in the Disclosure Schedule rather than in the operative indemnification article. It converts what should be an uncapped, deductible-free indemnity for the single most material known risk in the transaction into a limited one.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** The 2025 Recall involved nine lots distributed to the retail level. While 81% of units have been returned (Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 2), approximately 19% remain in the field. Product liability claims from patients who took the recalled lots could emerge over the next several years (statute of limitations for personal injury in Delaware is 2 years from discovery, which can be well beyond closing). The recall also carries ongoing regulatory costs (effectiveness checks, FDA correspondence, potential consumer notification). Capping Vaikiv's recovery at $18.6 million—and making Vaikiv eat the first $1.4 million—shifts substantial known risk to the buyer.

**Recommended Fix.** Delete the last sentence of Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 3 in its entirety. The APA as drafted already provides the correct allocation: recall-related Excluded Liabilities are indemnified under § 9.2(c) without the Cap or Deductible per § 9.5(b). If Seller's position is that the recall is a "known" item and should be priced into the deal, the appropriate mechanism is a **purchase price adjustment** or a **specific escrow holdback** for the recall, not a cap on indemnification for an Excluded Liability.

If Seller insists on some limitation, propose:

> "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Seller's aggregate indemnification obligation for Losses arising out of or relating to the 2025 Recall shall not exceed $[25,000,000], and the Deductible shall not apply to such Losses."

This preserves the no-deductible protection (Vaikiv should not bear the first dollar of a known, disclosed recall) while giving Seller a defined maximum exposure that is above the current Cap.

### 3. TSA Governing Law and Jurisdiction Conflict with the APA

**Where:** TSA § 9.3; APA § 11.8 (Governing Law), APA § 11.9 (Exclusive Forum), APA § 11.4 (Conflicts with Ancillary Agreements).

**The Problem.** TSA § 9.3 provides:

> "This Agreement, **and any claim, controversy or dispute arising out of or relating to this Agreement or the Purchase Agreement**, shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of New York … Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts located in New York County, New York for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement **or the Purchase Agreement**."

This clause does two things that conflict with the APA:

1. **Governing law:** The APA is governed by Delaware law (§ 11.8). The TSA purports to subject even Purchase Agreement disputes to New York law.
2. **Jurisdiction:** The APA designates the Delaware Court of Chancery as the exclusive forum (§ 11.9). The TSA purports to drag Purchase Agreement disputes into New York County courts.

APA § 11.4 provides that in the event of a conflict, the APA prevails "except that each Ancillary Agreement shall control with respect to matters arising under such Ancillary Agreement." This savings clause helps for pure TSA disputes, but the TSA's language is overbroad: it explicitly captures "the Purchase Agreement" in both the governing-law and jurisdiction sentences. A seller's counsel could argue that a dispute about, say, the scope of transition services that implicates a representation in the APA (e.g., § 4.4, that the Purchased Assets plus TSA services constitute all assets necessary to conduct the Business) is a "dispute arising out of or relating to … the Purchase Agreement" and therefore belongs in New York under New York law.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If the conflict is not resolved, Vaikiv faces the risk of (a) having to litigate in two forums, (b) applying different substantive laws to intertwined claims, and (c) Seller invoking the TSA's New York forum to avoid the Delaware Court of Chancery, which is generally more predictable for complex commercial disputes.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend TSA § 9.3 to conform to the APA:

> "This Agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the internal laws of the State of Delaware, without giving effect to any choice or conflict of law provision or rule. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of the State of Delaware (or, only if that court lacks subject matter jurisdiction, the Superior Court of the State of Delaware or the United States District Court for the District of Delaware) for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement. Each Party waives any objection to venue in those courts and any claim that any such action has been brought in an inconvenient forum."

Delete all references to "the Purchase Agreement" from TSA § 9.3.

### 4. TSA Liability Cap Is Limited to Total Service Fees

**Where:** TSA § 8.3.

**The Problem.** Section 8.3 caps each party's aggregate liability under the TSA at "the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement." Summing the fixed monthly fees in Schedule A:

| Service | Monthly Fee |
|---|---|
| A-1 Regulatory Support | $42,000 |
| A-2 Pharmacovigilance | $58,000 |
| A-3 Quality and Batch Release | $47,500 |
| A-4 Supply Chain | $21,000 |
| A-5 Distribution and Order-to-Cash | $36,500 |
| A-7 Data Extraction and Migration | $15,000 |
| **Total fixed (per month)** | **$220,000** |

Over the 9-month term, total fixed Service Fees are approximately **$1.98 million** (plus time-and-materials for A-6 Recall Support at $225/hour, and reimbursable costs). The cap therefore sits in the low single-digit millions at most.

The carve-outs from the cap are limited to: gross negligence, willful misconduct, fraud, breach of § 6 (Confidentiality and Data), and third-party claim indemnification. Notably, **breach of § 5 (Regulatory and Quality Matters) is NOT carved out of the cap** for the non-indemnification remedies. So if Seller's failure to process an adverse event report on time causes an FDA regulatory action against the NDA (which by then may be in Buyer's name), and the failure constitutes ordinary negligence rather than gross negligence, Vaikiv's recovery under the TSA is capped at ~$2 million.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** The transition services are not ancillary—they are the mechanism by which Vaikiv maintains regulatory compliance, batch release, and pharmacovigilance for the first 6–9 months post-closing. A failure in any of these functions could result in FDA enforcement action, product withdrawal, or patient harm. The potential downside vastly exceeds $2 million.

**Recommended Fix.** Add § 5 (Regulatory and Quality Matters) to the list of carve-outs from the liability cap in TSA § 8.3:

> "Except for Losses arising out of a Party's gross negligence, willful misconduct, fraud, **breach of Section 5 (Regulatory and Quality Matters)**, breach of Section 6 or indemnification obligations under this Section 8 for third-party claims, neither Party's aggregate liability under this Agreement shall exceed …"

Alternatively, raise the cap to a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $10 million) or to 2× total Service Fees.

## Tier 2 – Significant Issues

### 5. Insurance Proceeds Relating to the 2025 Recall Are Excluded Assets

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule 2.2(4); APA § 2.2.

**The Problem.** Schedule 2.2(4) excludes "all insurance policies of Seller and its Affiliates and all rights to proceeds under them, **including any proceeds relating to the 2025 Recall**." If Seller carries product liability or recall insurance that would respond to claims arising from the recalled lots, those proceeds belong to Seller, not Buyer. Yet Buyer bears the operational and reputational burden of the ongoing recall (consumer inquiries, wholesaler relationships, FDA correspondence) while Seller pockets the insurance recovery.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If a product liability claim arises from a recalled lot and Seller's insurance responds, Seller is made whole while Vaikiv's indemnification recovery is subject to the Cap and Deductible (per Issue 2 above). This creates an asymmetry: the party with the insurance (Seller) has the least incentive to resolve claims quickly, and the party bearing the commercial fallout (Vaikiv) has limited recourse.

**Recommended Fix.** Either:
- (a) Include in the Purchased Assets "all rights to insurance proceeds relating to the 2025 Recall" by deleting the italicized language in Schedule 2.2(4); or
- (b) Add a covenant in the APA requiring Seller to assign to Buyer, at or before Closing, all rights to insurance proceeds for the 2025 Recall, with Seller cooperating in the claims process; or
- (c) At minimum, require Seller to disclose at Closing the existence and limits of any product liability or recall insurance coverage, and to provide Vaikiv a copy of the relevant policy(ies) and any reservation-of-rights letters.

**Client Input Needed:** Confirm whether Taigon carries product liability or recall insurance, and if so, the policy limits and whether a claim has been made or a notice of potential claim given.

### 6. "Fraud" Is Defined Narrowly as Actual and Intentional Fraud

**Where:** APA § 1.1 (definition of "Fraud").

**The Problem.** "Fraud" is defined as "actual and intentional fraud by a Party in the making of the representations and warranties." This requires proof that the party **knew** the representation was false when made. It does not encompass constructive fraud, reckless disregard, or gross negligence in the making of a representation.

The fraud carve-out is the only route to uncapped recovery for a breached representation (other than Excluded Liabilities and Fundamental Reps, per § 9.5(b)–(c)). Given the 90-day survival period (Issue 1) and the recall cap override (Issue 2), the fraud carve-out is Vaikiv's backstop. A narrow definition makes that backstop nearly unusable.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If, for example, Seller knew before signing that the CMO's moisture control fix (implemented July 28, 2025) was ineffective and that additional lots would fail, but represented in § 4.6 that no OOS result "remains uninvestigated," Vaikiv would need to prove Seller acted with actual knowledge of falsity. Reckless indifference would not suffice.

**Recommended Fix.** Broaden the definition:

> "**Fraud**" means actual fraud, intentional misrepresentation, or reckless disregard for the truth in the making of the representations and warranties in Article IV or Article V, as applicable.

If Seller resists "reckless disregard," at minimum add "scienter" (knowledge of falsity or reckless indifference) as an alternative to "intentional."

### 7. VAI Classification Disclosed but No Protective Covenant

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule 4.5(1); APA § 4.5(c); APA § 6.3.

**The Problem.** The FDA classified the CMO's inspection as Voluntary Action Indicated on November 4, 2025. A VAI means the FDA may refuse to accept new NDAs or supplements from that facility until the issues are resolved. The APA contains no covenant requiring Seller to (a) monitor the CMO's VAI status, (b) notify Buyer of any escalation (e.g., a warning letter or import alert), or (c) use best efforts to have the VAI designation lifted before Closing.

Section 6.4 requires notice of "recall, market withdrawal, adverse event trend or communication from a Governmental Authority," which would capture a new FDA letter. But there is no affirmative obligation on Seller to work toward resolving the VAI, and no closing condition tied to the VAI status.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If the VAI escalates to a warning letter or an import alert before or shortly after Closing, the CMO may be unable to manufacture the Product. Vaikiv would then face a supply interruption with no contractual remedy against Seller (the VAI is disclosed, so the § 4.5(c) rep is technically accurate). The non-compete (§ 6.6) prevents Seller from helping with an alternative manufacturer.

**Recommended Fix.** Add a specific covenant:

> "**6.9 CMO Regulatory Status.** From the Signing Date until the Closing, Seller shall (a) use commercially reasonable efforts to work with the CMO to resolve the observations underlying the Form FDA 483 dated June 12, 2025 and to obtain removal of the Voluntary Action Indicated classification; (b) promptly notify Buyer of any escalation in the FDA's classification of the CMO's facility (including any warning letter, untitled letter, or import alert); and (c) provide Buyer with copies of all correspondence between the CMO and the FDA relating to the foregoing."

Consider adding a closing condition: the VAI has not been escalated to a warning letter or import alert as of the Closing Date.

### 8. Press Release Omits Recall and VAI from Risk Factors

**Where:** Closing Press Release § 8 (Forward-Looking Statements).

**The Problem.** The forward-looking statements disclaimer identifies risks including "timing of FDA acknowledgment of the NDA transfer," "manufacturing and supply continuity," "regulatory developments," and "market conditions." It does **not** mention:
- The ongoing Class II recall (Recall Event No. 94417) and that the FDA has not terminated it;
- The VAI classification of the CMO;
- The Form 483 observations.

If Vaikiv is a public company (or if this press release is used in any SEC filing or investor communication), the omission of a known, active Class II recall from the risk factors could be viewed as materially misleading. The recall is not a speculative risk—it is a present fact.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** Securities law exposure. If the stock trades on the announcement and the recall or VAI subsequently causes a decline, investors could argue the press release was misleading by omission.

**Recommended Fix.** Add to the risk factors in § 8:

> "- the ongoing Class II recall of certain lots of Oravexin (FDA Recall Event No. 94417) and the associated regulatory, commercial and potential product liability implications; and
> - the Voluntary Action Indicated classification of the contract manufacturing facility and the risk of further FDA enforcement action."

**Client Input Needed:** Confirm whether Vaikiv is a public reporting company and whether this press release will be furnished on 8-K or used in investor presentations. If so, involve securities counsel.

### 9. Product Quality Rep: Single Investigation for Nine Affected Lots

**Where:** APA § 4.6 (last sentence); Disclosure Schedule 4.6(3).

**The Problem.** Section 4.6 represents that "no out-of-specification or out-of-trend stability result for any commercial lot remains uninvestigated." The Disclosure Schedule discloses only one investigation: QI-25-0418 for Lot OX-2514 (the lot that triggered the recall). However, the root cause—a granulation moisture deviation at the CMO—affected **all nine lots** (OX-2511 through OX-2519) (Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 1).

The schedule does not state whether separate investigations were opened for the other eight lots, or whether the single investigation for OX-2514 was expanded to cover all nine. If the other eight lots were never formally investigated (because they were recalled before stability results came in), the § 4.6 rep could be argued to be inaccurate, but the Disclosure Schedule's silence makes it ambiguous.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If Vaikiv later discovers that the other eight lots had out-of-trend (but not out-of-specification) results that were never investigated, it could indicate a broader quality issue in the CMO's process that extends beyond the recalled lots. This bears on the safety of the remaining Inventory (which is represented to exclude the recalled lots, per Disclosure Schedule 4.6(2)).

**Recommended Fix.** Request that Seller supplement the Disclosure Schedule to confirm:
- Whether formal investigations were opened for each of Lots OX-2511 through OX-2519;
- If not, the rationale (e.g., lots were recalled before the applicable stability time point);
- Whether any out-of-trend results (not just OOS) were observed for the recalled lots or for any non-recalled lot manufactured in the same period (November 2024 – March 2025).

Add to the VDR request list: all investigation reports, OOT trend data, and the CMO's CAPA for the moisture deviation.

## Tier 3 – Moderate Issues

### 10. Press Release Characterizes Full $186 Million as "Cash"

**Where:** Closing Press Release § 2.

**The Problem.** The press release states Vaikiv acquired the assets "for a purchase price of $186,000,000 in cash." In fact, $18.6 million (10%) is deposited into escrow and is not freely available to Seller until the Release Date (or later, for pending claims). While the escrow is technically "paid" by Buyer, the characterization of the entire amount as cash to Seller is imprecise.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If Vaikiv is public, the press release feeds into the 8-K and investor materials. Mischaracterizing the deal structure could create confusion about Vaikiv's actual cash outlay at closing ($167.4 million, not $186 million) and its contingent obligations.

**Recommended Fix.** Revise to: "for a purchase price of $186,000,000, of which $167,400,000 was paid at closing and $18,600,000 was deposited into an escrow account to secure Seller's indemnification obligations."

### 11. No Timeline or Remedy for FDA NDA Transfer Acknowledgment

**Where:** APA § 6.3; APA § 3.1 (Closing effective 12:01 a.m.).

**The Problem.** Section 6.3 requires the Parties to submit NDA Transfer Letters on the Closing Date and obligates Seller to "maintain the NDA for the benefit of Buyer" until FDA acknowledges the transfer. There is no stated timeline for FDA acknowledgment, no obligation on Seller to follow up or escalate with FDA, and no remedy if the transfer is delayed or rejected. In practice, FDA NDA transfers can take 30–90 days, but complex cases (especially with an open VAI at the manufacturing facility) can take longer or draw additional questions.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** Until FDA acknowledges the transfer, Seller remains the legal NDA holder. If a regulatory issue arises during the gap (e.g., FDA questions the transfer in light of the VAI), Seller controls the response. Vaikiv has directional rights ("as Buyer reasonably directs") but no enforcement mechanism if Seller is slow or uncooperative.

**Recommended Fix.** Add to § 6.3:

> "Seller shall use commercially reasonable efforts to respond promptly to any FDA inquiries relating to the transfer and shall provide Buyer with a copy of all FDA correspondence within two Business Days of receipt. If the FDA has not acknowledged the transfer within [60] days after the Closing Date, the Parties shall meet to discuss remedial steps, which may include filing a supplemental transfer request or, if necessary, a consent decree."

### 12. TSA Extension Limited to 90 Days

**Where:** TSA § 7.1.

**The Problem.** Buyer may extend any Service by up to 90 days. The base term is 9 months. If the data migration (Schedule A, item A-7) or the quality unit transfer (item A-3) is not complete by month 9, Vaikiv has at most an additional 3 months. For a complex pharma transition involving ERP data extraction, safety database migration, and quality unit stand-up, 12 months total may be insufficient.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** If a Service ends before Vaikiv is ready to assume it, there is a gap in coverage. For regulatory and pharmacovigilance services, a gap could mean missed FDA reporting deadlines.

**Recommended Fix.** Either extend the base term to 12 months, or increase the extension period to 180 days. Alternatively, make the end date for each Service in Schedule A the later of the stated date and the date on which Buyer provides written notice that it is ready to assume the Service (with an outside date of 15 months).

### 13. Non-Assignable Contract Fallback Creates Ambiguous Tripartite Arrangement

**Where:** APA § 2.5.

**The Problem.** If a third-party consent is not obtained by Closing, the contract is not assigned. Instead, "Seller shall … provide Buyer the benefits of that Assigned Contract, and Buyer shall perform Seller's obligations under it to the extent of those benefits." This creates a situation where Buyer is performing under a contract to which it is not a party, with no privity, no direct enforcement rights against the counterparty, and no clarity about who bears the risk of counterparty breach.

The CMO consent is listed on Schedule 3.2(d), so presumably it will be delivered at Closing. But if it is not, the Manufacturing Agreement—the single most important commercial contract in the deal—would fall into this ambiguous category.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** The Manufacturing Agreement governs the supply of the Product. If it is not assigned and the fallback applies, Vaikiv has no direct contractual relationship with the CMO. Quality disputes, pricing changes, or CMO default would have to be routed through Seller, which has no economic incentive to advocate for Vaikiv post-closing.

**Recommended Fix.** Make the CMO consent a **condition to Closing** (add to § 7.2). If it is not obtained, Buyer should have the right to terminate. Alternatively, require that the CMO consent include a provision making the CMO acknowledge Buyer as the successor and agree to deal directly with Buyer post-closing, even if the formal assignment is delayed.

### 14. Minimum Remaining Shelf Life of 15 Months Is Aggressive for a Product with Quality History

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule 4.6(2); APA § 4.6.

**The Problem.** The approved shelf life is 24 months. The minimum remaining shelf life at Closing is 15 months, meaning inventory could be up to 9 months old at Closing. Given the granulation moisture deviation that affected lots manufactured November 2024 – March 2025, any inventory manufactured in that window (even if not from the recalled lots) was produced during the period when the CMO's moisture controls were deficient. The CMO implemented revised controls on July 28, 2025 (Disclosure Schedule 2.4(c) ¶ 2). Inventory manufactured before that date carries elevated risk.

**Why It Matters to Vaikiv.** Vaikiv is taking title to inventory that may have been manufactured during the period of the CMO's process deviation. If a quality issue emerges in non-recalled lots from the same manufacturing window, the § 4.6 rep (that inventory is not adulterated or misbranded) could be breached—but only if discovered within the 90-day survival period (see Issue 1).

**Recommended Fix.** Require that all Inventory included in the Purchased Assets was manufactured **on or after July 28, 2025** (the date the CMO implemented revised moisture controls), or alternatively, require that Seller provide lot-by-lot manufacturing dates and that Vaikiv have the right to reject (and require Seller to replace or credit) any lot manufactured before that date. Add to § 4.6:

> "No unit of Inventory included in the Purchased Assets was manufactured before [July 28, 2025], the date on which the CMO implemented revised in-process moisture controls."
