## I. CRITICAL ISSUES

### 1. Ridgeview Nitrate Contamination Is Subject to the Indemnity Cap (Contrary to LOI)

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities"); SPA § 9.5(a) and (c); Disclosure Schedule § 9, Part A, Item 1; LOI § 5.3.

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" as "the environmental conditions and matters set forth in **Part B** of Schedule 4.17 of the Disclosure Schedule." Part B of Schedule 4.17 lists only the Hollis Farm decommissioned manure storage pond (estimated $640,000 closure cost). The Ridgeview Farm groundwater nitrate contamination—estimated at **$3,400,000 to $7,800,000** per the Phase II Report—is disclosed in **Part A** of Schedule 4.17, not Part B.

Section 9.5(c) carves "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" out of the Cap and the Deductible. Because the Ridgeview issue is not a "Pre-Closing Environmental Liability" under the SPA's definition, it falls within § 9.2(c) and is therefore subject to the 10% Cap ($4,620,000) under § 9.5(a).

The LOI expressly provided otherwise. Section 5.3 states: "The parties agree that pre-closing environmental liabilities, **including Ridgeview Farm nitrate contamination**, [are] not subject to the general cap."

**Why it matters.** The Ridgeview remediation estimate ($3.4M–$7.8M) could exceed the $4.62M Cap, particularly at the high end. If the regulator requires the more expensive remedial approach, Lepund's recovery from Seller would be truncated. Additionally, the Vt. ANR has required a corrective action work plan by January 31, 2027 (Disclosure Schedule § 9, Part A, Item 1(e)), meaning Lepund will be operating under an active regulatory deadline immediately post-closing.

**Recommended fix.** Amend the definition of "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" in SPA § 1.1 to reference **Part A and Part B** of Schedule 4.17 (or, alternatively, to add the Ridgeview matter specifically). Conform § 9.5(c) accordingly. This implements the parties' agreement as recorded in the LOI.

### 2. Tax Indemnity Survival Period Is 18 Months Instead of Statute of Limitations Plus 60 Days

**Where:** SPA § 9.1(b); LOI § 5.2.

**Issue.** SPA § 9.1(b) provides that "The obligations of Seller under Section 9.2(b) [Pre-Closing Taxes] shall survive until the **Maturity Date** (as defined in the Seller Note)." The Seller Note matures 18 months after the Closing Date (Seller Note § 1.3). The LOI, by contrast, provided: "This tax indemnity would survive until **60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations**" (LOI § 5.2).

For a taxable period ending in late 2026, the federal statute of limitations on assessment is generally three years (26 U.S.C. § 6501(a)), potentially longer for substantial omissions. The LOI therefore contemplated survival of roughly three to four years or more. The SPA cuts this to 18 months.

**Why it matters.** If the IRS or a state authority opens an examination of a pre-closing return after month 18, Lepund has no indemnity recourse against Seller even though SPA § 7.3 gives Buyer control of the contest. Seller's tax representation (§ 4.14) also survives only 15 months under § 9.1(a), compounding the gap.

**Recommended fix.** Amend SPA § 9.1(b) to provide that Seller's obligations under § 9.2(b) survive until 60 days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (including any extensions), consistent with LOI § 5.2.

### 3. IP Assignment "Business" Definition Excludes Yogurt, Butter, and Cheese

**Where:** IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); LOI § 3.1.

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Business" as "the production, processing, marketing and sale of organic fluid milk, **yogurt, butter and cheese**." The IP Assignment Agreement defines "Business" as "the production, processing and sale of organic **fluid milk**"—omitting yogurt, butter, cheese, and the word "marketing."

"Assigned IP" is defined as IP "used or held for use in the **Business**" (IP Assignment § 1.1). Because the IP Assignment's narrower definition controls within that agreement (SPA § 11.9 provides that each Ancillary Agreement controls as to its subject matter), the assignment reaches only IP used in the fluid milk line. Recipes, starter cultures, and know-how used exclusively or primarily in the yogurt, butter, or cheese operations are **not** Assigned IP.

The LOI specifically identified "the Company's **yogurt and cheese recipes**" as IP to be assigned (LOI § 3.1).

**Why it matters.** The Company's farmstead cheese production line and yogurt operations are core revenue generators. If the proprietary recipes, starter cultures, and processing protocols for those products remain with Brouloul Farmstead IP Holdings, LLC, Lepund acquires a business it cannot operate independently. Seller (or its IP affiliate) could theoretically license the same recipes to a competitor or withhold critical know-how.

**Recommended fix.** Conform the "Business" definition in IP Assignment § 1.1 to the SPA definition (i.e., "the production, processing, marketing and sale of organic fluid milk, yogurt, butter and cheese, as conducted by Assignee as of the date of the Purchase Agreement"). Alternatively, expand the "Assigned IP" definition to expressly include all IP used in any of the Company's product lines.

### 4. Transition Services Agreement Indemnity Is Grossly Asymmetric

**Where:** TSA § 8.1 (Provider indemnity); TSA § 8.2 (Recipient indemnity); TSA § 8.4 (liability cap).

**Issue.** Three provisions combine to create a heavily one-sided risk allocation:

- **§ 8.1** limits Provider's indemnity to "gross negligence or willful misconduct" in performing the Services (plus breach of confidentiality). Ordinary negligence—e.g., a hauling error that cross-contaminates an organic load, a payroll miscalculation, or a missed certification deadline—is **not** indemnifiable by Provider.
- **§ 8.2** requires Recipient (Lepund) to indemnify Provider for "all Losses arising out of or relating to the provision of the Services or Recipient's use of the Services," carved back only for Provider's "willful misconduct." This means that if Provider's ordinary negligence causes a third-party loss (e.g., a customer sues because a milk load arrived late or contaminated), Lepund must indemnify Provider, and Provider has no reciprocal obligation to Lepund.
- **§ 8.4** caps Provider's aggregate liability at "the total Fees paid by Recipient under this Agreement." At the scheduled rates (TSA Schedule A), total fees over 9 months are approximately $218,700 in fixed monthly fees plus per-cwt hauling charges (volume-dependent). Even assuming $500,000 in total fees, that is the maximum Lepund can recover from Provider for any claim, including a catastrophic organic certification failure.

**Why it matters.** The Services include milk hauling of certified-organic product (a single contamination event could trigger loss of organic certification for an entire farm, with revenue impact in the millions) and organic certification support (a missed deadline or recordkeeping failure could result in suspension, which is a closing condition under SPA § 8.1(d) and an existential business risk). The current structure leaves Lepund bearing nearly all operational risk of the transition.

**Recommended fix.**
- Amend § 8.1 to cover Provider's **breach of any obligation** under the TSA (including breach of the Service Standard in § 4.1), not just gross negligence or willful misconduct.
- Amend § 8.2 to exclude from Recipient's indemnity any Losses "to the extent caused by Provider's negligence or breach of this Agreement" (not just willful misconduct).
- Either raise the cap in § 8.4 to a meaningful multiple of fees (e.g., 3×) or carve out from the cap any Losses resulting from Provider's breach of § 7 (confidentiality) or from Provider's acts or omissions that cause loss or suspension of organic certification.

## II. SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 5. "Estimated Indebtedness" Is Used but Not Defined

**Where:** SPA § 2.2(a).

**Issue.** Section 2.2(a) provides that the cash portion is "$40,200,000, less the **Estimated Indebtedness**," but the term "Estimated Indebtedness" does not appear in Article I (Definitions) or anywhere else in the SPA. The funds flow memo in the email thread (Priya Raman, Nov. 16) treats it as the Green Mountain Farm Credit payoff amount, but the SPA itself does not say so.

**Why it matters.** Without a definition, there is no agreed mechanism for determining the deduction. If the actual payoff differs from the estimate, the parties have no contractual basis for adjustment. This creates ambiguity at the wire transfer step.

**Recommended fix.** Add a definition in SPA § 1.1: "'Estimated Indebtedness' means the payoff amount set forth in the Green Mountain Farm Credit payoff letter delivered under Section 3.2(e), including principal, accrued interest, per diem, and any prepayment charges." Alternatively, specify that the cash payment equals $40,200,000 minus the amount actually paid to Green Mountain at Closing, with a post-closing true-up if the estimate was off by more than a de minimis threshold.

### 6. "Brouloul Farmstead" Brand Is Not in IP Schedule A

**Where:** IP Assignment Agreement, Schedule A; LOI § 3.1.

**Issue.** The LOI identifies "the 'Kettlebrook Organic' **and 'Brouloul Farmstead' brands**" as IP held by IP Holdco to be assigned (LOI § 3.1). Schedule A to the IP Assignment lists only three registered trademarks: KETTLEBROOK ORGANIC (word), KETTLEBROOK ORGANIC and Design, and RIDGEVIEW PASTURE MILK. No "Brouloul Farmstead" mark appears.

While IP Assignment § 1.1(a) captures "all other Trademarks used in the Business," the narrow "Business" definition (see Issue 3) may exclude the Brouloul Farmstead brand if it is used only in connection with non-fluid-milk products or in a marketing capacity.

**Why it matters.** If the Brouloul Farmstead brand is used on any product label, packaging, or marketing material, its absence from the assignment could create a post-closing ownership dispute or require a separate license from Seller's affiliate.

**Recommended fix.** Confirm whether "Brouloul Farmstead" is a registered or common-law mark used in the Business. If so, add it to Schedule A or include a specific recital in the IP Assignment confirming that it is captured by the "all other Trademarks used in the Business" language. At minimum, require Seller to confirm in writing that no Brouloul Farmstead branding will appear on post-closing product.

### 7. TSA Permits Provider to Delegate Services Without Recipient's Consent

**Where:** TSA § 12.3.

**Issue.** Section 12.3 provides: "Provider may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, or **delegate the performance of any Service to any Person upon written notice to Recipient and without the consent of Recipient**." There is no qualification that the delegate must be qualified, licensed, or bound by equivalent confidentiality obligations.

**Why it matters.** The Services include (a) hauling certified-organic milk in dedicated bulk tank trucks operated by licensed drivers (TSA § 3.1), (b) maintaining organic certification records and system plans (TSA § 3.2), and (c) processing producer payroll under specific milk supply agreements (TSA § 3.3). Delegating any of these to an unqualified or unvetted third party could result in loss of organic certification, food safety violations, or producer payment errors. The confidentiality obligations in § 7 bind "Provider" and "Service Personnel," but a delegate that is not a "Service Personnel" (i.e., not an employee, agent, or subcontractor of Provider) might argue it is not covered.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 12.3 to require that any delegate (a) is bound by written confidentiality and data-protection obligations no less protective than § 7, (b) holds all licenses and certifications required to perform the delegated Service, and (c) is reasonably acceptable to Recipient (not to be unreasonably withheld). Alternatively, require Recipient's prior written consent for any delegation of the Organic Certification Support or Producer Payroll services.

### 8. TSA Service Standard Uses a Relative Measure

**Where:** TSA § 4.1(c).

**Issue.** The Service Standard requires Provider to perform with "the degree of care that Provider applies to **its own comparable operations**." This is a relative benchmark: if Provider's own operations are substandard, the contractual floor is equally low.

**Why it matters.** In a dispute over whether a service failure occurred, the question becomes what Provider does for its *other* clients, which may be difficult to prove and may reflect a lower standard than what the Company requires for organic compliance.

**Recommended fix.** Replace § 4.1(c) with an objective standard: "with the degree of care that a reasonably prudent provider of similar services would exercise under the same or similar circumstances, and in all events in compliance with the USDA National Organic Program, the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance, and applicable state dairy and wage payment laws."

### 9. Secretary's Certificate Omits Certification of Secretary's Authority

**Where:** Lepund Board Resolutions, Secretary's Certificate, ¶ 3.

**Issue.** The Secretary's Certificate states that "the persons holding the offices of Chief Executive Officer and Chief Financial Officer of the Corporation are duly elected officers of the Corporation and are authorized to sign the Transaction Documents." It does **not** certify that the Secretary is a duly elected officer, even though § 3.7 names the Secretary as an Authorized Officer who may execute the Transaction Documents.

**Why it matters.** If the Secretary signs any Transaction Document (e.g., a certificate of incumbency delivered at Closing, or an amendment), a counterparty could raise a corporate authority objection because the certificate does not cover the Secretary.

**Recommended fix.** Add to ¶ 3 of the Secretary's Certificate: "the person holding the office of Secretary of the Corporation is a duly elected officer of the Corporation and is authorized to sign the Transaction Documents on its behalf."

## III. MODERATE ISSUES

### 10. Non-Compete Geographic Scope Is Narrower Than LOI

**Where:** SPA § 6.5; LOI § 4.2.

**Issue.** The LOI contemplated a non-compete "covering the Business in the **northeastern United States**." The SPA limits the restriction to "the States of Vermont, New York, New Hampshire, Massachusetts or Maine"—five states. Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Pennsylvania (all typically included in "northeastern United States") are omitted.

**Why it matters.** If Seller or an affiliate launches a competing organic dairy operation in, e.g., upstate New York's neighboring Connecticut or western Massachusetts (which *is* covered) or Pennsylvania's Lehigh Valley, the non-compete may not reach it. Given that the Company's customer base includes distributors that may serve multi-state territories (see Disclosure Schedule § 11, Part A), a competitor in an adjacent uncovered state could poach customers.

**Recommended fix.** Expand the geographic scope to include Connecticut and Rhode Island, or use the LOI's "northeastern United States" formulation with a defined list of states. At minimum, add Connecticut.

### 11. Disclosure Schedule Cross-Disclosure Provision Could Narrow Representations

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule § 1.3.

**Issue.** Section 1.3 provides: "Any matter disclosed in one schedule is deemed disclosed for purposes of each other schedule where its relevance is **reasonably apparent** on the face of the disclosure." This means the Ridgeview nitrate matter (disclosed in Schedule 4.17) could be deemed disclosed against, e.g., the compliance-with-laws representation (§ 4.16), the absence-of-changes representation (§ 4.6), or the financial statements representation (§ 4.5) if a court or arbitrator finds the relevance "reasonably apparent."

**Why it matters.** Broad cross-disclosure can erode the scope of representations. For example, if the Ridgeview remediation costs are material to the Company's financial condition, one could argue the Phase II Report's cost estimate is "reasonably apparent" as a qualification to the financial statements representation, thereby reducing or eliminating Seller's indemnity for any inaccuracy in § 4.5.

**Recommended fix.** Delete § 1.3 or limit it to express cross-references only (i.e., a matter is deemed disclosed against another schedule only if the disclosure in the first schedule expressly states "see also Schedule [X]").

---

### 12. SPA Hierarchy Clause Could Subordinate SPA Protections to Ancillary Agreements

**Where:** SPA § 11.9.

**Issue.** Section 11.9 provides: "Each Ancillary Agreement controls as to its subject matter; otherwise this Agreement controls." Because the IP Assignment and TSA are Ancillary Agreements, any provision in those documents that addresses a matter "within their subject matter" will override the SPA, even if the SPA is more protective of Buyer.

**Why it matters.** This is the mechanism by which the narrow "Business" definition in the IP Assignment (Issue 3) and the asymmetric indemnity in the TSA (Issue 4) can override what might otherwise be broader protections in the SPA. For example, SPA § 4.12 represents that the Company will own "all right, title and interest in and to the Assigned IP," but if the IP Assignment's definition of "Assigned IP" is narrow, § 11.9 means the IP Assignment controls and the representation is read in light of the narrower definition.

**Recommended fix.** Add a proviso to § 11.9: "provided that nothing in any Ancillary Agreement shall limit or reduce Buyer's rights under Article IV (Representations and Warranties of Seller) or Article IX (Indemnification) of this Agreement." Alternatively, conform the ancillary agreements to the SPA before signing (as recommended in Issues 3 and 4).

### 13. TSA Force Majeure Includes Animal Disease Quarantine Without a Backstop

**Where:** TSA § 12.4.

**Issue.** Force majeure expressly includes "animal disease quarantine." If a quarantine (e.g., for a notifiable animal disease) prevents milk hauling for more than 72 hours, Recipient may engage a substitute hauler and fees are suspended. However, the provision does not address: (a) who bears the cost of the substitute hauler; (b) whether the substitute hauler must use organic-dedicated equipment; or (c) what happens if no qualified substitute is available.

**Why it matters.** In a quarantine scenario, the Company's milk supply could be interrupted, with cascading effects on production, customer deliveries, and organic certification (if milk is not processed within required timeframes). The current provision gives Recipient the *right* to engage a substitute but is silent on cost allocation and qualification requirements.

**Recommended fix.** Add to § 12.4: "Any substitute hauler engaged under this Section 12.4 shall use bulk tank trucks dedicated to certified-organic milk and shall be operated by drivers holding current bulk milk hauler and sampler licenses. The cost of the substitute hauler shall be borne by [Recipient / shared equally / Provider], and the Fees for the affected routes shall be suspended for the period of the force majeure event."

### 14. Seller Note Subordination Covers Future Credit Facilities

**Where:** Seller Note § 6.1.

**Issue.** "Senior Indebtedness" is defined to include credit facilities "in effect **from time to time**." This means any future debt Lepund incurs (including at the holding company or subsidiary level) will automatically be senior to the Seller Note.

**Why it matters.** While subordination was agreed in the LOI, the "from time to time" language means that if Lepund later refinances or takes on new project debt, the Seller Note drops further in priority without any additional negotiation. This is standard in seller-note structures but worth flagging to the client for awareness, particularly if Lepund plans to leverage the acquired assets.

**Recommended fix.** No change required if the client accepts standard subordination. However, consider adding a carve-out: "Senior Indebtedness" shall not include any credit facility incurred solely to fund the Purchase Price or the working capital of the Company post-closing, unless such facility expressly subordinates the Seller Note by agreement.
