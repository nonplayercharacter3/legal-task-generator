## Issue 1 – Ridgeview Nitrate Contamination Is Subject to the Indemnity Cap (Contrary to LOI)

**Where:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities")
- SPA § 9.5(a) (Cap applies to § 9.2(c))
- SPA § 9.5(c) (only "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" escape the Cap)
- Disclosure Schedule § 9, Part A, Item 1 (Ridgeview nitrate) vs. Part B, Item 1 (Hollis pond)
- LOI § 5.3

**Why it matters:**
The LOI expressly states: "pre-closing environmental liabilities, **including Ridgeview Farm nitrate contamination**, [are] not subject to the general cap." The SPA does not implement this. The defined term "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" is limited to "the environmental conditions and matters set forth in **Part B** of Schedule 4.17." Part B contains only the Hollis Farm manure pond ($640,000 closure cost). The Ridgeview groundwater nitrate contamination—estimated at **$3.4 million to $7.8 million** per the Phase II Report (Schedule 4.17, Part A, Item 1(d))—sits in Part A. Consequently, Ridgeview remediation costs fall under § 9.2(c) but remain subject to the $4,620,000 Cap in § 9.5(a). Because the Cap is shared between all § 9.2(a) and § 9.2(c) claims, even a moderate general-rep breach would erode the amount available for Ridgeview. At the high end of the estimate, Lepund could be out more than $3 million with no recourse.

Additionally, the VARNR has required a corrective action work plan by **January 31, 2027** (Schedule 4.17, Part A, Item 1(e))—a deadline that falls after the expected December 1 closing. Lepund will own the Company and be the regulated party when that deadline hits.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend SPA § 1.1 to define "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" as "the environmental conditions and matters set forth in **Parts A and B** of Schedule 4.17 of the Disclosure Schedule, to the extent arising from the ownership or operation of the Farms or the Plant before the Closing." This brings the Ridgeview issue within § 9.5(c) and outside the Cap and Deductible, consistent with the LOI.

## Issue 2 – Tax Indemnity Survival Period Is Shorter Than the LOI Provided

**Where:**
- SPA § 9.1(b) (tax indemnity survives "until the Maturity Date")
- Seller Note § 1.3 (Maturity Date = 18 months after Closing)
- LOI § 5.2 (tax indemnity survives "until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations")

**Why it matters:**
The Maturity Date is approximately **June 2028** (18 months from a December 2026 closing). The applicable statute of limitations for the Company's 2025 and 2026 federal income tax returns will not expire until at least **2029–2030** (three years from filing, plus any extensions). Under the SPA as drafted, if the IRS or a state opens an audit in 2029 for a pre-closing tax year, the § 9.2(b) indemnity will have expired and Lepund will bear the cost. The LOI clearly contemplated a longer survival tied to the statute of limitations.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend SPA § 9.1(b) to provide: "The obligations of Seller under Section 9.2(b) shall survive until sixty (60) days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (including any extensions) for the relevant Tax period." This restores the LOI position.

## Issue 3 – IP Assignment "Business" Definition Excludes Yogurt, Butter, and Cheese

**Where:**
- IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Business": "the production, processing and sale of **organic fluid milk**")
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Business": "the production, processing, marketing and sale of organic **fluid milk, yogurt, butter and cheese**")
- IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Assigned IP" keyed to "the Business")

**Why it matters:**
"Assigned IP" is defined as IP "used or held for use in the Business." Because the IP Assignment's "Business" definition covers only fluid milk, any trademark, recipe, starter culture, or other IP used in the **yogurt, butter, or cheese** product lines is not captured. The Company's registered trademarks (Schedule A to the IP Assignment) are in Class 29 for "Milk" and "Milk; flavored milk"—but the Company also produces yogurt and farmstead cheese under the Kettlebrook Organic brand. Starter cultures for yogurt and cheese, recipes for those products, and associated trade dress would fall outside the assignment. SPA § 4.12 (Intellectual Property rep) references "Assigned IP" as defined in the IP Assignment, so Seller's rep is correspondingly narrow.

**Recommended fix:**
Before the IP Assignment is executed, conform its § 1.1 definition of "Business" to the SPA definition: "the production, processing, marketing and sale of organic fluid milk, yogurt, butter and cheese, as conducted by Assignee as of the date of the Purchase Agreement." Alternatively, decouple "Assigned IP" from "Business" and define it as all IP "used or held for use in any product line or operation of Assignee."

## Issue 4 – TSA Liability Structure Is Heavily Asymmetric Against Recipient (Lepund)

**Where:**
- TSA § 8.1 (Provider indemnifies only for "gross negligence or willful misconduct" and confidentiality breach)
- TSA § 8.2 (Recipient indemnifies Provider for "all Losses arising out of or relating to the provision of the Services")
- TSA § 8.4 (cap = total Fees; but cap does **not** apply to Recipient's § 8.2 obligation or fee payments)

**Why it matters:**
Three compounding problems:

1. **Provider's indemnity threshold is gross negligence.** If a Provider driver causes a temperature excursion that spoils a day's milk haul, or if a payroll error underpays producers, that is ordinary negligence at most—no indemnity from Provider.
2. **Recipient's indemnity to Provider is broad and uncapped.** Section 8.2 covers "all Losses arising out of or relating to the provision of the Services." Read literally, if a milk load is rejected at the Plant due to a hauling error, the resulting loss (spoilage, lost production, customer claims) could trigger Recipient's duty to indemnify Provider. The only carve-out is Provider's "willful misconduct."
3. **The cap is one-directional in effect.** Provider's maximum exposure is the total Fees (roughly $250,000–$400,000 over 9 months, depending on hauling volumes). Recipient's exposure under § 8.2 is unlimited.

Lepund will be operationally dependent on Provider for milk hauling, organic certification support, and producer payroll for 9 months. A service failure could disrupt the entire supply chain and jeopardize organic certifications.

**Recommended fix:**
- Lower Provider's indemnity threshold in § 8.1 from "gross negligence or willful misconduct" to "negligence or willful misconduct."
- Narrow § 8.2 to cover only Losses "caused by Recipient's breach of this Agreement or Recipient's negligence," deleting the open-ended "arising out of or relating to the provision of the Services" language.
- Make the § 8.4 cap mutual and apply it to both parties' indemnification obligations (while preserving the exclusion for fee payments and confidentiality breaches).

## Issue 5 – "Estimated Indebtedness" Is Undefined in the SPA

**Where:**
- SPA § 2.2(a) ("$40,200,000, less the Estimated Indebtedness")
- SPA § 1.1 (no definition of "Estimated Indebtedness")
- Email thread, Nov. 16 (P. Raman funds flow memo references "Estimated Indebtedness (Green Mountain payoff)")

**Why it matters:**
The cash payment at closing is calculated by subtracting an undefined term from $40.2 million. If the Green Mountain payoff letter shows a different figure than what the parties anticipated, there is no contractual mechanism to resolve the discrepancy. Seller could argue the payoff amount (including per diem interest and fees) is the "Estimated Indebtedness," while Buyer could argue only principal was estimated. This creates settlement-day friction and potential dispute over the wire amount.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a definition in SPA § 1.1: "'**Estimated Indebtedness**' means the payoff amount set forth in the payoff letter delivered by Seller under Section 3.2(e), including principal, accrued interest, per diem charges and prepayment fees, but excluding any post-closing interest." Alternatively, state the specific dollar amount as a schedule item and provide a true-up mechanism.

## Issue 6 – Provider May Delegate TSA Services Without Buyer's Consent

**Where:**
- TSA § 12.3 ("Provider may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, or **delegate the performance of any Service to any Person upon written notice to Recipient and without the consent of Recipient**")

**Why it matters:**
The organic certification support service (§ 3.2) involves preparing organic system plans, maintaining pasture records, and interfacing with certifying agents. The certifying agents (NOCS, GSOC) have established relationships with the current personnel. If Provider delegates to an unknown subcontractor without Buyer's approval, the quality of organic compliance could suffer, risking certification—the very thing SPA § 8.1(d) makes a closing condition and SPA § 6.1 requires Seller to maintain pre-closing. Similarly, producer payroll (§ 3.3) involves sensitive compensation data; an unvetted delegate increases data-security risk.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend TSA § 12.3 to require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any delegation of the Organic Certification Support or Producer Payroll services. For Milk Hauling, require that any delegate use dedicated organic-certified equipment and hold the same licenses, and that Recipient be given at least ten (10) Business Days' prior notice.

## Issue 7 – SPA § 8.1(e) Closing Condition Includes Non-Consent Items

**Where:**
- SPA § 8.1(e) ("receipt of all third-party consents listed in Schedule 4.4")
- Disclosure Schedule § 2 (Schedule 4.4), Items (b) and (d)

**Why it matters:**
Schedule 4.4 lists four items:
- (a) Green Mountain Farm Credit – consent required (but debt is being paid off)
- (b) Harvest Basket – "**No consent is required**" (notice only, post-closing)
- (c) Northeast Natural Grocers – consent required
- (d) Vermont Agency of Agriculture – notice within 30 days **after** Closing

Items (b) and (d) are not consents and cannot be "received" before closing. As drafted, § 8.1(e) technically requires "receipt" of all four listed items, creating an impossible or ambiguous condition. A strict reading could give Seller an argument that closing is not ready if the Harvest Basket notice or Vt. Agency notice has not yet been "received" (they are post-closing obligations).

**Recommended fix:**
Amend SPA § 8.1(e) to read: "receipt of all third-party **consents** listed in Schedule 4.4 **for which consent (as opposed to notice) is required**." Or, more cleanly, limit the condition to items (a) and (c) of Schedule 4.4 by reference.

## Issue 8 – General Rep Survival (15 Months) Expires Before Seller Note Maturity (18 Months)

**Where:**
- SPA § 9.1(a) (general reps survive 15 months)
- Seller Note § 1.3 (Maturity Date = 18 months)
- SPA § 9.1(c) ("No claim may be brought after the expiration of the applicable survival period")
- SPA § 9.6 (set-off against Seller Note)

**Why it matters:**
Between month 15 and month 18 post-closing, the Seller Note is still outstanding, but general representation claims have expired under § 9.1(c). If a breach of a general rep (e.g., a Material Contract issue under § 4.8, or an employee benefit plan problem under § 4.15) is discovered in month 16, Lepund cannot bring a claim **and** cannot exercise the § 9.6 set-off against the Note, because the set-off right is limited to amounts "to which a Buyer Indemnitee is entitled under this Article IX"—and no entitlement exists after the survival period lapses. Lepund would be paying interest on $6 million for three additional months with no security for latent rep breaches.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend SPA § 9.1(a) to extend general rep survival to the Maturity Date: "…shall survive the Closing until the earlier of (i) fifteen (15) months after the Closing Date and (ii) the Maturity Date." Or, more simply, conform the survival to 18 months. At minimum, add a proviso to § 9.6: "For purposes of this Section 9.6, the survival period in Section 9.1(a) shall be deemed extended to the Maturity Date."

## Issue 9 – Non-Compete Geographic Scope Is Narrower Than LOI

**Where:**
- SPA § 6.5 (five states: VT, NY, NH, MA, ME)
- LOI § 4.2 ("the northeastern United States")

**Why it matters:**
The LOI contemplated a broader non-compete covering "the northeastern United States," which would typically include Connecticut, Rhode Island, and possibly Pennsylvania and New Jersey. The SPA narrows this to five states. If Seller (or an Affiliate) launches a competing organic dairy operation in Connecticut or Rhode Island after closing, Lepund would have no contractual restriction. Given that the Company's customer base includes distributors (Coastal Fresh Distributors, Greenleaf Natural Foods) whose territories may extend beyond the five stated states, this gap could be commercially significant.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend SPA § 6.5 to add Connecticut and Rhode Island, or to use the LOI's "northeastern United States" formulation with a defined list. If Seller resists, at minimum add Connecticut, which borders Vermont and New Hampshire and is within the Company's likely distribution radius.

## Issue 10 – TSA Service Standard Is Subjective and Potentially Unenforceable

**Where:**
- TSA § 4.1(c) ("with the degree of care that Provider applies to its **own comparable operations**")

**Why it matters:**
After the closing, Provider (Brouloul Family Dairy Partners) will no longer operate a dairy creamery—it sold the Company. It may have no "comparable operations" at all. If Provider's remaining business is, say, a feed store or a real estate holding, the "degree of care" benchmark is meaningless. A court could find the standard too vague to enforce, leaving Lepund with only the "substantially the same manner, scope, priority and quality as provided… during the twelve (12) months before the Closing" language in § 4.1(a)—which is itself a historical benchmark that becomes less useful over a 9-month transition as routes, volumes, and personnel change.

**Recommended fix:**
Replace § 4.1(c) with an objective standard: "with the degree of care that a reasonably prudent dairy services provider would exercise in performing similar services in the northeastern United States." Retain § 4.1(a) and (b) as additional (not alternative) requirements.
