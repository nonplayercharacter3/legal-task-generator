## 1. IP Reacquisition Right on Change of Control — CRITICAL

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement, §§ 1.1 ("Change of Control"), 8.3, 7.3

**Issue:** Odile Fenwright (founder) holds a contractual right to reacquire all Assigned IP — the wash-chemistry formulations (Schedule A), the PelDose dosing software, and all improvements — for **$1.00** if the Company undergoes a Change of Control "without Assignor's prior written consent." A Change of Control is defined as any Person acquiring more than 50% of the Company's voting stock. Our stock purchase is squarely within that definition. Fenwright has 12 months post-closing to exercise.

Section 7.3 requires the Company to give Fenwright 30 days' prior written notice of a proposed Change of Control, but Section 8.3 conditions the reacquisition right on the absence of her "prior written consent" — notice alone is not consent. There is no evidence in the folder that Fenwright has been notified or has consented.

**Why it matters:** The formulations and PelDose software are core to the Business (SPA § 4.11; LOI ¶ 3). If Fenwright exercises, Tretort loses the IP it paid $48.5 million to acquire, with no contractual right to prevent it. The SPA's Required Consents (Section 7.2(d)) list only the landlord estoppel; Fenwright's consent is not a closing condition.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Obtain Fenwright's written consent to the transaction before closing, or negotiate a waiver of Section 8.3 as a condition to closing. (b) Add Fenwright's consent (or a waiver) to the SPA's Required Consents in Section 7.2(d). (c) Confirm whether the IP Assignment Agreement is listed in Section 4.10 of the Disclosure Schedule; if not, this is a breach of the Material Contracts representation. (d) As a fallback, negotiate a specific indemnity from Seller covering the $1.00 reacquisition risk.

## 2. Environmental Objection Notice Was Late — CRITICAL

**Document / Section:** SPA § 6.7; Board Consent, Recitals (¶¶ 6–8)

**Issue:** SPA § 6.7 gives Buyer **15 Business Days** after delivery of the Phase I Report to deliver an Environmental Objection Notice. The Board Consent recites that the Phase I Report was delivered **August 3, 2026** and the Objection Notice was delivered **September 4, 2026** — approximately 22 Business Days later, roughly a week past the deadline. Section 6.7 provides that if Buyer does not deliver the notice within the period, "Buyer is deemed to have accepted the environmental conditions identified in the Phase I Report" and "waives any right to indemnification under Section 9.2(d) for those conditions."

The Board Consent acknowledges that Seller "may take the position" that Tretort accepted the conditions, and the Board "disputes" that position. But the contractual language is unambiguous.

**Why it matters:** The Phase I identified RECs related to perchloroethylene (a Hazardous Material under SPA § 1.1) from former dry-cleaning operations and historical wastewater discharge. If the waiver in § 6.7 applies, Tretort has **no indemnification under § 9.2(d)** for pre-closing environmental conditions at the plant — potentially the largest contingent liability in the deal.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Immediately assess whether there is any basis to argue the notice was timely (e.g., if "delivery" of the Phase I Report is disputed, or if a holiday shortened the count). (b) If the notice was in fact late, negotiate a specific environmental indemnity from Seller as part of the ongoing § 6.7 good-faith discussions, or as a condition to closing. (c) Consider whether Seller's course of conduct (engaging in negotiations rather than asserting the waiver) could support an argument of waiver or estoppel by Seller. (d) Ensure the Board's reservation of rights (Board Consent § 3) is preserved in all communications with Seller.

## 3. Survival Period Expiring Before Financial-Statement Claims Can Be Asserted — CRITICAL

**Document / Section:** SPA §§ 9.1, 9.4(c)

**Issue:** Section 9.1 provides that General Representations (which include § 4.7 Financial Statements and § 4.12 Customer Contracts) survive only **9 months** after the Closing Date. Section 9.4(c) provides that no claim for breach of § 4.7 or § 4.12 may be asserted until Buyer delivers audited financial statements for the **first full fiscal year** after closing, to be delivered within 120 days after year-end.

If closing occurs in, say, November 2026, the 9-month survival period expires in August 2027. The first full fiscal year is 2027; audited financials would be due by approximately April 30, 2028. The survival period lapses **eight months before** Tretort is even permitted to bring the claim. The result: Tretort can never recover for a breach of the financial statements or customer contracts representations.

**Why it matters:** These are among the most important representations in a laundry business — working capital, rebate accruals, customer concentration, and linen-loss credits all flow through them. The LOI (¶ 4) contemplated an 18-month survival period, which would have avoided this gap.

**Recommended fix:** Amend § 9.4(c) to provide that the survival period for §§ 4.7 and 4.12 is extended until the later of (a) the general survival period or (b) 30 days after delivery of the required audited financial statements. Alternatively, extend the General Rep survival period to 18 months per the LOI. This should be raised before we go back to the other side.

## 4. Seller Note "Losses" Definition Conflicts with SPA Set-Off Right — HIGH

**Document / Section:** Seller Note § 1 ("Losses") and § 5.1; SPA §§ 1.1 ("Losses"), 9.6, 11.12

**Issue:** The SPA defines "Losses" broadly to include diminution in value, attorneys' and consultants' fees, and Remediation Costs, and § 9.6 expressly permits set-off of "any Losses … including Losses consisting of diminution in value, attorneys' fees or Remediation Costs." The Seller Note, however, defines "Losses" for set-off purposes (Note § 5.1) as only "out-of-pocket damages actually paid or payable … finally awarded by a non-appealable judgment," and **expressly excludes** attorneys' fees, consultants' fees, environmental response costs, diminution in value, lost profits, and any settled amount not reduced to judgment.

SPA § 11.12 provides that the SPA prevails over Ancillary Agreements "except that each Ancillary Agreement controls matters it specifically governs." The Seller Note specifically governs set-off against it (Note § 5). The narrower Note definition likely controls, gutting the practical value of the set-off remedy.

**Why it matters:** After the escrow is exhausted (or for claims outside the escrow, such as Fundamental Reps, fraud, covenants, environmental, or taxes), Tretort's primary recovery tool is set-off against the $6 million Seller Note. If set-off requires a final non-appealable judgment and excludes fees and remediation costs, Tretort must litigate to judgment before it can reduce note payments — a years-long process for what should be a self-help remedy.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Seller Note § 1 to conform the definition of "Losses" to the SPA definition, or add a carve-out providing that the SPA's broader definition controls for purposes of Note § 5. At minimum, remove the requirement of a "non-appealable judgment" and the exclusions for attorneys' fees and Remediation Costs.

## 5. Survival Period Halved from LOI — HIGH

**Document / Section:** SPA § 9.1; LOI ¶ 4

**Issue:** The LOI specified an **18-month** survival period for General Representations. The SPA provides only **9 months**. This is a material reduction that is not explained by any document in the folder.

**Why it matters:** Nine months is short for a business with seasonal revenue patterns, long-cycle customer contracts, and potential environmental or tax liabilities that may not manifest until after the first year. Combined with Issue 3, the 9-month period is particularly dangerous.

**Recommended fix:** Restore the 18-month survival period per the LOI, or at minimum extend to 12 months. If Seller resists, consider extending only for specific reps (e.g., environmental, tax, customer contracts) while accepting 9 months for the rest.

## 6. Transition Services Agreement — Provider May Assign Without Consent — MODERATE

**Document / Section:** TSA § 11.3

**Issue:** "Provider may assign this Agreement or delegate any Services, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient." There is no requirement that the assignee have comparable capability, no approval right for Recipient, and no step-in right if the assignee underperforms.

**Why it matters:** The Company will depend on Seller's billing, dispatch, and portal systems for up to 9 months post-closing (TSA Schedule A). If Seller delegates these to a low-cost third party or winds down its own operations, service quality could degrade with no contractual recourse short of a "material breach" termination (TSA § 10.1), which carries a 30-day cure period.

**Recommended fix:** Amend § 11.3 to require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment or delegation, or at minimum require that any delegate provide services meeting the same standard in § 2.2 and that Recipient may terminate the affected Service without penalty if the delegate fails to meet that standard.

## 7. Escrow Agreement Governed by New York Law; SPA Governed by Delaware Law — MODERATE

**Document / Section:** Escrow Agreement § 12.6; SPA §§ 11.8–11.9

**Issue:** The SPA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware. The Escrow Agreement is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan. A dispute over escrow disbursement that also implicates the SPA's indemnification provisions could require parallel or sequential litigation in two jurisdictions under two bodies of law.

**Why it matters:** Creates procedural complexity, increased cost, and risk of inconsistent interpretations. In a contested claim, Seller could forum-shop or delay by invoking the New York venue.

**Recommended fix:** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and forum to Delaware (matching the SPA), or at minimum add a provision that the Escrow Agent's disbursement obligations are to be construed consistently with the SPA and that any ambiguity is resolved in favor of the SPA's terms.

## 8. Side Letter — Uncapped Seller Credit Support (Parent Guaranty) — MODERATE

**Document / Section:** Side Letter § 3; Annex 2, Item 3

**Issue:** Annex 2 lists a parent guaranty by Seller of the Company's obligations under its linen-rental agreement with [●] Hotels & Resorts, LLC, marked "Uncapped (contract obligations)." Side Letter § 3.2 requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to replace it, but if the hotel counterparty demands an amendment or refuses to accept a replacement from Tretort (which may not yet have the credit profile), the guaranty remains in place. Section 3.3 then requires Buyer to indemnify Seller for any post-closing draws.

**Why it matters:** An uncapped indemnity to Seller for post-closing draws on a hotel contract is an open-ended contingent liability. If the hotel contract has significant volume commitments or penalty provisions, this could be material.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Before closing, confirm the scope and value of the underlying hotel contract obligations. (b) Negotiate a cap on the § 3.3 indemnity (e.g., limited to the contract's remaining term value or a stated dollar amount). (c) Require Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain the hotel's agreement to a release or replacement before closing, making it a condition to closing.

## 9. TSA — No Service Levels or Specific Remedies for Underperformance — MODERATE

**Document / Section:** TSA § 2.2, § 10.1

**Issue:** The standard of performance is "substantially consistent with" the prior 12 months and "at least the same degree of care Provider uses for its own operations." There are no objective service levels (e.g., invoice accuracy rate, dispatch on-time percentage, portal uptime), no liquidated damages, and the only termination right for underperformance is for "material breach" with a 30-day cure period (§ 10.1).

**Why it matters:** Billing errors affect customer relationships and cash flow; dispatch failures affect par levels and customer satisfaction; portal downtime affects order intake. In a 9-month transition, even modest service degradation could cause customer attrition that is difficult to quantify or remedy.

**Recommended fix:** Add a service-level schedule (even if basic: e.g., 99.5% portal uptime, invoices issued by the 5th of the following month, dispatch plans delivered by 4:00 p.m. the prior day) with a remedy for chronic failure (e.g., fee credits or termination right without cure period for failure to meet SLAs in 2 consecutive months).

## 10. IP Assignment — Reversion if Company Ceases Use for 24 Months — LOWER

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement § 8.2

**Issue:** If the Company "permanently ceases to use the Assigned IP in the conduct of its business for a continuous period of twenty-four (24) months," all IP reverts to Fenwright on her request. The section carves out a "sale of the Company or its business as a going concern," so the acquisition itself does not trigger it. However, if Tretort later rationalizes the business and discontinues certain product lines (e.g., the food-and-beverage linen program, Schedule A Item 4) for 24 months, the IP for those lines could revert.

**Why it matters:** This is a long-term risk, not an immediate closing issue, but it could constrain Tretort's post-acquisition strategic flexibility.

**Recommended fix:** In the consent/waiver negotiated under Issue 1, ask Fenwright to also waive or amend § 8.2, or clarify that a strategic business decision to discontinue a product line does not constitute a "permanent cessation" for § 8.2 purposes.
