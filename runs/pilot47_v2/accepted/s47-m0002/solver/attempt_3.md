## 1. IP Reversion Right Allows Founder to Claw Back Core Intellectual Property for $1.00

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement § 8.3 (Reacquisition on Change of Control); § 1.1 (definition of "Change of Control")

**Why it matters:** The IP Assignment Agreement between Odile Fenwright (founder) and Pelmont defines "Change of Control" as any transaction in which a Person acquires more than 50% of Pelmont's voting stock. Tretort's purchase of 100% of the Shares is squarely a Change of Control. Under § 8.3, if the transaction occurs "without Assignor's prior written consent," Fenwright may reacquire all Assigned IP—the wash-chemistry formulations (Schedules A, items 1–6), the PelDose dosing software, and all related trade secrets, patents, copyrights and goodwill—for a purchase price of **$1.00**, exercisable at any time within 12 months after Closing. These are the core operating assets of the Business. The SPA's representation in § 4.11 that the Company "owns or has a valid right to use all intellectual property used in the Business" is technically true at signing but is subject to this reversion right, which is not referenced anywhere in the SPA or its Disclosure Schedule (as far as the documents in the folder show).

**Recommended fix:** Before Closing, obtain Fenwright's prior written consent to the transaction (which defeats the § 8.3 trigger), or negotiate a waiver/release of the reversion right as a condition to Closing. If consent is not forthcoming, consider (a) requiring an amendment to the IP Assignment Agreement that deletes § 8.3 or narrows it to exclude acquisitions by strategic buyers, (b) adding a specific representation and indemnity in the SPA covering the risk, or (c) treating this as a walk-away item given the centrality of the IP to the Business.

## 2. Environmental Objection Notice Was Delivered Outside the Contractual Deadline

**Document / Section:** SPA § 6.7 (Environmental Assessment); Board Consent § 1 (Recitals) and § 3

**Why it matters:** SPA § 6.7 gives Buyer **15 Business Days** after delivery of the Phase I Report to object, or else Buyer "is deemed to have accepted the environmental conditions identified in the Phase I Report" and "waives any right to indemnification under Section 9.2(d) for those conditions." Per the Board Consent, Seller delivered the Phase I Report on **August 3, 2026** (a Monday). Fifteen Business Days later is **August 24, 2026**. Buyer's officers did not deliver the Environmental Objection Notice until **September 4, 2026**—nine Business Days late. The Board Consent confirms that Seller "has informed the Company that it may take the position that the Company has accepted the environmental conditions." If Seller's position is correct, Buyer has lost its § 9.2(d) indemnity for the perchloroethylene and wastewater conditions identified in the Phase I Report—potentially the largest environmental exposure in the deal.

**Recommended fix:** Immediately assess whether the delay is curable. Options include: (a) seeking a written waiver or extension from Seller (leverage: Seller wants to close before the December 31 Outside Date); (b) arguing that Seller's own conduct (e.g., failure to deliver the Phase I Report until August 3, or miscommunication about the deadline) tolled or excused the period; (c) if neither works, negotiating a specific environmental indemnity or escrow holdback that survives regardless of the § 6.7 deemed-acceptance language. Do not proceed to Closing without resolving this.

## 3. General Representation Survival Period Cut in Half from the LOI

**Document / Section:** SPA § 9.1 (Survival); Letter of Intent ¶ 4

**Why it matters:** The LOI provided that General Representations would survive for **18 months** after Closing. The SPA as drafted provides only **9 months**. This halves the window in which Tretort can bring indemnification claims for breaches of most Seller representations (financial statements, customer contracts, material contracts, employees, etc.). Given that the first full post-Closing audit will not be available until well after 9 months (see Issue 4 below), the practical protection is even shorter.

**Recommended fix:** Restore the survival period to 18 months per the LOI. If Seller resists, 12 months is a reasonable fallback, but 9 months is inadequate for a business with complex customer rebate arrangements and environmental exposure.

## 4. Financial Statement and Customer Contract Claims Are Effectively Unassertable

**Document / Section:** SPA § 9.4(c) (Financial Statement and Customer Claims) read together with § 9.1 (Survival)

**Why it matters:** Section 9.4(c) bars any claim for breach of § 4.7 (Financial Statements) or § 4.12 (Customer Contracts and Rebates) until Buyer delivers audited financial statements for the **first full fiscal year beginning after the Closing Date**, which must be delivered within 120 days after that fiscal year ends. If Closing occurs in, say, November 2026, the first full fiscal year is FY 2027 (ending December 31, 2027), and the audited financials would be due around **April 30, 2028**. But under § 9.1, General Representations survive only **9 months** after Closing—i.e., until approximately **August 2027**. The survival period expires roughly eight months before Buyer is even permitted to make the claim. The two provisions are irreconcilable as drafted, and the Financial Statements and Customer Contracts representations are dead letters.

**Recommended fix:** Either (a) extend the survival period for §§ 4.7 and 4.12 specifically to at least 18 months after Closing (or 15 months after the end of the first post-Closing fiscal year, whichever is later), or (b) delete the condition precedent in § 9.4(c) and allow claims to be made based on the existing financial statements and customer data. Option (a) is the cleaner fix and is consistent with the LOI's 18-month survival.

## 5. Seller Note Set-Off Right Is Dramatically Narrower Than the SPA's Set-Off Right

**Document / Section:** Seller Note § 1 (definition of "Losses") and § 5.1 (Right of Set-Off); SPA § 9.6 (Set-Off) and § 1.1 (definition of "Losses")

**Why it matters:** SPA § 9.6 permits Buyer to set off "any Losses for which a Buyer Indemnitee is entitled to indemnification under this Article IX, **including Losses consisting of diminution in value, attorneys' fees or Remediation Costs**." The SPA's definition of "Losses" (§ 1.1) expressly includes those items. But the Seller Note redefines "Losses" for set-off purposes to mean only "out-of-pocket damages actually paid or payable… **finally awarded by a non-appealable judgment**… **excluding** attorneys' fees, consultants' fees, costs of investigation, sampling, monitoring, removal, remediation or other environmental response, diminution in value, lost profits, and any amount agreed or settled… that has not been reduced to such a judgment." The practical effect is that Buyer can set off against the $6,000,000 Note **only** amounts that have survived full litigation to a final, non-appealable money judgment, and **only** out-of-pocket damages. Environmental remediation costs, diminution in value, attorneys' fees, and settled claims—all of which the SPA says are set-off-eligible—are excluded. This renders the Note set-off nearly useless as a practical remedy and contradicts the LOI (¶ 2(b): "Buyer would have the right to set off indemnifiable losses against amounts owing under it").

**Recommended fix:** Amend the Seller Note's definition of "Losses" in § 1 to conform to the SPA's definition, and delete the exclusions in the Note's § 5.1. At minimum, the Note should permit set-off for (a) amounts determined by a final judgment or binding arbitration, (b) amounts agreed in a settlement, (c) Remediation Costs, and (d) diminution in value. The SPA's order-of-precedence clause (§ 11.12) is ambiguous here because the Note "specifically governs" set-off against itself, so the SPA's broader language may not control. This must be fixed in the Note itself.

## 6. Transition Services Agreement Permits One-Sided Delegation by Provider

**Document / Section:** TSA § 11.3 (Assignment)

**Why it matters:** The TSA allows the Provider (Drusisk/Seller) to "assign this Agreement or delegate any Services, in whole or in part, to **any Person** without the consent of Recipient," while Recipient may not assign without Provider's consent. Because the Services include billing, route dispatch, and customer-portal hosting—operations that touch every customer relationship—delegation to an unknown third party could degrade service quality, create data-security risks, or violate customer contracts. There is no standard-of-performance requirement for a delegatee, no notice obligation, and no right of Recipient to object.

**Recommended fix:** Amend § 11.3 to require that (a) Provider give Recipient at least 30 days' prior written notice of any delegation, (b) the delegatee be an entity of comparable capability and agree to be bound by the TSA's confidentiality, data-security, and performance standards, and (c) Recipient may object to a delegatee that is a competitor of the Business or that poses a material data-security risk.

## 7. IP Assignment Agreement Requires 30-Day Pre-Closing Notice of Change of Control

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement § 7.3 (Notice of Certain Transactions)

**Why it matters:** The Company is obligated to give Fenwright written notice of any proposed Change of Control "at least thirty (30) days before it is consummated." The SPA contains no covenant requiring Seller or the Company to give this notice, and no closing condition or delivery item addresses it. If the notice is not given, the Company (now owned by Tretort) would be in breach of the IP Assignment Agreement as of Closing. While a breach of § 7.3 does not by itself trigger the § 8.3 reversion (which requires the absence of "prior written consent"), it could give Fenwright a damages claim and, more importantly, could be argued to undermine any later assertion that the consent was "prior" or informed.

**Recommended fix:** Add to the SPA (or the Side Letter) a covenant requiring Seller to cause the Company to deliver the § 7.3 notice to Fenwright no later than 30 days before the anticipated Closing Date, and make delivery of evidence of that notice a Seller closing delivery under § 3.2. Coordinate with the fix in Issue 1 (obtaining consent).

## 8. Escrow Agreement Governed by New York Law; All Other Transaction Documents by Delaware Law

**Document / Section:** Escrow Agreement § 12.6 (Governing Law; Jurisdiction); SPA § 11.8

**Why it matters:** The SPA, Seller Note, TSA, and Side Letter are all governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware courts. The Escrow Agreement is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan. While this is not unusual (escrow agents often insist on their home-state law), it creates a split-jurisdiction risk: a dispute over the proper disbursement of escrow funds could require parallel or sequential proceedings in two different states under two different bodies of law, increasing cost and delay.

**Recommended fix:** Low priority. If Seller's counsel is amenable, conform the Escrow Agreement to Delaware law. If not, ensure the SPA's order-of-precedence clause (§ 11.12) and the Escrow Agreement's interplay provisions (§ 6.1, requiring Joint Written Instructions) are clear enough to minimize the chance of a jurisdictional dispute. This is a "nice to have," not a deal point.

## 9. Payroll Transition Services Term Is Only Three Months

**Document / Section:** TSA Schedule A, Item 4 (Payroll Processing – Service Term ends 3 months after the Effective Date)

**Why it matters:** The Company's payroll is processed through Provider's payroll vendor. A three-month window to stand up independent payroll (including tax registrations, withholding setup, and direct-deposit arrangements) is tight, particularly for a company with a multi-state workforce and complex hourly/overtime structures typical of a plant environment. If the migration is not complete by the end of the term, the Company would need to invoke the § 3.2 extension (90 days at +10% fees) or risk a payroll disruption.

**Recommended fix:** Extend the payroll Service Term to six months in Schedule A to align with the billing and IT help-desk terms. Alternatively, add a condition that the extension in § 3.2 for payroll is automatic (without the 30-day advance notice requirement) if Recipient's own payroll system is not fully operational by the original end date.
