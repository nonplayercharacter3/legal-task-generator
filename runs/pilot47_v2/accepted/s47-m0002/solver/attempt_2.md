## 1. Founder's Change-of-Control Reversion Right Over Core IP

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement § 8.3; SPA § 4.11

**Issue:** The 2021 IP Assignment Agreement between founder Odile Fenwright and Pelmont provides that if the Company undergoes a "Change of Control" (defined in § 1.1 as any acquisition of >50% of voting stock) without Fenwright's prior written consent, she may reacquire all Assigned IP—the wash-chemistry formulations (Schedule A) and the PelDose dosing software—for $1.00 within 12 months after closing. Our acquisition is squarely a Change of Control. SPA § 4.11 represents that the Company "owns or has a valid right to use" this IP, but that representation is only as good as the reversion right allows. No document in the folder evidences Fenwright's consent or waiver.

**Why it matters:** The formulations and dosing software are the core proprietary assets of the Business. If Fenwright exercises § 8.3, Tretort loses the IP it paid $48.5M to acquire, with no recourse against Seller (the reversion is a contractual right of a third party, not a breach of Seller's representations unless undisclosed in the Disclosure Schedule).

**Recommended fix:** Before closing, obtain a written waiver or consent from Fenwright under § 8.3, or an amendment to the IP Assignment Agreement deleting or narrowing the reversion right. If Seller resists, add a specific closing condition in Article VII requiring delivery of such waiver, and a specific indemnity in Article IX for loss of the Assigned IP. Confirm whether the IP Assignment Agreement is disclosed in the Disclosure Schedule; if not, it is a breach of § 4.11 and § 4.10.

## 2. Environmental Objection Notice Delivered Late – Seller Asserting Deemed Acceptance and Waiver

**Document / Section:** SPA § 6.7; Board Consent, Recitals (¶¶ 5–7) and § 3

**Issue:** SPA § 6.7 gives Buyer 15 Business Days after delivery of the Phase I Report to object, failing which Buyer "is deemed to have accepted the environmental conditions" and "waives any right to indemnification under Section 9.2(d) for those conditions." The Phase I Report was delivered August 3, 2026 (a Monday). The 15th Business Day is August 24, 2026. The Objection Notice was delivered September 4, 2026—nine Business Days late. Seller has communicated that it will assert deemed acceptance. The Board Consent (§ 3) confirms the dispute is live.

**Why it matters:** The Phase I identified recognized environmental conditions tied to historical perchloroethylene use and wastewater discharge—precisely the liabilities § 9.2(d) was designed to cover. If the waiver is enforced, Tretort bears all pre-closing environmental exposure with no indemnity backstop.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a waiver of the late-delivery issue with Seller, supported by the argument that Seller's own conduct (e.g., delay in delivering the Phase I, or failure to flag the deadline) contributed to the miss, or that equitable estoppel applies. In the alternative, seek a specific environmental indemnity for the identified RECs as part of the § 6.7 good-faith negotiation (which the SPA contemplates for timely objections). Document the dispute and preserve all rights, as the Board Consent already does. If Seller will not budge, consider whether the late notice is a material breach of the SPA that supports termination under § 10.1(c)—though this is a nuclear option given the Outside Date pressure.

## 3. Seller Note "Losses" Definition Strips Buyer's Set-Off Rights for Environmental and Other Key Losses

**Document / Section:** Seller Note § 1 (definition of "Losses"); SPA § 9.6 and § 1.1 (definition of "Losses")

**Issue:** SPA § 9.6 permits set-off against the Seller Note for "any Losses for which a Buyer Indemnitee is entitled to indemnification under this Article IX, including Losses consisting of diminution in value, attorneys' fees or Remediation Costs." The SPA's defined term "Losses" (§ 1.1) expressly includes Remediation Costs, diminution in value, and reasonable attorneys' and consultants' fees. But the Seller Note defines "Losses" for set-off purposes (§ 5.1) to mean only "out-of-pocket damages actually paid or payable … finally awarded by a non-appealable judgment," and *excludes* "attorneys' fees, consultants' fees, costs of investigation, sampling, monitoring, removal, remediation or other environmental response, diminution in value, lost profits, and any amount agreed or settled … that has not been reduced to such a judgment."

**Why it matters:** The practical effect is that Tretort cannot set off environmental remediation costs, diminution-in-value claims, or attorneys' fees against the $6M Seller Note—the very categories most likely to arise in a commercial laundry acquisition with known PERC contamination. Buyer would be forced to litigate to a final non-appealable judgment before exercising set-off, defeating the purpose of the set-off right as a self-help remedy.

**Recommended fix:** Amend the Seller Note's "Losses" definition to conform to the SPA. At minimum, include Remediation Costs, diminution in value, and reasonable attorneys' and consultants' fees. Delete the requirement of a "non-appealable judgment" for set-off purposes (the SPA only requires "written notice describing the claim in reasonable detail"). If Seller resists, argue SPA § 11.12 (order of precedence): the SPA prevails over Ancillary Agreements except where the Ancillary Agreement "specifically governs" the matter—but the SPA specifically governs set-off in § 9.6, so the Note should yield.

## 4. SPA § 9.4(c) Effectively Time-Bars Financial Statement and Customer Contract Claims

**Document / Section:** SPA § 9.4(c); § 9.1; LOI ¶ 4

**Issue:** Section 9.4(c) provides that no claim for breach of § 4.7 (Financial Statements) or § 4.12 (Customer Contracts and Rebates) may be asserted until Buyer delivers audited financial statements for "the first full fiscal year beginning after the Closing Date." The Company's fiscal year ends December 31. If closing occurs in Q4 2026, the first full fiscal year is FY 2027, and the audit must be delivered within 120 days after December 31, 2027—i.e., by approximately April 30, 2028. But § 9.1 limits General Representation survival to 9 months after closing (i.e., approximately July 2027). The condition in § 9.4(c) cannot be satisfied before the survival period expires. The result: no claim under § 4.7 or § 4.12 can ever be brought.

**Why it matters:** Misstated financials and undisclosed customer contract issues (rebates, terminations, volume reductions) are among the most common and valuable post-closing indemnity claims in a middle-market acquisition. This provision silently eliminates them. The LOI contained no such restriction.

**Recommended fix:** Delete § 9.4(c) in its entirety, or replace it with a provision that tolls the survival period for § 4.7 and § 4.12 claims until 120 days after delivery of the first post-closing annual audit (i.e., the survival period for those specific reps is extended to accommodate the audit timeline). This is a standard market provision when included, but it must be paired with a corresponding survival extension.

## 5. Survival Period Halved from LOI (18 Months → 9 Months)

**Document / Section:** SPA § 9.1; LOI ¶ 4

**Issue:** The LOI specified 18-month survival for General Representations. The SPA provides only 9 months. Combined with the § 9.4(c) problem above, the effective window for most indemnity claims is very short.

**Why it matters:** Nine months is aggressive for a business with seasonal revenue patterns (hospital and hospitality linen contracts often have year-end true-ups) and for identifying customer contract issues that surface during the first billing cycle.

**Recommended fix:** Restore the 18-month survival period per the LOI. If Seller insists on 9 months, at minimum extend survival for § 4.7, § 4.8 (Undisclosed Liabilities), and § 4.12 to 18 months or to the later of 9 months or delivery of the first post-closing annual financial statements.

## 6. Escrow Agreement Governed by New York Law; SPA Governed by Delaware Law

**Document / Section:** Escrow Agreement § 12.6; SPA § 11.8

**Issue:** The Escrow Agreement selects New York law and exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan. The SPA selects Delaware law and the Court of Chancery. A dispute over whether the Escrow Agent should release funds (e.g., based on a contested indemnity claim) could require parallel or sequential litigation in two jurisdictions under two bodies of law.

**Why it matters:** Procedural friction, inconsistent interpretations of the SPA's indemnity provisions, and increased cost. The Escrow Agent's duties are "solely those expressly set out in this Agreement" (§ 2.2), but the underlying right to funds is determined by the SPA—a Delaware document.

**Recommended fix:** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and forum to Delaware, matching the SPA. If the Escrow Agent (a New York-based national trust company) resists, at minimum add a provision that the Escrow Agent shall look to a Delaware court for interpretation of the SPA's indemnity provisions in the event of a dispute.

## 7. Transition Services Agreement: Provider May Delegate to Any Third Party Without Consent

**Document / Section:** TSA § 11.3

**Issue:** "Provider may assign this Agreement or delegate any Services, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient." There is no quality standard, no notice requirement, and no right of termination if the delegatee underperforms (beyond the general material-breach termination in § 10.1).

**Why it matters:** The Company's billing, route dispatch, and customer portal are operationally critical during the transition. If Drusisk delegates to an unknown or under-resourced third party, service quality could degrade, affecting customer relationships and revenue. The Recipient Data protections in § 6 would also extend to the delegatee by implication, increasing data-security risk.

**Recommended fix:** Require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any delegation, or at minimum require (a) 30 days' advance notice, (b) the delegatee to assume all TSA obligations in writing, and (c) a right for Recipient to terminate the affected Service without penalty if the delegatee fails to meet the § 2.2 standard.

## 8. D&O Tail Policy Not a Specific Closing Condition

**Document / Section:** Side Letter § 2.1; SPA Art. VII

**Issue:** The Side Letter requires Seller to obtain a 6-year D&O tail at its expense before closing. Side Letter § 5.1 makes this a "covenant of Seller under the Purchase Agreement for purposes of Section 9.2(b)." However, Article VII does not list delivery of the tail policy as a specific closing condition. It is only covered by the general condition in § 7.2(b) (covenants performed "in all material respects").

**Why it matters:** If Seller fails to obtain the tail, Buyer's ability to refuse closing depends on whether the failure is "material"—a fact-intensive determination. Post-closing, Buyer's remedy is an indemnity claim against Seller, which may be uncollectible if Seller has distributed the purchase price. The D&O tail protects the Company's (and thus Buyer's) directors and officers for pre-closing acts; without it, they are personally exposed.

**Recommended fix:** Add delivery of the D&O tail policy (in the form and with the limits specified in Side Letter § 2.1) as an express item in § 3.2 (Seller Closing Deliveries) and/or § 7.2(f) (Deliveries condition). This makes it a hard condition to closing.

## 9. No SNDA Required as a Closing Condition

**Document / Section:** Lease § 12.1; SPA § 7.2(d)

**Issue:** The lease is subordinate to any mortgage on the property, *provided* the mortgagee delivers a subordination, non-disturbance and attornment agreement (SNDA). SPA § 7.2(d) requires only a landlord estoppel certificate. No SNDA is required as a closing condition or delivery.

**Why it matters:** If the property is (or becomes) encumbered by a mortgage and the lender forecloses, the Company's lease could be extinguished unless an SNDA is in place. Losing the 86,000 sq. ft. plant would be catastrophic to the Business. The estoppel confirms no default exists but does not protect against a mortgagee's rights.

**Recommended fix:** Require delivery of an SNDA from the current mortgagee (if any) as a closing condition under § 7.2(d), or at minimum as a Seller closing delivery under § 3.2. Confirm in diligence whether the property is currently mortgaged.

## 10. IP Assignment Agreement: 30-Day Advance Notice of Change of Control

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement § 7.3

**Issue:** The Company was required to give Fenwright written notice of any proposed Change of Control "at least thirty (30) days before it is consummated." There is no document in the folder evidencing that this notice was given.

**Why it matters:** Failure to give notice is a breach of the IP Assignment Agreement by the Company (now a Tretort subsidiary). While this does not trigger the § 8.3 reversion (which requires absence of *consent*, not absence of *notice*), it gives Fenwright a breach claim and strengthens her negotiating position. It also raises the question of whether Seller's representation in SPA § 4.11 (no material breach of material contracts) is accurate.

**Recommended fix:** Confirm whether notice was given. If not, provide notice immediately and seek Fenwright's written acknowledgment and consent/waiver as part of the fix for Issue 1 above.

## 11. Seller Note Set-Off Notice Deadline More Restrictive Than SPA

**Document / Section:** Seller Note § 5.2; SPA § 9.6

**Issue:** The Seller Note requires set-off notice "no later than the date on which the payment affected by it is due." SPA § 9.6 requires only "written notice of the set-off describing the claim in reasonable detail" with no deadline tied to a payment date.

**Why it matters:** If a claim arises between payment dates (e.g., an environmental assessment cost incurred in February, with the next payment due March 31), Buyer must give notice by March 31 to set off against that payment. If Buyer is still quantifying the claim, it risks losing the set-off opportunity for that installment and must wait for the next one.

**Recommended fix:** Conform § 5.2 to the SPA: notice may be given at any time before or after the payment due date, and the set-off applies to the next payment or, if the claim exceeds the next payment, to successive payments.

## 12. SPA § 6.7: 10-Business-Day Negotiation Window for Environmental Resolution Is Unworkable

**Document / Section:** SPA § 6.7

**Issue:** If Buyer timely objects, the parties have only 10 Business Days to "negotiate in good faith" regarding a Phase II investigation, pre-closing remediation, or a specific indemnity. Complex environmental negotiations (scoping a Phase II for PERC plume migration, negotiating remediation obligations, drafting a specific indemnity) cannot realistically be completed in 10 Business Days.

**Why it matters:** If negotiations fail within 10 Business Days, the SPA is silent on the consequence. Does the deal break? Does Buyer proceed without resolution? This ambiguity creates leverage for Seller to stall or for the deal to fail at the Outside Date (December 31, 2026) without a clear remedy.

**Recommended fix:** Extend the negotiation period to 30 Business Days (or such longer period as the parties agree). Add a provision that if negotiations are ongoing in good faith at the end of the period, the period is extended until resolution. Clarify that failure to reach agreement does not itself constitute a breach or trigger the Outside Date, but gives Buyer the right to terminate under § 10.1(c) if Seller refuses to provide a specific indemnity for the objected matters.
