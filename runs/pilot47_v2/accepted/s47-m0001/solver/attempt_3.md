## I. CRITICAL ISSUES

### Issue 1: Film Library Definition Mismatch Between SPA and Bill of Sale

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 ("Film Library" defined as "all released and unreleased motion picture titles that Seller or any of its Affiliates distribute, **including the 2026 Release Slate**")
- SPA § 1.1 ("2026 Release Slate" defined as titles "scheduled by Seller or the Company for initial theatrical release in calendar year 2026")
- SPA § 6.8 (requires contribution of "the Film Library" by the Bill of Sale)
- Bill of Sale § 1.1 ("Film Library" defined as titles "that were **theatrically released on or before December 31, 2024**")
- Bill of Sale § 2.1 (conveys only the Bill of Sale's "Film Library")

**Why it matters:** The SPA obligates Seller to contribute the SPA-defined Film Library (which includes all unreleased titles and the entire 2026 Release Slate) to the Company before Closing. But the Bill of Sale—the actual instrument of conveyance—uses a materially narrower definition that excludes (a) all titles released after December 31, 2024, and (b) all unreleased titles, including the 2026 Release Slate. Because SPA § 11.9 provides that an Ancillary Agreement governs over the SPA with respect to its own subject matter, the Bill of Sale's definition controls the contribution. The Company will close without the 2026 Release Slate or any 2025 titles. Buyer is paying $46 million for a film library that is significantly smaller than the one described in the SPA.

This also means SPA § 4.10 (representation that "upon completion of the contribution under Section 6.8, the Company will own or hold valid and enforceable distribution rights in each title in the Film Library") will be false at Closing as to every title excluded by the Bill of Sale's definition.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Bill of Sale § 1.1 to adopt the SPA's definition of "Film Library" (all released and unreleased titles, including the 2026 Release Slate). Alternatively, if Seller intends to retain the 2026 Release Slate, the Purchase Price must be re-negotiated and SPA § 4.10 and the Disclosure Schedule must be conformed. Confirm with the client which titles were factored into the $46 million valuation.

- SPA § 4.6 (consents required: only Kestrelwave is identified)
- SPA § 7.2(d) (Closing condition: only Kestrelwave consent)
- Deal Call Notes § 6 (discusses only Kestrelwave; no mention of Ventrice)
- Closing Funds Flow Memo § 7.1 (pre-funding conditions: no Ventrice consent)

**Why it matters:** The SPA contemplates the sale of 100% of the Company's stock—unquestionably a Change of Control under § 10.1(a). The employment agreement prohibits the Company from undergoing a Change of Control without Ventrice's prior written consent. No document in the folder reflects that her consent has been sought or obtained. If the Closing proceeds without it:

1. Ventrice can resign for Good Reason within 90 days post-Closing.
2. On resignation, all Executive Titles automatically revert to her (§ 10.2(c))—no negotiation, no consideration. The Company (and therefore Buyer) would lose the distribution rights, Elements, and revenue streams for every title she originated, sourced, or recommended during the Term (June 1, 2023 through June 1, 2027).
3. Buyer would also owe her $850,000 in severance plus a pro-rated target bonus (50% of $425,000 = up to $212,500) plus 12 months of COBRA premiums.

The scope of "Executive Titles" is unknown because Exhibit A is not in the folder, but given that she has been President since June 2023 and the Company is an independent film distributor, the titles she brought in likely represent a material portion of the library.

**Recommended fix:**
- Immediately request a copy of Exhibit A to assess the scope of Executive Titles.
- Make Ventrice's written consent to the Change of Control a condition to Buyer's Closing obligation (add to SPA § 7.2) and a pre-funding condition in the funds flow memo.
- Alternatively, negotiate a waiver or amendment to § 10.2 before Closing. If she consents, confirm in writing that the consent is irrevocable and that she will not resign for Good Reason based on the Change of Control.
- If she will not consent or waive, assess whether the deal economics still work after subtracting the value of the Executive Titles and the severance cost.

### Issue 3: Escrow Agreement Makes Escrow the "Sole and Exclusive" Source of Recovery, Eliminating Buyer's Right to Pursue Seller Directly

**Documents and Sections:**
- Escrow Agreement § 7.4: "The Escrow Funds shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** of the Buyer Indemnified Parties for **all Losses under Article IX** of the Purchase Agreement, **whether or not subject to the Cap**."
- SPA § 9.6: "Any amount owed to a Buyer Indemnified Party under this Article IX shall **first** be satisfied from the Escrow Funds in accordance with the Escrow Agreement." (The word "first" implies a secondary source—i.e., Seller directly.)
- SPA § 11.9: "If any provision of this Agreement conflicts with any provision of an Ancillary Agreement, the Ancillary Agreement governs with respect to its own subject matter."
- SPA § 9.5(a): Cap = $4,600,000 (10% of Purchase Price)
- SPA § 9.5(d): Carve-outs from Cap and Basket: Fraud, Fundamental Representations, Taxes
- Escrow Agreement § 2.1: Escrow Amount = $3,450,000

**Why it matters:** SPA § 9.6 and Escrow Agreement § 7.4 are in direct conflict. The SPA says the escrow is the *first* source (implying Seller remains liable beyond the escrow). The Escrow Agreement says the escrow is the *sole and exclusive* source for *all* Article IX losses, "whether or not subject to the Cap." Under SPA § 11.9, the Escrow Agreement governs. The practical consequences:

1. **Cap is illusory.** The stated Cap is $4,600,000, but Buyer can never recover more than the $3,450,000 in the escrow. Buyer has given up $1,150,000 of potential recovery for no stated consideration.
2. **Fraud, Fundamental Rep, and Tax claims are capped at the escrow.** SPA § 9.5(d) carves these out from the Cap and Basket, implying they are uncapped. But Escrow Agreement § 7.4 expressly states the escrow is the sole source "whether or not subject to the Cap." If Seller commits fraud, Buyer's maximum recovery is $3,450,000.
3. **No recourse after escrow release.** Once the escrow is fully disbursed (at the Initial Release Date plus resolution of pending claims), Buyer has zero remedy for any Article IX claim, regardless of when the breach is discovered.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Escrow Agreement § 7.4 to provide that the Escrow Funds are the *first* source of recovery (conforming to SPA § 9.6), and that Buyer may pursue Seller directly for any amount in excess of the Escrow Funds. If Seller insists on the escrow being the exclusive source, then (a) increase the Escrow Amount to at least the Cap ($4,600,000), and (b) add a carve-out from § 7.4 for Fraud, Fundamental Representations, and Taxes so those claims remain actionable against Seller directly.

## II. HIGH-PRIORITY ISSUES

### Issue 4: Survival Periods Exceed the Escrow Claim Window, Leaving Buyer Without a Remedy for Late-Discovered Breaches

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.1: General Representations survive until the Initial Release Date. Fundamental Representations survive until "60 days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations."
- SPA § 1.1: "Fraud" is defined but has no stated survival limit (under New York law, fraud claims generally have a six-year statute of limitations, running from discovery).
- Escrow Agreement § 5.1: Initial Release Date = 120 days after the Closing Date.
- Escrow Agreement § 7.1: Claim Notices may be delivered only "at any time before the Initial Release Date."
- Escrow Agreement § 7.4: Escrow Funds are the sole and exclusive source of recovery for all Article IX Losses.

**Why it matters:** The SPA provides that Fundamental Representations survive for years (60 days beyond the SOL), and Fraud claims survive even longer. But the only source of recovery (per Issue 3) is the escrow, and claims can only be made against the escrow before the Initial Release Date (120 days post-Closing). After 120 days:

- The escrow is released to Seller (less pending claims).
- Buyer cannot make new Claim Notices (Escrow Agreement § 7.1 limits claims to before the Initial Release Date).
- Buyer has no direct right to pursue Seller (per Issue 3).

So the extended survival periods in SPA § 9.1 are meaningless. A Fundamental Rep breach discovered in month 6, or a fraud discovered in year 3, gives Buyer no remedy.

**Recommended fix:** This issue is derivative of Issue 3. If Escrow Agreement § 7.4 is amended to allow direct recovery from Seller, the survival periods will function as intended. Alternatively, extend the Initial Release Date or add a provision allowing Buyer to make claims against Seller directly for breaches discovered after the Initial Release Date but within the applicable survival period.

### Issue 5: Buyer's Uncapped Indemnification Obligation for Seller Guarantees

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1: "Seller Guarantees" includes "Seller's guarantees of the Company's minimum guarantee payment obligations to producers and licensors of motion pictures."
- SPA § 6.7: After Closing, Buyer shall use "commercially reasonable efforts" to cause Seller to be released from the Seller Guarantees. Until released, Seller keeps them in effect.
- SPA § 9.3(c): Buyer indemnifies Seller for "any Seller Guarantee, including any payment made or obligation incurred by Seller or any of its Affiliates under any Seller Guarantee."
- SPA § 9.5(e): "The Cap, the Basket and the other limitations in this Section 9.5 shall not apply to claims under Section 9.3(c)."

**Why it matters:** In the film distribution business, minimum guarantees to producers and licensors are a core operating expense. If the Company's films underperform and the minimum guarantees are not fully earned out, the guaranteed parties will look to Seller (the guarantor) for the shortfall. Under § 9.3(c), Buyer must indemnify Seller for any such payment—and this indemnity is **uncapped** (§ 9.5(e)). There is no basket, no cap, and no survival limit (covenants survive until fully performed under § 9.1).

The only mitigation is § 6.7, which requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to get Seller released. But if the guaranteed parties refuse to release Seller (which is likely, since they prefer to keep the credit support), Seller remains on the hook, and Buyer's uncapped indemnity remains live for the life of the guarantees.

**Recommended fix:**
- Request a schedule of all Seller Guarantees, the underlying obligations, and their remaining terms and maximum exposure.
- Negotiate a cap on Buyer's § 9.3(c) indemnity (e.g., limited to the stated maximum guarantee obligations).
- Add a time limit: Buyer's obligation should end when the underlying guarantees expire or are released, whichever is earlier.
- If the guaranteed parties will consent to a release at or after Closing (perhaps in exchange for a replacement guaranty from Buyer or the Company), make that a condition to Closing or a post-closing covenant with a specific deadline.

## III. MODERATE-PRIORITY ISSUES

### Issue 6: Transition Services Agreement Allows Provider to Delegate Without Recipient's Consent

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 12.3: "Provider may assign this Agreement, or delegate the performance of any Service, in whole or in part, to **any Person without the consent of Recipient**."
- TSA § 3.2 (Service Standard): Provider must perform with "at least the same degree of care, skill, priority and timeliness with which Provider performed them for Recipient's business during the 12 months before the Closing Date."
- TSA § 3.3: Provider shall use "commercially reasonable efforts to keep assigned the personnel who performed the corresponding functions before the Closing."

**Why it matters:** The Service Standard is calibrated to Seller's own historical performance. If Seller delegates the theatrical booking, participation accounting, or digital cinema delivery functions to a third party (a competitor, an under-resourced vendor, or an entity with no relevant experience), the Company has no contractual right to object. This undermines both the Service Standard (§ 3.2) and the personnel continuity obligation (§ 3.3), which are only "commercially reasonable efforts."

For a film distributor, these are not back-office functions. Theatrical booking relationships, participation accounting accuracy, and digital cinema delivery timing are operationally critical and relationship-dependent.

**Recommended fix:** Amend TSA § 12.3 to require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any delegation. At minimum, require that any delegate (a) has comparable experience in film distribution, (b) agrees to be bound by the Service Standard and confidentiality provisions, and (c) is identified to Recipient in advance.

### Issue 8: Participation Statement Timing Gap at Closing

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.12: "Producer participation statements are rendered within 90 days after each June 30 and December 31." The representation covers statements "rendered since January 1, 2023."
- SPA § 4.7: Financial statements are as of December 31, 2025.
- SPA § 3.1: Closing Date is March 31, 2026.

**Why it matters:** The participation statement for the period ending December 31, 2025 is due 90 days later—i.e., March 31, 2026, the Closing Date itself. As of the representation date (February 17, 2026) and likely as of the Closing Date, that statement will not yet have been rendered. Therefore:

- The § 4.12 representation ("Each statement rendered since January 1, 2023 was accurate") does not cover the December 31, 2025 period.
- Buyer has no contractual right to see the H2 2025 participation statement before Closing.
- If H2 2025 participation obligations are materially higher than expected (e.g., a film hit its second or third participation tier), the Company's post-Closing cash flow will be worse than the financial statements suggest, and the § 4.8 "Absence of Changes" representation (which covers the period since December 31, 2025) may not capture it because the statement hasn't been rendered.

**Recommended fix:** Add a closing deliverable requiring Seller to provide a draft or preliminary participation statement for the period ending December 31, 2025, or at minimum a certificate from Seller's controller stating the estimated H2 2025 participation liability. Alternatively, extend the § 4.12 representation to cover estimated amounts for the current period.

## IV. LOWER-PRIORITY / OBSERVATIONAL ISSUES

### Issue 10: Basket Is a Deductible, Not a Tipping Threshold

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.5(b): "Seller is not liable under Section 9.2(a) unless and until the aggregate Losses under that Section exceed $230,000 (the 'Basket'). **After that, Seller is liable only for Losses in excess of the Basket.**"

**Why it matters:** This is a true deductible. If Buyer's aggregate General Rep losses are $300,000, Buyer recovers only $70,000. In a "tipping basket" structure, Buyer would recover the full $300,000 once the threshold is crossed. The deductible structure reduces Buyer's recovery by up to $230,000 on any claim.

**Recommended fix:** This is a business judgment call. If the client is comfortable with the deductible structure (it was presumably negotiated), no change is needed. If not, propose changing to a tipping basket: "After that, Seller is liable for all Losses under that Section."

### Issue 11: Bill of Sale Non-Assignable Contracts Create Ongoing Entanglement

**Documents and Sections:**
- Bill of Sale § 2.4: If a Contributed Contract cannot be assigned without third-party consent, Seller holds it "for the benefit of the Company," passes through benefits, acts at the Company's direction, and uses "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain consent. The Company performs Seller's post-Effective Date obligations under that contract.

**Why it matters:** For any contract where consent is not obtained by the Effective Date (March 30, 2026), the Company is operationally dependent on Seller as a nominal party. This creates (a) ongoing administrative friction, (b) counterparty confusion (who is the contracting party?), and (c) a potential gap if Seller becomes uncooperative or financially distressed. The "commercially reasonable efforts" standard is weak—there is no deadline for obtaining consent.

**Recommended fix:** Require Schedule B (Contributed Contracts) to identify which contracts require third-party consent and the status of each. For any contract where consent is not in hand by the Effective Date, add a specific deadline (e.g., 60 days) for Seller to obtain consent, with a liquidated damages or indemnity provision if consent is not obtained.

### Issue 12: Seller Board Consent Authorizes Broad Officer Discretion

**Documents and Sections:**
- Seller Board Consent § 4.1: Each Authorized Officer may "approve any change to a Transaction Document that the Authorized Officer, with the advice of counsel, determines to be necessary or appropriate and **not materially adverse to the Corporation**."

**Why it matters:** This gives Seller's CEO, CFO, or General Counsel (each acting alone) the ability to make changes to the SPA, Escrow Agreement, Bill of Sale, or TSA without further Board approval, so long as the change is not "materially adverse" to Seller. A change could be neutral or even beneficial to Seller while being materially adverse to Buyer (e.g., shortening the escrow period, narrowing a representation, or adding a condition that benefits Seller). The "not materially adverse to the Corporation" standard is entirely one-sided.

**Recommended fix:** This is a Seller governance document and Buyer cannot directly amend it. However, Buyer should be alert to last-minute changes. Require that any change to a Transaction Document after the date of this consent be delivered to Buyer's counsel for review, and confirm in the closing checklist that no changes have been made without Buyer's agreement. SPA § 11.3 already requires mutual written consent for amendments, so this is a backstop—but it is worth flagging to the client.
