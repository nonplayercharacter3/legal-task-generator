## I. CRITICAL ISSUES

### 1. Ventrice Change of Control Consent Not Required as Closing Condition

**Documents:** Ventrice Executive Employment Agreement § 10.1–10.2; SPA §§ 4.6, 7.2

**Issue.** Section 10.2 of the Ventrice agreement provides that the Company "shall not undergo a Change of Control without the Executive's prior written consent." Section 10.1 defines Change of Control to include the sale of more than 50% of the Company's voting stock. The SPA contemplates a 100% stock sale—unambiguously a Change of Control.

If the Change of Control proceeds without Ventrice's consent, three consequences follow under § 10.2:
- (a) She may resign for Good Reason within 90 days post-Closing;
- (b) She receives 24 months' Base Salary ($850,000) as a lump sum; and
- (c) **All Executive Titles revert to her**, including distribution rights, Elements, marketing materials, and revenue rights.

The SPA does not list Ventrice's consent as a condition to Closing. Section 7.2 lists only five conditions, and the sole third-party consent referenced is Kestrelwave's (§ 7.2(d)). Section 4.6 represents that no consent is required "other than the consent of Kestrelwave Streaming, Inc."—a representation that appears false on its face given § 10.2 of the Ventrice agreement.

**Why it matters.** Buyer could close the transaction and, within 90 days, lose the Executive Titles entirely. We do not have Exhibit A to the Ventrice agreement in the folder, so we cannot quantify how many titles or what revenue is at stake. If the Executive Titles represent a meaningful portion of the Film Library, this could materially impair the asset base for which Buyer is paying $46 million. Additionally, the inaccuracy of the § 4.6 representation gives Buyer a technical walk right under § 7.2(a), but exercising it after investing deal costs is not a practical solution.

**Recommendation.**
- Obtain and review Exhibit A to the Ventrice agreement immediately to assess the scope of Executive Titles.
- Add Ventrice's written Change of Control consent (or a negotiated waiver/amendment eliminating the reversion right) as a condition to Closing under § 7.2.
- Alternatively, require Seller to deliver an amended and restated Ventrice agreement at or before Closing that (i) consents to the Change of Control, (ii) eliminates or narrows the reversion in § 10.2(c), and (iii) is executed by Ventrice.
- Correct the § 4.6 representation to disclose the Ventrice consent requirement.
- If Seller resists, consider a specific indemnity (carved out from the Cap, Basket, and Escrow exclusivity) for any loss resulting from the reversion of Executive Titles.

### 2. Film Library Definition Mismatch Between SPA and Bill of Sale

**Documents:** SPA § 1.1 ("Film Library"); Bill of Sale § 1.1 ("Film Library")

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Film Library" as:

> "all released and unreleased motion picture titles that Seller or any of its Affiliates distribute, **including the 2026 Release Slate**."

The Bill of Sale defines "Film Library" as:

> "all motion picture titles, whether feature-length or short-form, that Seller or any of its Affiliates owns or holds distribution rights in and that were **theatrically released on or before December 31, 2024**, together with all prequels, sequels, remakes and derivative rights in those titles."

The Bill of Sale's definition excludes (i) all titles first released in 2025, (ii) all unreleased titles, and (iii) the entire 2026 Release Slate. Because the Bill of Sale contains its own definition of "Film Library," that definition controls the scope of the contribution under § 2.1 of the Bill of Sale (per the Bill of Sale's § 1.1 interpretive rule: capitalized terms "used but not defined" in the Bill of Sale take the SPA meaning, but "Film Library" *is* defined in the Bill of Sale).

**Why it matters.** SPA § 4.10 represents that, upon completion of the contribution, the Company will own distribution rights in "each title in the Film Library" (using the SPA's broader definition). But the Bill of Sale will not actually contribute 2025 releases or the 2026 Release Slate. This means:
- The § 4.10 representation will be inaccurate at Closing;
- Buyer is paying $46 million for a Film Library that includes assets the Company will not own;
- The 2026 Release Slate—presumably a key value driver for a theatrical distributor—is not being contributed.

SPA § 11.9 provides that in a conflict, the Ancillary Agreement (Bill of Sale) governs "with respect to its own subject matter." The Bill of Sale's subject matter is the contribution, so its narrower definition controls what actually transfers. The SPA's broader definition still governs the representation in § 4.10, creating an inherent breach at Closing.

**Recommendation.**
- Conform the Bill of Sale's definition of "Film Library" to the SPA's definition, or add a separate schedule of 2025 releases and 2026 Release Slate titles to be contributed.
- If Seller intends to retain 2025 and 2026 titles outside the Company, the Purchase Price must be re-evaluated, and the § 4.10 representation must be revised to reflect the actual scope.
- Confirm what is listed on Bill of Sale Schedule A (Film Library Titles) and reconcile it against the SPA's Disclosure Schedule § 4.10.

### 3. Escrow Agreement Makes Escrow the "Sole and Exclusive Source of Recovery," Undermining SPA Survival Periods

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 7.4; SPA §§ 9.1, 9.5(d), 9.7, 11.9

**Issue.** Escrow Agreement § 7.4 states:

> "The Escrow Funds shall be the sole and exclusive source of recovery of the Buyer Indemnified Parties for all Losses under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **whether or not subject to the Cap**."

The Escrow is released to Seller 120 days after Closing (the Initial Release Date, per Escrow Agreement § 5.1), less any Pending Claim Amount. After that date, no Escrow Funds remain.

SPA § 9.1 provides that:
- General Representations survive until the Initial Release Date (120 days);
- **Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations** (potentially 4–6+ years);
- Covenants survive until fully performed.

SPA § 9.5(d) carves Fundamental Reps, Fraud, and Taxes out of the Cap and Basket. SPA § 9.7 preserves Fraud claims outside the exclusive remedy.

The conflict: After the Initial Release Date, the Escrow is empty. If a Fundamental Rep breach is discovered in year two (e.g., a capitalization defect or a tax liability), Buyer has no Escrow Funds to claim against. Section 7.4's "sole and exclusive source" language, read literally, eliminates Buyer's remedy entirely. SPA § 11.9 resolves conflicts in favor of the Ancillary Agreement, so the Escrow Agreement's limitation would control.

**Why it matters.** This provision effectively converts the long survival periods for Fundamental Representations and covenants into a 120-day window. It also caps Buyer's recovery for Fundamental Reps (which should be uncapped under § 9.5(d)) at the Escrow Amount ($3.45 million). For a $46 million acquisition, this is an unacceptable limitation on Buyer's protection against the most serious types of misrepresentation.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend Escrow Agreement § 7.4 to provide that the Escrow Funds are the **first** source of recovery (not the sole and exclusive source), and that Buyer may pursue Seller directly for any amount in excess of the Escrow or for claims arising after the Escrow is released.
- Alternatively, carve Fundamental Representations, Fraud, and Taxes out of § 7.4 entirely, consistent with SPA § 9.5(d) and § 9.7.
- If Seller insists on the exclusive-source language, increase the Escrow Amount to at least equal the Cap ($4.6 million) and extend the Initial Release Date to align with the longest survival period.

### 4. Escrow Amount Is Less Than the Indemnification Cap

**Documents:** SPA §§ 1.1 (Escrow Amount), 9.5(a) (Cap); Escrow Agreement § 7.4

**Issue.** The Escrow Amount is $3,450,000 (approximately 7.5% of the Purchase Price). The Cap on Seller's liability for General Rep breaches is $4,600,000 (10% of the Purchase Price). Because the Escrow is the exclusive source of recovery (see Issue 3 above), Buyer's practical maximum recovery for General Rep claims is $3,450,000—not the stated Cap of $4,600,000. The Cap is therefore illusory.

**Why it matters.** Buyer is exposed for the $1,150,000 gap between the Escrow and the Cap with no recourse. In a film distribution business, where participation disputes, contract breaches, and title defects can easily generate seven-figure losses, this gap is material.

**Recommendation.**
- Increase the Escrow Amount to at least $4,600,000 to match the Cap, or
- Amend Escrow Agreement § 7.4 to permit recovery from Seller beyond the Escrow (see Issue 3), or
- Reduce the Cap to $3,450,000 to match the Escrow (less favorable to Buyer, but at least internally consistent).

The first option is strongly preferred.

## II. SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 5. Basket Is a True Deductible, Not a Tipping Threshold

**Documents:** SPA § 9.5(b)

**Issue.** Section 9.5(b) provides: "Seller is not liable under Section 9.2(a) unless and until the aggregate Losses under that Section exceed $230,000 (the 'Basket'). **After that, Seller is liable only for Losses in excess of the Basket.**"

This is a deductible: if Buyer's aggregate losses are $300,000, Buyer recovers only $70,000. A tipping (or "threshold") basket would provide that once losses exceed $230,000, Seller is liable for all losses from dollar one (i.e., $300,000).

**Why it matters.** The deductible structure reduces Buyer's recovery by the first $230,000 of losses. Combined with the Escrow limitation (Issue 4), Buyer's effective maximum recovery for General Reps is $3,450,000 − $230,000 = $3,220,000.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate a tipping basket: "After that, Seller is liable for all Losses under Section 9.2(a)." If Seller resists, at minimum reduce the Basket to $100,000 or less.

### 6. Buyer's Indemnity for Seller Guarantees Is Uncapped and Potentially Open-Ended

**Documents:** SPA §§ 1.1 ("Seller Guarantees"), 6.7, 9.3(c), 9.5(e)

**Issue.** Section 9.3(c) requires Buyer to indemnify Seller for "any Seller Guarantee, including any payment made or obligation incurred by Seller or any of its Affiliates under any Seller Guarantee." Section 9.5(e) expressly excludes these claims from the Cap, Basket, and all other limitations. The definition of "Seller Guarantees" in § 1.1 includes guarantees "whether arising before or after the Closing" and specifically includes "Seller's guarantees of the Company's minimum guarantee payment obligations to producers and licensors."

Section 6.7 requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain releases of the Seller Guarantees post-Closing, but until released, Seller must keep them in effect.

**Why it matters.** In the independent film distribution business, minimum guarantees to producers can be substantial (often $1–10+ million per title). If a guarantee is called before Buyer obtains a release, Buyer must indemnify Seller for the full amount with no cap. The "arising after the Closing" language in the definition is particularly concerning: it could capture new guarantees Seller voluntarily extends post-Closing, for which Buyer would still be liable.

**Recommendation.**
- Delete "whether arising before or after the Closing" from the "Seller Guarantees" definition, or limit it to guarantees arising from pre-Closing obligations.
- Cap Buyer's indemnity under § 9.3(c) at a stated amount (e.g., the aggregate face value of all Seller Guarantees listed in the Disclosure Schedule as of the Closing Date).
- Require Seller to deliver a schedule of all existing Seller Guarantees (with counterparty, face value, and expiration) in the Disclosure Schedule so Buyer can assess exposure.
- Add a time limit on Buyer's release efforts (e.g., Buyer's obligation under § 6.7 terminates 18 months post-Closing, after which Seller bears the risk of any guarantee being called).

### 7. Transition Services Agreement Permits Provider to Assign or Delegate Without Consent

**Documents:** TSA § 12.3

**Issue.** Section 12.3 provides: "Provider may assign this Agreement, or delegate the performance of any Service, in whole or in part, to any Person **without the consent of Recipient**."

**Why it matters.** The three Services (theatrical booking, participation accounting, digital cinema delivery) are operationally critical during the 9-month transition. Theatrical booking requires relationships with exhibitors; participation accounting requires specialized knowledge of the Company's producer contracts and guild obligations; digital cinema delivery requires technical infrastructure. If Seller delegates these to an unknown third party without Buyer's approval, service quality could degrade, potentially harming the Company's revenue and compliance.

The asymmetry is notable: Recipient (the Company/Buyer) may not assign without Provider's consent, but Provider may assign freely.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend § 12.3 to require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment or delegation.
- Alternatively, require that any delegate meet the Service Standard in § 3.2 and that Provider remains fully liable for the delegate's performance.
- Add a right for Recipient to terminate the affected Service (without penalty) if Provider delegates to a Person with which Recipient has a competitive or confidentiality conflict.

### 8. Bill of Sale – Non-Assignable Contracts Remain with Seller

**Documents:** Bill of Sale § 2.4; SPA §§ 4.9, 4.10

**Issue.** Section 2.4 of the Bill of Sale provides that if a Contributed Contract "cannot be assigned without the consent of a third party that has not been obtained by the Effective Date," the Bill of Sale does not assign it. Instead, Seller holds the contract "for the benefit of the Company" and must pass through benefits and act at the Company's direction.

**Why it matters.** The Effective Date is March 30, 2026—the day before Closing. If any material producer, licensor, or distribution contracts have not been assigned by that date, the Company will not hold them directly at Closing. The Company would depend on Seller's cooperation to enforce or benefit from those contracts. This creates:
- Uncertainty about which contracts the Company actually owns;
- Ongoing operational dependence on Seller beyond the TSA period;
- Potential conflicts if Seller's interests diverge from the Company's (e.g., Seller has its own film distribution business).

The SPA's § 4.10 representation (that the Company will own rights "free and clear of all Liens other than the rights of producers and licensors") does not address the possibility that the underlying contracts themselves have not been assigned.

**Recommendation.**
- Require Seller to deliver a schedule of all Contributed Contracts for which third-party consent is needed, with the status of each consent request, in the Disclosure Schedule.
- Add a condition to Closing (or a specific representation) that all Contributed Contracts involving annual payments over a stated threshold (e.g., $250,000, consistent with § 4.9(a)) have been assigned or that consents have been obtained.
- Limit the "hold for benefit" period in § 2.4 (e.g., 90 days post-Closing), after which Seller must either obtain consent or the contract is deemed assigned.

### 9. General Representation Survival Is Only 120 Days

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 5.1

**Issue.** General Representations survive only until the Initial Release Date (120 days post-Closing). For a film distribution company, many issues do not manifest within 120 days:
- Participation and residual disputes often surface when statements are rendered (semi-annually, per SPA § 4.12);
- Exhibitor settlement disputes can take months to resolve;
- Title defects may only become apparent when a film is picked up for sub-distribution or digital release;
- Contract breaches (e.g., by a producer or licensor) may not be discovered until a reporting period closes.

**Why it matters.** If a General Rep breach is discovered 130 days post-Closing, Buyer has no indemnification claim (the rep has expired) and no Escrow to claim against (it has been released). The 120-day window is too short for this type of business.

**Recommendation.**
- Extend General Rep survival to at least 18 months post-Closing (to cover two full participation statement cycles and one annual audit period).
- If the Escrow is to remain the exclusive source of recovery, extend the Initial Release Date correspondingly.
- Alternatively, carve specific reps (e.g., § 4.10 Film Library, § 4.12 Participations, § 4.9 Material Contracts) out of the 120-day limit and give them survival until the later of (a) 18 months post-Closing or (b) 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations.

## III. MODERATE ISSUES

### 10. Outside Date Provides Only 30 Days of Buffer

**Documents:** SPA § 8.1(b); Deal Call Notes § 3.3

**Issue.** The target Closing Date is March 31, 2026, and the outside date is April 30, 2026—only 30 days. The Kestrelwave consent (a Closing condition under § 7.2(d)) was still in negotiation as of the March 10 deal call. If Kestrelwave's "minor comments" (Deal Call Notes § 6.1) prove more substantive than expected, or if any other condition is delayed, the outside date could be reached before all conditions are satisfied.

**Why it matters.** If the outside date passes, either party may terminate (unless the delay is caused by the terminating party's breach). Buyer would lose its deal protections and any time/money invested.

**Recommendation.**
- Extend the outside date to May 31, 2026 (60 days of buffer), or
- Add a provision that the outside date is automatically extended by the number of days any condition remains unsatisfied due to Seller's failure to use commercially reasonable efforts (per § 6.3).

### 11. Kestrelwave Consent Not Yet Executed

**Documents:** SPA §§ 4.6, 7.2(d); Deal Call Notes §§ 6.1–6.2

**Issue.** The Kestrelwave consent is a condition to Closing under § 7.2(d). As of the March 10 deal call, Kestrelwave had returned a form of consent "with minor comments" that Seller's counsel was still reviewing. The consent had not been signed.

**Why it matters.** If the consent is not delivered at Closing, Buyer has no obligation to close (§ 7.2(d)). The "minor comments" could impose ongoing obligations on the Company (e.g., most-favored-nations pricing, volume commitments, or termination rights) that affect the business model Buyer is acquiring.

**Recommendation.**
- Track this as a critical path item. Buyer's counsel should review Kestrelwave's comments before Seller accepts them (as agreed in Deal Call Notes § 6.2).
- Confirm the final consent is delivered no later than the closing call on March 31.
- If Kestrelwave's comments impose material ongoing obligations, assess the impact on the Company's financial projections and the Purchase Price.

### 12. Participation Statements for Period Ending December 31, 2025 May Not Be Rendered at Closing

**Documents:** SPA § 4.12

**Issue.** Section 4.12 provides that "Producer participation statements are rendered within 90 days after each June 30 and December 31." The statement for the period ending December 31, 2025 would be due by approximately March 31, 2026—the Closing Date. The representation covers statements "rendered since January 1, 2023," but if the December 2025 statement has not yet been rendered, it falls outside the representation.

**Why it matters.** Buyer would close without knowing the final participation obligations for the most recent six-month period. If the statement reveals significant underpayments or disputes, Buyer absorbs that risk (subject to the limited indemnification).

**Recommendation.**
- Require Seller to deliver the December 31, 2025 participation statements (or a certified draft) as a Closing deliverable.
- Add a specific representation that, based on the Company's books and records, no participant has claimed underpayment or dispute for the period ending December 31, 2025.
- Alternatively, make the delivery of the final statements a condition to Closing.

### 13. Closing Effective Time Precedes Actual Funding by Approximately 11 Hours

**Documents:** SPA § 3.1; Funds Flow Memorandum § 7.3

**Issue.** The SPA provides that "The Closing is effective as of 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on the Closing Date." The Funds Flow Memorandum states that Buyer intends to initiate wires "by 11:00 a.m. Eastern Time on March 31, 2026." This creates an approximately 11-hour gap during which the Closing is legally effective (ownership has transferred) but funds have not moved.

**Why it matters.** If a wire fails or is delayed, the legal Closing has occurred but the economic exchange is incomplete. This could create disputes over whether the Closing "took place" for purposes of the conditions, the outside date, and risk allocation. It also means the Company is legally owned by Buyer for a period during which Seller still holds the purchase price.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend SPA § 3.1 to provide that the Closing is effective at the time both wires are confirmed received (or a specified time on the Closing Date, e.g., 12:01 p.m.).
- Alternatively, add a provision that if wires are not confirmed by a specified time (e.g., 2:00 p.m.), the Closing is deemed not to have occurred and the parties revert to pre-Closing status.

### 14. Transition Services Agreement Lacks Specific Service Level Agreements

**Documents:** TSA § 3.2

**Issue.** The Service Standard in § 3.2 requires Provider to perform with "at least the same degree of care, skill, priority and timeliness" as the prior 12 months. This is a subjective standard with no objective metrics (e.g., "participation statements rendered within X days of period end," "DCPs delivered to exhibitors by Y time on release date").

**Why it matters.** Without specific SLAs, it is difficult to establish a breach. If Provider's participation accounting is late or inaccurate, causing guild disputes or producer claims, Buyer would need to prove that the service fell below the prior 12-month standard—a fact-intensive and potentially litigious determination. The liability cap in § 10.2 (total Service Fees) further limits recovery.

**Recommendation.**
- Add specific SLAs to Schedule A for each Service (e.g., theatrical booking reports delivered within 5 Business Days of settlement; participation statements rendered within 90 days of period end; DCPs delivered no later than 48 hours before scheduled release date).
- Provide that failure to meet an SLA constitutes a material breach if it continues for more than 30 days after written notice.
- Consider adding liquidated damages for repeated SLA failures (e.g., a credit against Service Fees for each missed deadline).

### 15. Escrow Tax Treatment Creates Immediate Tax Liability for Buyer

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement §§ 4.1–4.3

**Issue.** Section 4.1 provides that Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds for tax purposes, and all income is reported as Buyer's income. Section 4.3 provides for quarterly distributions of 40% of income to cover Buyer's tax, but only "at Buyer's written request."

**Why it matters.** Buyer will have a current-year tax liability on the Escrow income (however modest) even though the principal may ultimately be paid to Seller. If Buyer fails to request the quarterly distributions, it could face a tax underpayment penalty.

**Recommendation.**
- Calendar the quarterly distribution requests (within 30 days after each quarter-end).
- Confirm with the tax associate that the 40% distribution is sufficient to cover Buyer's actual tax rate on the Escrow income (if Buyer is in a higher bracket, 40% may be insufficient).
- Consider amending § 4.3 to make the distributions automatic rather than request-based.

## IV. LOWER-PRIORITY OBSERVATIONS

### 16. "Seller Guarantees" Definition Captures Post-Closing Guarantees

**Documents:** SPA § 1.1 ("Seller Guarantees")

**Issue.** The definition includes guarantees "whether arising before or after the Closing." Read literally, if Seller voluntarily extends a new guarantee for a Company obligation after Closing, it is a "Seller Guarantee" and Buyer must indemnify Seller under § 9.3(c) (uncapped, per § 9.5(e)) if it is called.

**Recommendation.** Limit the definition to guarantees arising from pre-Closing obligations, or delete the "after the Closing" language. (Addressed in Issue 6 above.)

### 17. Bill of Sale Does Not Address Transfer Tax or Recording Costs

**Documents:** Bill of Sale §§ 2.1, 2.3, 5.1

**Issue.** The contribution is structured as a capital contribution (§ 2.3), which should not trigger transfer taxes. However, § 5.1 requires recording of copyright assignments with the U.S. Copyright Office. There is no allocation of recording fees or any potential transfer tax (e.g., if a state imposes a tax on the contribution).

**Recommendation.** Add a provision that each party bears its own costs of recording and that Seller is responsible for any transfer or documentary taxes arising from the contribution.

### 19. SPA § 4.7 (Financial Statements) – Only One Year of Audited Financials

**Documents:** SPA § 4.7

**Issue.** Seller has delivered only the audited balance sheet as of December 31, 2025 and related statements for the year then ended. There is no representation regarding prior-year financials or interim (YTD) financials for 2026.

**Why it matters.** For a business being acquired for $46 million, a single year of audited financials is thin. Buyer should have visibility into 2026 year-to-date performance, especially given the 2026 Release Slate (which, per Issue 2, may not even be contributed to the Company).

**Recommendation.**
- Require delivery of unaudited YTD financials for 2026 (through at least February 28, 2026) as a Closing deliverable.
- Add a representation that the YTD financials are prepared on a basis consistent with the audited 2025 financials and fairly present the Company's results.
- Consider adding a "no Material Adverse Effect since December 31, 2025" representation specific to the YTD period (the existing § 4.8 covers this but is a General Rep subject to the 120-day survival).

### 20. Funds Flow Memorandum – Wire Instruction Security

**Documents:** Funds Flow Memorandum § 6; SPA § 2.2(b)

**Issue.** The Funds Flow Memorandum provides for telephone confirmation of wire instructions (good practice). However, the SPA only requires Seller to deliver written instructions "at least three Business Days before the Closing Date." There is no contractual requirement for telephone verification.

**Recommendation.** Add to the SPA (or the closing checklist) a requirement that Seller confirm wire instructions by telephone to a number previously provided, consistent with the Funds Flow Memorandum. This protects against wire fraud.
