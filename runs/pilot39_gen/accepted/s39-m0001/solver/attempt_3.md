## Summary

I have reviewed all five documents in the matter folder. I have identified ten issues, three of which are critical and should be resolved before we return to the other side. The most serious problems are (1) a definitional mismatch between the SPA and the Escrow Agreement that effectively strips the $3 million Extended Holdback of its intended protection for food safety and tax claims, (2) a provision in the Transition Services Agreement under which Trouseind indemnifies the Seller for the Seller's own ordinary negligence in producing inflight meals, and (3) a Seller side letter that would cut tax survival to nine months—less time than it takes to file the very returns at issue.

## Tier 1 – Critical Issues

### 1. Escrow Agreement "Fundamental Representation Claims" Definition Is Narrower Than the SPA's "Seller Fundamental Representations"

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Fundamental Representation Claims"); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Seller Fundamental Representations"); SPA § 2.4; SPA § 9.6(b); SPA § 11.10.

**The problem.** The SPA defines "Seller Fundamental Representations" to include Sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, **3.14 (Taxes)** and **3.17 (Food Safety and Regulatory Compliance)**. SPA § 2.4 provides that the $3,000,000 Extended Holdback "shall secure claims by Buyer Indemnitees for breach of the Seller Fundamental Representations **and** for Pre-Closing Taxes under Section 9.2(c)." SPA § 9.6(b) confirms that the Extended Holdback is the second source of recovery for "claims for breach of the Seller Fundamental Representations or under Section 9.2(c)."

The Escrow Agreement, however, defines "Fundamental Representation Claims" as claims for breach of "Sections 3.1, 3.2 **and** 3.3 of the Purchase Agreement" only. Escrow Agreement § 5.3(a) then provides that the Extended Holdback "shall be available **solely** for Fundamental Representation Claims" and that the Escrow Agent "shall not disburse any portion of the Extended Holdback Amount to Buyer for any other Claim."

Because SPA § 11.10 provides that "the Escrow Agreement shall govern the disbursement of the Escrow Funds," the Escrow Agent would be contractually bound to release the Extended Holdback only for claims under 3.1, 3.2, or 3.3. A food safety claim under 3.17 or a Pre-Closing Tax claim under 9.2(c) would fall outside the Extended Holdback entirely, even though the SPA expressly contemplates that they should be secured by it.

**Why it matters.** Food safety is the single greatest operational risk in this business. The Company prepares inflight meals for commercial airlines; a contamination event, recall, or FDA action could generate eight- or nine-figure losses. The parties clearly intended (per the SPA) to ring-fence $3 million for exactly this scenario, with a three-year holdback period. As drafted, the Escrow Agreement defeats that intent. Similarly, the Company has been in Seller's consolidated group since January 1, 2019 (SPA § 3.14(e)), creating potential exposure under Treas. Reg. § 1.1502-6 that the SPA assigns to the Extended Holdback.

**Recommended fix.** Amend Escrow Agreement § 1.1 to define "Fundamental Representation Claims" as claims for breach of the "Seller Fundamental Representations" as defined in the Purchase Agreement (i.e., Sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.14, and 3.17), and to include claims under Section 9.2(c) (Pre-Closing Taxes). Alternatively, add a separate defined term "Extended Holdback Claims" that tracks SPA § 2.4 and use it in § 5.3(a). The SPA's "Seller Fundamental Representations" definition should not be changed; the Escrow Agreement should conform to it.

### 2. TSA § 8.1: Recipient Indemnifies Provider for Provider's Own Negligence

**Where:** Transition Services Agreement § 8.1 (indemnification by Recipient) and § 8.2 (indemnification by Provider).

**The problem.** TSA § 8.1 provides:

> "Recipient shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless Provider, its Affiliates and their respective officers, directors, employees and agents (the 'Provider Indemnitees') from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to the provision or receipt of the Services, **including any negligence of Provider**. The only exception is Losses for which Provider is obligated to indemnify the Recipient Indemnitees under Section 8.2."

TSA § 8.2 limits Provider's indemnity to "the **gross negligence or willful misconduct** of Provider or its personnel."

Read together, this means that if Provider's employees handle food in a negligently (but not grossly negligent) manner—cross-contamination, failure to follow a temperature protocol, mishandling of allergens—Trouseind must indemnify Seller for the resulting losses. Seller's own indemnity obligation is triggered only by gross negligence or willful misconduct, a far higher threshold.

**Why it matters.** This is a food production business. Ordinary negligence in food handling (e.g., failing to segregate allergens, improper cooling, sanitation lapses) is the most common cause of foodborne illness events. If a negligently prepared meal causes an outbreak on an airline, the resulting claims (from passengers, the airline, regulators) could be substantial. As drafted, Trouseind bears that risk while Seller is protected. This inverts the standard allocation of risk in a services agreement and is inconsistent with the overall deal structure, in which Seller is representing (SPA § 3.17) that the Company has been in compliance with Food Safety Laws since 2021 and is providing a five-year non-compete specifically to protect the food safety value of the business.

**Recommended fix.** Delete "including any negligence of Provider" from TSA § 8.1. Recast § 8.1 so that Recipient indemnifies Provider only for Losses arising from Recipient's own negligence or willful misconduct in directing the Services. Recast § 8.2 so that Provider indemnifies Recipient for Losses arising from Provider's negligence (not just gross negligence) or willful misconduct in performing the Services. At a minimum, the threshold in § 8.2 should be lowered to ordinary negligence.

### 3. Seller Side Letter: Tax Survival Cut to Nine Months

**Where:** Seller Side Letter § 2.1 (proposed replacement of SPA § 9.1(b)); SPA § 9.1(b) (current); SPA § 6.8(a)–(b); SPA § 3.14(e).

**The problem.** Current SPA § 9.1(b) provides that the tax representations (Section 3.14) and the Pre-Closing Tax indemnity (Section 9.2(c)) survive "until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (giving effect to any waiver, mitigation or extension thereof)." For federal income tax, the general statute of limitations is three years from the date the return is filed (26 U.S.C. § 6501(a)); for substantial omissions, it is six years.

The Seller's side letter would replace this with survival of only "nine (9) months after the Closing Date." Assuming a March 31, 2026 closing, tax claims would be time-barred on December 31, 2026.

This is inadequate for several reasons:

- The Company's 2025 federal income tax return (a Pre-Closing Tax Period) will not be filed until at least April 15, 2026, and possibly as late as October 15, 2026 (with extension). Under SPA § 6.8(b), Seller prepares it and delivers it to Buyer for review 30 days before filing. The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the return is actually filed. If filed in October 2026, Trouseind would have only two and a half months to investigate and assert a claim before the nine-month period expires.
- The 2026 straddle period return (which Buyer prepares under SPA § 6.8(c)) will not be due until 2027 at the earliest. A nine-month survival from a March 2026 closing expires before that return is even filed.
- The Company has been in Seller's consolidated group since January 1, 2019 (SPA § 3.14(e)). Pre-Closing Taxes include taxes of the Seller Group attributable to the Company under Treas. Reg. § 1.1502-6 (SPA § 1.1, definition of "Pre-Closing Taxes," clause (b)). Unwinding seven years of consolidated group allocations could surface issues well beyond nine months.
- Nine months is shorter than the general 18-month survival for non-fundamental representations (SPA § 9.1(a)), which is internally inconsistent and signals that the Seller is attempting to use the side letter to extract a concession that the SPA's structure does not support.

**Why it matters.** Pre-Closing Tax exposure in a consolidated group context can be significant. If the Seller Group had intercompany transactions with the Company that were not properly allocated, or if the Company benefited from group-level tax attributes (NOLs, credits) that are no longer available post-closing, the economic burden could fall on the Company (now owned by Trouseind) unless the indemnity is still available. Nine months is not enough time to discover, quantify, and assert these claims.

**Recommended fix.** Do not accept the side letter as drafted. The current SPA § 9.1(b) (survival until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations) is market-standard for tax matters and should be retained. If the Seller is concerned about open-ended exposure, a reasonable compromise would be survival until the later of (a) 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations or (b) 24 months after the Closing Date, with the Extended Holdback securing such claims for the full three-year period (as SPA § 2.4 already provides). **This requires a business decision from the client** on how much tax exposure they are willing to accept versus the purchase price benefit of conceding.

## Tier 2 – High-Priority Issues

### 4. TSA § 12.3: Provider May Assign or Delegate Without Recipient's Consent

**Where:** Transition Services Agreement § 12.3 (Assignment).

**The problem.** TSA § 12.3 provides that "Provider may assign or delegate this Agreement, or any of its rights or obligations hereunder, in whole or in part, to any Person **without the consent of Recipient**." By contrast, Recipient may not assign without Provider's consent.

**Why it matters.** The entire purpose of the TSA is to ensure continuity of food production at the Milwaukee Commissary during the transition. If Seller can delegate the Meal Production Services to a third party without Trouseind's approval, Trouseind loses control over who is actually preparing the meals, what food safety standards apply, and who is subject to the insurance and indemnity requirements in the TSA. In a regulated food safety context, this is unacceptable.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 12.3 to require Provider to obtain Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) before assigning or delegating any of its obligations under the Agreement, or at minimum before delegating the Meal Production Services. If the business rationale is to allow Seller to use a subcontractor for non-critical Support Services, a carve-out for IT or logistics sub-contracting (with notice) could be acceptable, but food production should not be delegable without consent.

### 5. TSA § 9.2: Liability Cap Tied to Total Fees May Be Inadequate

**Where:** Transition Services Agreement § 9.2 (Cap); § 9.1 (Exclusion of Consequential Damages).

**The problem.** TSA § 9.2 caps each party's aggregate liability (other than Article VIII indemnity obligations) at "the total Fees paid and payable under this Agreement." While the cap does not apply to indemnity obligations under Article VIII, the practical effect depends on how the indemnity provisions are drafted (see Issue 2 above). If the indemnity is fixed so that Provider covers its negligence, the cap in § 9.2 would not limit that exposure. But if the indemnity remains as currently drafted (Recipient covers Provider's negligence), then the cap on Provider's liability for its own claims (e.g., breach of the standard of performance in § 2.2, failure to maintain the food safety program under § 2.3) is limited to the Fees.

Additionally, § 9.1 excludes consequential damages and lost profits (except for third-party amounts under Article VIII and breach of confidentiality). In a food safety context, the most significant losses (airline customer termination, brand damage, regulatory fines) are arguably consequential.

**Why it matters.** If a food safety failure during the transition causes an airline customer to terminate its contract with the Company, the lost revenue could far exceed the TSA Fees. The current structure may leave Trouseind without adequate recourse.

**Recommended fix.** First, fix the indemnity (Issue 2). Second, consider carving out Food Safety Laws violations from the consequential damages exclusion in § 9.1, or at minimum from the cap in § 9.2. Third, confirm that the insurance requirements in TSA § 7.1 (particularly the $2M product contamination and $5M CGL) are adequate for the risk profile, and that the Provider's insurance is the primary source of recovery for third-party food safety claims. **This may require a business decision** on the appropriate level of risk retention.

## Tier 3 – Medium-Priority Issues

### 6. Company's Insurance Program: Umbrella Does Not Layer Over Product Contamination Policy

**Where:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, Section 2 (Policy Summary), Policy No. 3.

**The problem.** The Commercial Umbrella Liability policy (Policy No. 3, $15,000,000) is stated to be "following form over Policies 1, 4 and 6." It does **not** follow form over Policy 2 (Product Contamination and Recall, $5,000,000). This means that if a contamination event exceeds the $5M limit of the standalone contamination policy, the $15M umbrella does not provide additional coverage for that loss.

**Why it matters.** A major foodborne illness outbreak or large-scale recall in the inflight catering industry can easily exceed $5M in direct costs (recall logistics, customer claims, regulatory response, lost contracts). Without umbrella layering, the Company's maximum contamination coverage is $5M.

**Recommended fix.** This is primarily a **business decision** for the client in coordination with its insurance broker (Pembury & Vane). At the July 1, 2026 renewal (which will be the first renewal Trouseind controls), the client should consider: (a) negotiating with the umbrella carrier to add Policy 2 to the following-form schedule, or (b) increasing the standalone contamination limit, or (c) purchasing a separate excess contamination policy. The broker's letter (Section 3.2) indicates renewal marketing will begin in the ordinary course; Trouseind should direct the broker to address this gap at renewal.

### 7. TSA § 3.2: Extension Limited to Three Months, One Occasion

**Where:** Transition Services Agreement § 3.2 (Extension).

**The problem.** Recipient may extend any Service Period by up to three months, on one occasion only, with 30 days' prior notice. If the Company's own flight kitchens are not ready to assume production within the initial Service Period plus a three-month extension, Trouseind would have no contractual right to continue the services.

**Why it matters.** The Company operates flight kitchens at three airports (SPA Recital B). Standing up or expanding in-house production capacity at multiple sites can take longer than expected, particularly if capital expenditures, permit modifications, or airline customer re-approvals are required. If the TSA expires before the Company is self-sufficient, there is a gap in service to airline customers.

**Recommended fix.** This is a **business decision**. The client should assess its internal timeline for achieving production self-sufficiency at each facility. If the timeline is uncertain, negotiate for two extension rights (each up to three months) or a single extension of up to six months. Alternatively, build a longer initial Service Period into Schedule A.

### 8. Split Governing Law: SPA (New York) vs. Escrow Agreement (Delaware)

**Where:** SPA § 11.8 (New York); Escrow Agreement § 10.2 (Delaware); SPA § 11.10 (Order of Precedence).

**The problem.** The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The Escrow Agreement is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery. SPA § 11.10 provides that the SPA prevails over other Transaction Documents "except that the Escrow Agreement shall govern the disbursement of the Escrow Funds."

**Why it matters.** A dispute over whether a claim is properly characterized as a "Fundamental Representation Claim" (see Issue 1) would likely be litigated in Delaware under Delaware law, while a dispute over the underlying representation would be in New York under New York law. This creates the possibility of inconsistent outcomes and increased litigation cost. In practice, the Escrow Agent's interpleader power (Escrow Agreement § 6.5) could compound the issue.

**Recommended fix.** This is a lower-priority drafting issue, but if the parties are amenable, consider conforming the Escrow Agreement's governing law to New York to match the SPA, or at minimum adding a provision that the Escrow Agreement's definitions of claims (e.g., "Fundamental Representation Claims") are to be construed consistently with the Purchase Agreement. If the Escrow Agent (Lanterwick, a national banking association) requires Delaware law for its own comfort, this may be non-negotiable—in which case, the definitional fix in Issue 1 becomes even more important to avoid jurisdictional ambiguity.

## Tier 4 – Lower-Priority / Informational Items

### 9. Insurance Policy Renewal Timing

**Where:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, Section 3.2.

**The problem.** All of the Company's current policies expire on July 1, 2026—approximately three months after the anticipated March 31, 2026 closing. The broker states it will "start renewal marketing for the July 1, 2026 renewal in the ordinary course" and will "coordinate with the Company, and after closing with the buyer, as directed."

**Why it matters.** Trouseind will need to be in a position to direct the renewal process on short notice. If there are coverage gaps (see Issue 6) or if the client wants to restructure the program (e.g., consolidate carriers, add cyber coverage for the IT Support Services under the TSA), the three-month window is tight.

**Recommended fix.** Not a drafting issue. Recommend that the client engage its own insurance broker or direct Pembury & Vane to begin a renewal planning process no later than 45 days post-closing, with a target of having binders in place before July 1, 2026.

### 10. TSA § 12.7: Company Is Not a Third-Party Beneficiary

**Where:** Transition Services Agreement § 12.7.

**The problem.** The TSA provides that "The Company may receive Services but is not a third-party beneficiary." This means the Company (which Trouseind will own) cannot directly enforce the TSA against the Provider. Only Trouseind, as Recipient, has standing to enforce the agreement.

**Why it matters.** In practice, this is not a significant issue because Trouseind will own the Company and can direct enforcement. However, if there is ever a corporate separation (e.g., Trouseind spins off the Company or sells a division), the Company would lose its ability to enforce the TSA. Additionally, if the Company is the entity that actually suffers a loss (e.g., an airline customer sues the Company directly), the Company would need Trouseind to bring the TSA claim on its behalf.

**Recommended fix.** Low priority. Acceptable as drafted given that Trouseind will own 100% of the Company. No change recommended unless the client anticipates a near-term reorganization of the Company.
