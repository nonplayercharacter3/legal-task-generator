## Issue 1 – Pellucid Change-of-Control Consent Not Obtained (or Not Documented)

**Where:** SPA Schedule 4.9(a)(iii)(1) (disclosing the Pellucid Co-Manufacturing and Supply Agreement and its change-of-control termination right); SPA Schedule 4.4 (listing "None" for required consents); SPA Section 7.2(c) (closing condition tied only to Schedule 4.4 consents); SPA Section 6.4 (post-closing consent covenant tied only to Schedule 4.4).

**Why it matters:** Schedule 4.9(a)(iii)(1) discloses that Pellucid Grocers, Inc. "accounted for approximately 38% of the Company's net sales for the year ended December 31, 2025" and that "Pellucid may terminate the agreement on 30 days' notice if the Company undergoes a change of control without Pellucid's prior written consent." The change of control occurred at Closing on July 1, 2026. Schedule 4.4, which is the exclusive list of third-party consents referenced by the closing condition (Section 7.2(c)) and the post-closing consent covenant (Section 6.4), states "None." This means either (a) Seller failed to identify the Pellucid consent as required, in which case Seller breached its Section 4.4 representation and the closing condition was satisfied only because the schedule was incomplete; or (b) Seller obtained Pellucid's consent but failed to list it on Schedule 4.4, in which case there is a documentation gap that could create disputes about the scope and terms of the consent.

In either case, as of the date of the Escrow Agreement (October 6, 2026), roughly three months have elapsed since Closing, and Pellucid retains the right to terminate on 30 days' notice. Loss of 38% of net sales would be existential for the acquired business and would likely constitute a Material Adverse Effect. If this is a breach of representation, Buyer's indemnification recovery is capped at the Indemnity Escrow Amount of $3,600,000 (SPA Section 9.4(b)) and is subject to the $363,750 deductible (SPA Section 9.4(a))—far less than the revenue at stake. Moreover, the six-month survival period for non-fundamental representations (SPA Section 9.1(a)(iii)) expires January 1, 2027, leaving a narrow window to assert a claim.

**Assumption:** I have assumed that no separate consent from Pellucid was obtained, because Schedule 4.4 states "None" and no consent document appears in the matter folder. If Seller in fact obtained Pellucid's written consent, this issue is mitigated, but the consent should be produced and reviewed immediately.

**Recommended fix:**
- Demand that Seller produce any written consent, waiver, or acknowledgment from Pellucid relating to the change of control within five Business Days.
- If no consent exists, immediately engage with Pellucid (through the Company, now that Laimul owns it) to obtain a retroactive consent or waiver, and document it.
- Reserve indemnification rights under SPA Section 9.2(a) for breach of Section 4.4, and deliver a Claim Notice under SPA Section 9.5 before the January 1, 2027 survival deadline.
- Assess whether the failure to list the Pellucid consent on Schedule 4.4 constitutes fraud (which would bypass the cap and deductible under SPA Section 9.4(d)), though this is a high bar.

## Issue 2 – Buyer's NWC Dispute Notice Appears to Be Four Days Late

**Where:** SPA Section 2.4(a) (Seller to deliver Closing Statement within 60 days post-Closing); SPA Section 2.4(b) (Buyer to deliver Notice of Disagreement "within 30 days after Seller's delivery of the Closing Statement"); SPA Section 2.4(c) (failure to deliver timely notice makes Closing Statement "final and binding"); SPA Section 10.3 (reference date excluded from calculation); Escrow Agreement Recital C (Closing Statement delivered August 25, 2026); Escrow Agreement Recital D (Buyer Dispute Notice delivered September 28, 2026).

**Why it matters:** The Closing Statement was delivered on August 25, 2026. Excluding the reference date per SPA Section 10.3, the 30-day period ends on September 24, 2026. The Buyer Dispute Notice was delivered on September 28, 2026—four days late. Under SPA Section 2.4(c), if Buyer does not deliver a timely Notice of Disagreement, "the Closing Statement and Seller's calculation of Closing Net Working Capital shall be final and binding on the parties." If Seller asserts the notice was untimely, Seller's NWC calculation stands, and Buyer's claimed $612,000 shortfall (Escrow Agreement Recital D) is lost. The $750,000 Adjustment Escrow Fund would be released to Seller.

The Escrow Agreement (Recital E) states that "no Party has accepted the Closing Statement" and that "Buyer reserves all of its rights." This language suggests Seller is treating the dispute as live, but it is not an express waiver of the timing requirement. Under SPA Section 11.10, a waiver must be in a "written instrument signed by Buyer and Seller." The Escrow Agreement is signed by both parties and references the Buyer Dispute Notice, which could support an argument of implied waiver or estoppel, but this is not airtight.

**Assumption:** I have assumed the Closing Statement was physically delivered (not merely mailed) on August 25, 2026, as stated in Escrow Agreement Recital C. If delivery occurred later (e.g., by mail with a later receipt date), the timing analysis changes. The record should be verified.

**Recommended fix:**
- Obtain written confirmation from Seller (or its counsel) that it does not intend to assert the Notice of Disagreement was untimely, ideally as a side letter or an amendment to the Escrow Agreement that expressly states the parties treat the Buyer Dispute Notice as timely.
- If Seller refuses, prepare a legal analysis of waiver/estoppel based on the Escrow Agreement's recitals and the parties' subsequent conduct (negotiation of the amended escrow, continued hold of the Adjustment Escrow Fund).
- In the interim, ensure that Buyer does not take any action that could be construed as accepting the Closing Statement (e.g., jointly instructing the Escrow Agent to release the Adjustment Escrow Fund to Seller).
- Monitor the 30-day good-faith negotiation period under SPA Section 2.4(d), which runs from delivery of the (allegedly late) Notice of Disagreement. If the parties are in negotiation, that conduct may further support estoppel.

## Issue 3 – Note's Narrow "Losses" Definition Controls Set-Off Against the Seller Note

**Where:** Note Section 1 (defining "Losses" as "out-of-pocket damages, liabilities, judgments, fines and penalties actually paid by Maker or the Company to a Person other than Maker, the Company or any of their Affiliates, excluding attorneys' fees, diminution in value, consequential damages and costs of any product withdrawal or recall"); SPA Section 1.1 (defining "Losses" broadly to include "reasonable attorneys' fees, diminution in value and product withdrawal or recall costs"); SPA Section 9.8 (granting Buyer the right to set off "any amount of Losses" against the Seller Note); SPA Section 11.9 (providing that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to its own payment, set-off and acceleration terms").

**Why it matters:** SPA Section 9.8 appears to give Buyer a broad set-off right against the $6,000,000 Note for any indemnification Losses. However, SPA Section 11.9 expressly subordinates the SPA to the Note "with respect to its own payment, set-off and acceleration terms." The Note's Section 4.2 (Set-Off) incorporates the Note's own definition of "Losses," which excludes attorneys' fees, diminution in value, consequential damages, and product recall costs. This means that for the most common and costly types of claims in a food manufacturing business—recall costs, diminution in value of the acquired business, and legal fees—Buyer cannot set off against the Note. Buyer's practical set-off right is limited to out-of-pocket amounts actually paid to third parties (e.g., a judgment paid to a plaintiff). This significantly narrows the set-off remedy that SPA Section 9.8 appears to provide on its face.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate an amendment to the Note (per Note Section 9.5, requiring signatures of both Maker and Holder) to conform the Note's "Losses" definition to the SPA's broader definition, at least for purposes of Section 4.2.
- If Seller resists, seek a compromise: include diminution in value and product recall costs in the Note's definition (these are the most likely to arise in a food business), while perhaps accepting the exclusion of attorneys' fees.
- In the interim, when evaluating any indemnification claim, model the recovery assuming only the Note's narrow definition applies for set-off purposes, and treat the broader SPA definition as available only for direct claims against Seller (subject to the cap and survival period).

## Issue 4 – Seller May Assign the Note Without Buyer's Consent

**Where:** Note Section 9.2 ("Holder may assign or transfer this Note, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Maker, on written notice to Maker identifying the assignee.").

**Why it matters:** Dekes can sell, pledge, or otherwise transfer the $6,000,000 Note to any third party—potentially a competitor in the commercial baking space, a distressed-debt fund, or any other Person—without Laimul's approval. The assignee succeeds to "all rights of Holder under this Note" (Note Section 9.2), including the extensive information covenants in Note Section 6.1 (monthly financials, customer-level sales reports with pricing by customer, audited annuals, and access to books, records and personnel). If the Note is assigned to a competitor or an aggressive creditor, Laimul's most sensitive commercial data—including customer pricing—would be disclosed to that party. Additionally, an unfamiliar holder may be more likely to assert technical defaults or demand aggressive enforcement.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate an amendment to Note Section 9.2 to require Maker's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment, or at minimum to prohibit assignment to any Person that is a competitor of Maker or the Company in the commercial baking industry.
- If Seller resists a consent requirement, seek a "permitted assignee" carve-out limited to Seller's Affiliates and existing lenders, with all other assignments requiring consent.
- As a fallback, require that any assignee execute a confidentiality undertaking consistent with the NDA before the assignment is effective.

## Issue 5 – Overly Broad Information Covenants and Access Rights in the Note

**Where:** Note Section 6.1(b) (monthly "customer-level sales reports for the Company... showing net sales, unit volumes and average net pricing by customer and by product category"); Note Section 6.1(d) (access "to the books, records and personnel of Maker and the Company" on five Business Days' notice); Note Section 6.1(a) (monthly unaudited consolidated financials of Maker and all subsidiaries).

**Why it matters:** These covenants go well beyond what is typical for a seller note and create ongoing operational and competitive risks:

- **Customer-level pricing data (Section 6.1(b)):** Dekes is a commercial baking company. Providing it monthly customer-level net sales, unit volumes, and average net pricing by customer gives Dekes a detailed real-time picture of the Company's revenue base and pricing strategy. Even though Dekes is subject to a five-year non-compete (SPA Section 6.2), that covenant expires in 2031, and the Note matures in 2030. More importantly, if the non-compete is ever challenged or found unenforceable (see Issue 6 below), this data would be immediately usable by a competitor. Even within the non-compete period, the data could inform Dekes' other business activities or be used in a dispute.

- **Access to Maker's (not just the Company's) books (Section 6.1(d)):** The access right extends to "Maker and the Company"—i.e., Laimul Baking Holdings, Inc. and all of its other subsidiaries and operations, not just Hearthline. This is extraordinarily broad for a seller note. A seller's legitimate interest is in the creditworthiness of the maker and the value of the underlying business, but a right to inspect and copy all of Laimul's books and records on five days' notice is more akin to a lender's information covenant than a seller's protective right.

- **Monthly consolidated financials of all subsidiaries (Section 6.1(a)):** Similarly, requiring Laimul to deliver consolidated financials of itself and all subsidiaries (not just the Company) monthly gives Dekes visibility into Laimul's entire corporate family.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend Note Section 6.1(b) to limit customer-level reporting to the top 10 customers (by revenue) rather than all customers, and to exclude unit pricing (reporting only net sales and volumes). Alternatively, aggregate pricing data by product category only.
- Amend Note Section 6.1(d) to limit access to the books, records, and personnel of the Company (Hearthline) only, not Maker's other subsidiaries. If access to Maker-level records is retained for creditworthiness purposes, limit it to annual audited financials and material contract files.
- Amend Note Section 6.1(a) to require monthly financials of the Company only, and annual (not monthly) consolidated financials of Maker and its subsidiaries.
- Add a confidentiality provision to the Note requiring Holder (and any assignee) to treat all information received under Section 6.1 as confidential, subject to the NDA.

## Issue 6 – Five-Year Non-Compete May Be Unenforceable, Removing Key Protection

**Where:** SPA Section 6.2 (five-year non-compete and non-solicitation in favor of Buyer, restricting Seller from "engage[ing] in the manufacture or sale of commercial wholesale bread, bun or par-baked roll products in the United States").

**Why it matters:** This covenant is Laimul's primary protection against Dekes re-entering the commercial baking space after selling the Company. However, five years is at the outer limit of what Delaware courts will enforce, and the geographic scope (all of the United States) and the breadth of the restricted activity ("manufacture or sale of commercial wholesale bread, bun or par-baked roll products") invite a challenge on public-policy grounds. If the non-compete is found unenforceable or is blue-penciled to a shorter duration or narrower scope, Dekes could re-enter the market with the customer-level pricing data it receives under Note Section 6.1(b) (see Issue 5), creating a direct competitive threat.

This issue is interrelated with Issue 4 (unrestricted assignment) and Issue 5 (broad information rights): the combination of a potentially unenforceable non-compete, a freely assignable note, and detailed customer pricing data creates a scenario in which Laimul's most sensitive commercial information could end up in the hands of a competitor.

**Recommended fix:**
- The non-compete is already in place and cannot be unilaterally strengthened. However, Laimul should: (a) ensure that the information covenants in the Note are narrowed (per Issue 5) to reduce the competitive harm if the non-compete fails; (b) consider adding a standalone confidentiality agreement between Laimul and Dekes (separate from the NDA) that specifically covers business information received under the Note and survives for the duration of the Note plus a tail period; and (c) in any future negotiation with Seller (e.g., to amend the Note), seek a shorter non-compete with a tighter geographic scope in exchange for stronger information protections.

## Issue 7 – Cross-Default with Senior Debt Creates Cascade Risk

**Where:** Note Section 7.1(e) (Event of Default if "any Senior Lender accelerates the maturity of Senior Debt in an aggregate principal amount exceeding $5,000,000"); Note Section 7.2(b) (Holder may accelerate the Note upon any Event of Default other than insolvency); Note Section 5.3 (payment blockage while Senior Debt default is continuing).

**Why it matters:** If Laimul's senior bank financing (defined broadly as "all obligations of Maker and its subsidiaries under any credit agreement, loan agreement or similar instrument for borrowed money with one or more banks or other institutional lenders") is accelerated in an amount exceeding $5,000,000, the Note is in Event of Default. Seller may then accelerate the full $6,000,000 Note balance (Note Section 7.2(b)), making it immediately due. This creates a liquidity cascade: a problem with senior debt triggers a $6,000,000 accelerated obligation to Seller on top of the senior debt itself. The $5,000,000 threshold is low relative to what could be a substantial senior facility.

Conversely, Note Section 5.3 provides that while a Senior Debt default is continuing, Maker is blocked from making payments on the Note (other than by set-off). This is protective in the short term but means that the set-off right (already narrowed per Issue 3) is the only available mechanism during a blockage.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate an increase in the cross-default threshold from $5,000,000 to a higher amount (e.g., $10,000,000 or a percentage of total Senior Debt), or add a cure period (e.g., the Senior Debt default must continue for 30 days after notice before it constitutes an Event of Default under the Note).
- Ensure that the definition of "Senior Debt" in Note Section 1 is not so broad as to capture trivial or intra-financing obligations. Currently it covers "all obligations... for borrowed money with one or more banks or other institutional lenders," which is standard but could be narrowed to exclude obligations below a de minimis threshold.
- Model the liquidity impact of a simultaneous senior debt acceleration and Note acceleration to understand the worst-case cash requirement.

## Issue 8 – Event of Default for Sale of the Company Limits Future Strategic Flexibility

**Where:** Note Section 7.1(f) (Event of Default if "Maker sells or otherwise transfers all or substantially all of the capital stock or assets of the Company to any Person other than an Affiliate of Maker").

**Why it matters:** If Laimul ever wishes to sell, merge, or restructure Hearthline (the Company) to a third party—whether as part of a portfolio rationalization, a sale to a strategic buyer, or a drop-down to a joint venture—the Note is in Event of Default and Seller may accelerate the full balance. The only carve-out is for transfers to an "Affiliate of Maker." This is a significant constraint on Laimul's ability to deploy the acquired asset strategically over the four-year Note term (maturing July 1, 2030).

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate an amendment to add carve-outs for (a) sales to a strategic buyer where the purchaser assumes the Note or pays it off at closing, (b) mergers or consolidations of the Company into Maker or an Affiliate, and (c) transactions that do not reduce the net tangible assets available to service the Note below a specified threshold.
- At minimum, add a cure period: the transfer must not be accompanied by a payment default under the Note, and Maker must prepay the Note (or provide an irrevocable letter of credit) within 30 days after the transfer.

## Issue 9 – Six-Month Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations Is Short

**Where:** SPA Section 9.1(a)(iii) (all representations other than Fundamental Reps and Tax reps "shall survive until the date that is six months after the Closing Date"); SPA Section 9.1(c) (no claim may be brought after the survival period expires; claims not commenced are "forever barred").

**Why it matters:** Closing occurred July 1, 2026. The six-month survival period expires January 1, 2027. This means Laimul has until January 1, 2027 to commence an Action in the Delaware Court of Chancery for any breach of the following representations: financial statements (Section 4.5), absence of changes (Section 4.6), title to assets (Section 4.7), real property (Section 4.8), material contracts (Section 4.9), customers and suppliers (Section 4.10), litigation (Section 4.11), compliance with laws (Section 4.12), employees and benefit plans (Section 4.13), environmental (Section 4.14), food safety (Section 4.15), intellectual property (Section 4.16), insurance (Section 4.18), and related party transactions (Section 4.19).

For a food manufacturing business, certain risks (e.g., latent food safety issues, environmental contamination, contract disputes) may not manifest within six months. The clock is already running, and as of the date of this memo, approximately four months remain.

**Recommended fix:**
- Immediately conduct a comprehensive post-closing audit of all non-fundamental representations, prioritizing: food safety (Section 4.15), material contracts (Section 4.9), financial statements (Section 4.5), and compliance with laws (Section 4.12).
- If any potential breach is identified, deliver a Claim Notice under SPA Section 9.5 and commence an Action in the Court of Chancery before January 1, 2027. The SPA requires that an Action be "commenced" (not merely that a Claim Notice be given) before the survival period expires (SPA Section 9.1(c)).
- Going forward, in any renegotiation with Seller, seek to extend the survival period for food safety, environmental, and material contract representations to 18 months or the applicable statute of limitations.

## Issue 10 – Deductible Is a True Deductible, Not a Tipping Basket

**Where:** SPA Section 9.4(a) ("Seller shall not be liable... until the aggregate amount of all Losses... exceeds $363,750... after which Seller shall be liable only for Losses in excess of the Deductible").

**Why it matters:** The $363,750 deductible (10% of the Indemnity Escrow Amount) operates as a true deductible: even after the threshold is crossed, Seller is liable only for Losses "in excess of" the deductible. This means the first $363,750 of aggregate Losses is always borne by Buyer. In contrast, a "tipping basket" (or "threshold") would make Seller liable for all Losses from dollar one once the aggregate exceeds the threshold. The true deductible structure reduces Buyer's recovery by $363,750 on any claim that crosses the threshold. Combined with the $3,600,000 cap (SPA Section 9.4(b)), Buyer's maximum net recovery for non-fundamental rep breaches is $3,236,250.

**Recommended fix:**
- This is a negotiated economic term and difficult to change post-closing. However, in any amendment or side agreement with Seller (e.g., to address the Pellucid issue or the NWC dispute), seek to convert the deductible to a tipping basket or reduce the deductible amount.
- In the interim, when evaluating whether to pursue an indemnification claim, model the net recovery after the deductible to ensure the claim is economically worthwhile.

## Issue 11 – Escrow Release Demand Mechanism Creates Risk of Premature Disbursement

**Where:** Escrow Agreement Section 3.2(b) (either Party may deliver a "Release Demand" for the Adjustment Escrow Fund; if the other Party does not deliver an "Objection Notice" within ten Business Days, the Escrow Agent disburses the demanded amount to the demanding Party).

**Why it matters:** If Seller delivers a Release Demand asserting that Closing Net Working Capital has been "finally determined" (potentially relying on the argument that Buyer's Notice of Disagreement was late, per Issue 2), and Laimul fails to deliver an Objection Notice within ten Business Days, the $750,000 Adjustment Escrow Fund (or the portion demanded) will be disbursed to Seller automatically. The Escrow Agent has no obligation to independently verify the certification in the Release Demand (Escrow Agreement Section 4.1: duties are "purely ministerial"). This creates a "use it or lose it" dynamic where Laimul must actively monitor for any Release Demand and respond within a tight deadline.

**Recommended fix:**
- Designate a specific individual at Laimul (e.g., the General Counsel or CFO, both of whom are Authorized Representatives under Escrow Agreement Schedule 1) to monitor for any communication from the Escrow Agent regarding the Adjustment Escrow Fund.
- If a Release Demand is received, deliver an Objection Notice well within the ten-Business-Day window, even if the objection is procedural.
- In parallel, pursue the resolution of the NWC dispute through the Independent Accountant process (SPA Section 2.4(d)) to obtain a Final Order, which would override any Release Demand.

## Issue 12 – Buyer Is Taxed on Escrow Interest

**Where:** Escrow Agreement Section 2.5 ("For tax reporting purposes, Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds, and all interest and earnings on them shall be reported as income of Buyer, until disbursed.").

**Why it matters:** Laimul will receive a 1099-INT (or similar) from the Escrow Agent for all interest earned on both the Indemnity Escrow Fund ($3,600,000) and the Adjustment Escrow Fund ($750,000) for as long as the funds remain in escrow. Given the money market fund investment (Escrow Agreement Section 2.4), the interest will be modest but non-zero. Laimul must include this in its taxable income even though the principal is held for the benefit of both parties and may ultimately be disbursed to Seller. If the Indemnity Escrow Fund is held until January 2027 (the end of the survival period) or longer (if claims are pending), Laimul bears the tax on interest for that entire period.

**Recommended fix:**
- Ensure Laimul's tax department is aware of the escrow interest income and can properly account for it.
- In any negotiation with Seller regarding the release of the Indemnity Escrow Fund, consider whether Seller should reimburse Laimul for the tax on interest attributable to the period after the survival deadline if the funds are held longer due to Seller's pending claims or disputes.
- Confirm that the W-9 delivered by Buyer (required by Escrow Agreement Section 2.5) is properly completed to avoid backup withholding.

## Issue 13 – NDA Employee Non-Solicitation Remains in Effect Post-Closing

**Where:** NDA Section 7.1 (12-month non-solicitation of Hearthline officers, plant managers, and director-level employees, running from the Effective Date of March 16, 2026, i.e., until March 16, 2027).

**Why it matters:** Post-closing, Laimul owns Hearthline. If Laimul wishes to reorganize its management structure—for example, by transferring Hearthline's plant manager or a director-level employee to a Laimul parent-level role, or by consolidating functions across its corporate family—this could be characterized as "solicit[ing] for employment or hir[ing]" a Hearthline employee within the meaning of NDA Section 7.1. The restriction applies to employees "with whom Laimul first had contact, or of whom Laimul first became aware, in connection with the Transaction." During the diligence process, Laimul likely had contact with or became aware of most of Hearthline's senior personnel.

The exceptions for (a) general solicitations and (b) employees whose employment ended six months prior provide some relief, but a targeted internal transfer or promotion is unlikely to fall within either exception.

**Recommended fix:**
- Before making any management changes involving Hearthline's officers, plant managers, or director-level employees, obtain written confirmation from Dekes (or its counsel) that the NDA non-solicitation does not apply to internal reorganizations within the Laimul corporate family post-closing.
- If Dekes objects, negotiate a release of NDA Section 7.1 as to the Company's employees, on the basis that Laimul now owns the Company and the restriction is no longer serving its original purpose (preventing a prospective buyer from poaching the target's team).
- Note that the restriction expires on March 16, 2027 in any event, so the practical impact is limited to the next ~7 months.

## Issue 14 – NDA Customer/Supplier Contact Restriction Creates Post-Closing Ambiguity

**Where:** NDA Section 7.2 ("Until the earlier of the termination of discussions regarding the Transaction and 12 months after the Effective Date, Laimul shall not... initiate contact with any customer, supplier or landlord of Hearthline concerning the Transaction or Hearthline's business without the prior written consent of Dekes." Exception: "contacts made in the ordinary course of Laimul's business unrelated to the Transaction.").

**Why it matters:** The Transaction was consummated, not terminated. The restriction therefore runs until March 16, 2027 (12 months from the Effective Date). Post-closing, Laimul is the owner of Hearthline, and its day-to-day interactions with Hearthline's customers, suppliers, and landlords (e.g., negotiating price renewals, resolving delivery issues, discussing lease modifications) are "concerning... Hearthline's business." Whether these contacts are "in the ordinary course of Laimul's business unrelated to the Transaction" is ambiguous: one could argue that managing the acquired business IS the ordinary course of Laimul's business post-closing, and the contacts are unrelated to the (now-completed) Transaction. However, a strict reading could require Laimul to obtain Dekes' consent for routine business contacts with Hearthline's counterparties for another ~7 months.

**Recommended fix:**
- Send a brief letter to Dekes confirming that, now that the Transaction has been consummated, Laimul's post-closing operational contacts with Hearthline's customers, suppliers, and landlords fall within the "ordinary course" exception to NDA Section 7.2, and request written acknowledgment.
- If Dekes does not acknowledge, treat the restriction as expired by operation of the consummation of the Transaction (arguing that "termination of discussions" encompasses successful completion), but document the analysis.

## Issue 15 – NDA Confidentiality Obligations Persist and Create Tension with Ownership

**Where:** NDA Section 8.1 (NDA remains in effect for two years from March 16, 2026, i.e., until March 16, 2028; trade secret obligations continue indefinitely); NDA Section 1.1 (defining "Confidential Information" to include information "disclosed or made available by or on behalf of a Disclosing Party to a Receiving Party"); NDA Section 3.1 (use restriction: Confidential Information may be used "solely for the purpose of evaluating, negotiating, financing and, if applicable, consummating the Transaction, and for no other purpose").

**Why it matters:** Post-closing, Laimul owns Hearthline and has full access to all of the Company's information as its parent. Much of this information was disclosed by Dekes (as Seller) to Laimul (as Buyer) during the deal process and is therefore "Confidential Information of Dekes" under the NDA. NDA Section 3.1 restricts use of Confidential Information to "evaluating, negotiating, financing and, if applicable, consummating the Transaction, and for no other purpose." Read literally, this would prohibit Laimul from using the Company's financial data, customer lists, recipes, and operational information for its post-closing ownership and management of the business—which is absurd, but the language does not expressly address the post-closing scenario.

The NDA has no provision stating that upon consummation of the Transaction, the Confidential Information restrictions cease to apply to the Receiving Party in its capacity as owner of the business. This creates a technical (if commercially unreasonable) basis for Dekes to assert that Laimul is using its Confidential Information for an impermissible purpose.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate a short side letter or amendment to the NDA (per NDA Section 10.5, requiring both parties' signatures) confirming that, as of the Closing Date, Laimul's use of Hearthline's information in its capacity as the Company's parent is permitted and does not constitute a breach of NDA Section 3.1.
- Alternatively, obtain Dekes' written acknowledgment that the NDA's use restriction in Section 3.1 is satisfied by the consummation of the Transaction and that Laimul may freely use the Company's information for all purposes related to its ownership.
- Ensure that the Company's trade secrets (recipes, formulations) are properly identified and documented to support the indefinite trade secret protection under NDA Section 8.1, which benefits Laimul as the current owner.
