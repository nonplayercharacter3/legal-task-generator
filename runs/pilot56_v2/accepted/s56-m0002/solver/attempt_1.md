## 1. Hollis Ridge Consent Missing from Schedule 4.4

**Where:** Seller Disclosure Schedule § 6 (Schedule 4.4) lists only two required consents: Dunmore and Ashgrove (landlord). But Seller Disclosure Schedule § 8, item 1 (Hollis Ridge Contract) states: *"the Contractor may not assign, transfer or sublet the contract or any part of it, and no change in control of the Contractor may occur, without the prior written approval of the District's Board of Education."*

**Why it matters:** The Hollis Ridge Contract is the largest district contract (~$3.1M/year; APA § 4.8, Disclosure Schedule § 8.1). APA § 4.4 represents that Schedule 4.4 sets forth *each* Consent required for the assignment of the Assigned Contracts. The Hollis Ridge consent is conspicuously absent. APA § 7.2(c) makes obtaining each Schedule 4.4 consent a condition to Buyer's closing obligation. If the Hollis Ridge consent is not on the schedule, it is not a closing condition, and Seller could close without it—leaving Buyer with an assigned contract that is technically in breach of its anti-assignment clause from day one. The District could refuse to recognize the assignment or treat it as a default.

**Recommendation:** Add the Hollis Ridge Board of Education consent to Schedule 4.4 before signing. Confirm with Seller whether the consent has already been requested and what the District's typical turnaround is. If the District's process may not complete before the target closing date, consider making the Hollis Ridge assignment effective only upon delivery of the consent (with a long-stop date), or adding a specific closing condition.

## 2. Transition Period May Be Too Short; No Extension Mechanism

**Where:** TSA § 2.1 sets the Transition Period at 150 days from Closing. TSA § 2.2 provides for automatic expiration at 11:59 p.m. on the last day. There is no extension provision.

**Why it matters:** During the Transition Period, Seller provides all 224 drivers, 41 monitors, and 6 dispatchers, plus operating authority under Seller's USDOT number (TSA §§ 3.1–3.2). After expiration, Buyer must operate the 212 buses under its own authority. If Buyer's application for its own USDOT number, operating authority, and NYSDOT motor carrier registrations is not approved within 150 days, the TSA expires and Buyer has no lawful means to operate the buses. The buses would sit idle while Buyer continues to pay on the acquisition loan and the Seller Note (first installment due at day 120). There is no remedy short of litigation to compel Seller to extend.

**Recommendation:** Add an automatic extension provision: if Buyer has filed a complete application for operating authority before the 150th day and the authority has not yet been issued, the Transition Period extends until 30 days after issuance (or a hard outside date, e.g., 270 days). Alternatively, extend the base period to 180 days and add a one-time 60-day extension at Buyer's election if the application is pending.

## 3. TSA Permits Seller to Subcontract Drivers Without Buyer Consent

**Where:** TSA § 12.3: *"Provider may assign, delegate or subcontract this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient."*

**Why it matters:** This means Seller could replace its drivers with subcontracted personnel—potentially from a different company, with different qualifications, training, or familiarity with the routes—without Buyer's approval. For a pupil transportation operation carrying schoolchildren, driver identity, qualification, and route familiarity are safety-critical. The District Contracts require compliance with Article 19-A of the NY Vehicle and Traffic Law and 8 NYCRR § 156.3 (TSA § 3.5). A subcontractor's drivers may not meet those standards, and a safety incident involving an unknown subcontractor driver could expose Buyer to district claims, regulatory action, and reputational harm. The provision also undermines the Driver Retention Side Letter, which is premised on Buyer ultimately employing the *same* drivers.

**Recommendation:** Delete or narrow § 12.3. At minimum, require Buyer's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any delegation or subcontracting of driver or monitor services, and require that any substitute personnel meet the same CDL, endorsement, Article 19-A, and drug-testing standards as the original Service Personnel.

## 4. Non-Fundamental Representations Survive Only ~120 Days

**Where:** APA § 1.1 defines "Survival Date" as "the first Scheduled Payment Date under the Seller Note." Seller Note § 2.2 sets the first Scheduled Payment Date at 120 days after the Issue Date (Closing). APA § 9.1 provides that all non-Fundamental reps and warranties survive only until the Survival Date.

**Why it matters:** The Fundamental Representations (Organization, Authority, Title, Taxes, Brokers) survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations. But every other Seller rep—vehicle condition (§ 4.6), contract status (§ 4.8), billing accuracy (§ 4.9), legal compliance (§ 4.10), permits (§ 4.11), litigation (§ 4.12), employees (§ 4.13), environmental (§ 4.15), and IP (§ 4.16)—dies 120 days after Closing. For a fleet of 212 buses, latent defects (e.g., a frame crack, a brake system issue, a missed recall) may not surface for months. District contract issues (e.g., a billing error discovered in a year-end audit) may not be identified within 120 days. After the Survival Date, Buyer's sole remedy for a rep breach is extinguished (APA § 9.8 makes indemnification the exclusive remedy).

**Recommendation:** Extend the Survival Date for non-Fundamental reps to at least 18 months (or the end of the first full school year after Closing, whichever is later). If Seller resists, carve out specific reps that warrant longer survival: § 4.6 (Vehicles), § 4.9 (District Billing), and § 4.10 (Compliance with Laws) should survive for at least 18 months given the cyclical nature of school bus inspections and district billing audits.

## 5. Indemnification Cap Is Low (12% of Purchase Price)

**Where:** APA § 9.5 caps Seller's aggregate indemnification liability under § 9.2(a) (rep breaches) at 12% of the Purchase Price = **$2,208,000**. Fundamental Rep breaches are capped at the full Purchase Price ($18.4M); fraud is uncapped.

**Why it matters:** The $2.2M cap is the ceiling for all non-Fundamental rep breaches combined. A single significant issue—e.g., 30 buses with undisclosed structural defects requiring $80K each in repairs ($2.4M), or a district billing restatement exceeding $2M—would exhaust the cap. Given that the cap is shared across *all* non-Fundamental reps (vehicles, contracts, compliance, IP, environmental, etc.), Buyer could be left without recovery for the second major issue it discovers. The 12% cap is at the low end of market for a deal of this size and complexity.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate the cap up to 15–20% of the Purchase Price ($2.76M–$3.68M). If Seller holds firm at 12%, consider (a) raising the deductible threshold proportionally lower (currently $184K = 1%), or (b) carving out specific high-risk reps (e.g., § 4.6 Vehicles) from the cap entirely, subject to a separate sub-cap.

## 6. Mechanics and Depot Staff Not Covered by Side Letter; Maintenance Gap During Transition

**Where:** Driver Retention Side Letter § 1.2 and Annex A define Covered Employees as 224 drivers, 41 monitors/attendants, and 6 dispatchers/routers (271 total). The 14 mechanics/shop personnel and 5 depot manager/office staff listed in Disclosure Schedule § 10.1 are *not* Covered Employees. TSA § 3.7 expressly excludes vehicle maintenance, repair, inspection, fueling, and cleaning from the Services, stating that *"Recipient shall perform or procure at its own cost using the Depot facilities and the shop personnel it engages."*

**Why it matters:** From Closing, Buyer is responsible for keeping 212 buses in compliance with NYSDOT semi-annual inspection requirements (APA § 4.6; 15 NYCRR Part 721). Without the 14 mechanics (who know the fleet, the depot layout, and the inspection process), Buyer must either hire replacements quickly or contract for third-party maintenance. If the incumbent mechanics are not offered employment, they may leave immediately after Closing (they are not bound by any non-solicitation in favor of Buyer—Side Letter § 4.4 only restricts Seller from soliciting *Transferred Employees*). A loss of all 14 mechanics on day one of the Transition Period would create an immediate operational crisis.

**Recommendation:** (a) Add the 14 mechanics and 5 depot/office staff to Annex A as Covered Employees, or (b) if Seller resists including them in the Side Letter, add a specific covenant in the TSA requiring Seller to make the mechanics available for a brief overlap period (e.g., 30 days post-Closing) at cost, and (c) require Seller to deliver a maintenance manual and fleet-specific service history for each bus unit at Closing (beyond what APA § 2.1(a) already covers with "maintenance logs").

## 7. UCC Lien on 42 Buses: Payoff Not an Express Closing Condition

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule § 7 (Schedule 4.5): *"UCC financing statement No. 2021-[●] filed by Keystone Commercial Equipment Finance, LLC with the Pennsylvania Department of State covers Units 149 through 190. It is not a Permitted Encumbrance. The secured debt will be paid off, and a UCC-3 termination statement and lien releases delivered, at or before the Closing."*

**Why it matters:** APA § 2.1 requires delivery of the Purchased Assets "free and clear of all Encumbrances other than Permitted Encumbrances." The Keystone UCC is not a Permitted Encumbrance, so Seller must clear it. However, APA Article VII (Conditions to Closing) does not list delivery of the UCC-3 terminations and lien releases as a specific closing condition. The only related condition is § 7.2(f) (delivery of items in § 3.2), and § 3.2(g) requires "certificates of title for each Purchased Vehicle, duly endorsed." A duly endorsed title does not by itself evidence that the UCC lien has been released. If the UCC-3 is not filed before or at Closing, the 42 buses (Units 149–190, the 2021 Thomas Built fleet) would transfer with an active lien, violating § 2.1 and § 4.5.

**Recommendation:** Add to APA § 3.2 (Seller Closing Deliverables) or § 7.2 (Conditions to Buyer): "UCC-3 termination statements and lien releases from Keystone Commercial Equipment Finance, LLC with respect to Units 149 through 190, or evidence of payoff and release satisfactory to Buyer." Also confirm the payoff amount and that Seller has the funds available at Closing.

## 8. Seller Note Governed by Pennsylvania Law; PA Jurisdiction

**Where:** Seller Note § 10.1: governed by Pennsylvania law. § 10.2: exclusive jurisdiction in Allegheny County, PA. APA § 11.8: governed by New York law. APA § 11.9: exclusive jurisdiction in New York County, NY. APA § 11.12: "the Seller Note shall control with respect to the payment, enforcement and collection of amounts owing thereunder."

**Why it matters:** Any dispute about the Note—whether a set-off under APA § 9.7 was properly exercised, whether an event of default occurred, or the effect of a partial prepayment—would be litigated in Pennsylvania under Pennsylvania law, even though the underlying transaction is a New York asset purchase governed by New York law. This creates a risk of inconsistent outcomes: a NY court might interpret the APA's set-off provision one way, while a PA court interprets the Note's set-off provision (which incorporates the APA's) differently. It also imposes the cost and inconvenience of PA litigation on Buyer.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate to change the Note's governing law to New York and jurisdiction to New York County, consistent with the APA. If Seller insists on PA law (it is a PA corporation), at minimum add a provision that the Note is construed consistently with the APA and that any conflict between the Note and the APA regarding set-off rights is resolved in favor of the APA's terms.

## 9. Asymmetric Indemnification in TSA

**Where:** TSA § 8.1: Buyer indemnifies Seller for *all* Losses from the Services or vehicle operation during the Transition Period, except Seller's gross negligence or willful misconduct. TSA § 8.2: Seller indemnifies Buyer only for (a) Seller's gross negligence or willful misconduct, (b) employment claims by Service Personnel, and (c) failure to maintain operating authority.

**Why it matters:** The standard for Seller's indemnity is gross negligence/willful misconduct, while Buyer's indemnity to Seller is for *all* losses (only carved back for Seller's gross negligence/willful misconduct). In practice, this means: if a Seller driver causes an accident through ordinary negligence, the injured third party sues both the driver's employer (Seller) and the vehicle owner (Buyer). Seller would indemnify Buyer only if its own gross negligence was involved. But Buyer has already assumed Operational Liabilities under APA § 2.3(b), so the vehicle-operation indemnity in TSA § 8.1 is largely redundant with the APA assumption. The real exposure is for non-vehicle losses "arising out of or relating to the Services"—e.g., a dispatch error causing a district to claim breach of contract, or a compliance failure (missed drug test) triggering a regulatory penalty. In those scenarios, Buyer indemnifies Seller for Seller's ordinary negligence in performing the Services.

**Recommendation:** Narrow TSA § 8.1 to exclude Losses caused by Provider's negligence (not just gross negligence). Alternatively, add to § 8.2 a provision that Seller indemnifies Buyer for Losses arising from Seller's failure to perform the Services in accordance with § 3.6 (standard of performance) or § 3.5 (compliance programs), regardless of whether the failure rises to gross negligence.

## 10. Depot Lease Estoppel Not Expressly a Closing Deliverable

**Where:** APA § 3.2 lists Seller's closing deliverables. The estoppel certificate is not among them. APA § 7.2(c) requires delivery of consents on Schedule 4.4 "in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer." The landlord consent (Schedule 4.4, item 2) is contained in the estoppel (§ 9.1), but the estoppel also provides critical representations about lease status, rent, no defaults, SNDA, and permitted use.

**Why it matters:** If Seller delivers only a bare consent letter from Landlord rather than the full estoppel certificate, Buyer loses the benefit of Landlord's representations (no defaults, correct rent, SNDA in place, permitted use confirmed). The estoppel is also relied upon by Buyer's lender (Estoppel § 1.2), so the lender will require it.

**Recommendation:** Add to APA § 3.2: "the estoppel certificate from Ashgrove Depot Realty LLC in the form attached as Exhibit [●] to the Disclosure Schedule, dated within 30 days of the Closing Date." Make it a condition under § 7.2(c) or a separate condition.

## 11. IP Assignment: Vague Backup Retention Exception

**Where:** IP Assignment Agreement § 3.3(c): Seller may retain *"archival copies held in routine electronic back-up systems that are not readily accessible."*

**Why it matters:** "Not readily accessible" is undefined. Seller could argue that data in a cloud backup, a tape archive, or a disaster-recovery site is "not readily accessible" and thus exempt from the deletion obligation. Combined with § 5.1 (no use after Closing except for TSA Services), the practical risk is limited—but if Seller later re-enters the New York market (despite the 3-year non-solicitation in APA § 6.8), it could argue it retained the routing data in backup and used it to re-establish operations. The non-solicitation in § 6.8 covers school districts, not the use of routing data per se.

**Recommendation:** Define "not readily accessible" (e.g., "copies that cannot be restored to active use within 5 Business Days without IT intervention") and add a requirement that Seller certify in writing, at the end of the Transition Period, that all actively accessible copies of the Routing Materials and Business Documentation have been deleted or transferred.

## 12. Uniform Supply Agreement: Auto-Renewal Without Alert Mechanism

**Where:** Uniform Supply Agreement § 2.2: auto-renews for successive 1-year terms unless either Party gives 60 days' notice of non-renewal.

**Why it matters:** This is a lower-priority issue, but if Buyer's operations team does not calendar the renewal date, the agreement locks in for another year at potentially increased prices (up to 4% per § 5.1). Given that the agreement is new and the initial term is only 2 years, the first renewal date is approximately 2 years from signing.

**Recommendation:** Add an internal compliance item to calendar the non-renewal notice deadline (60 days before each term end). Alternatively, negotiate to delete the auto-renewal and require affirmative renewal by both parties.
