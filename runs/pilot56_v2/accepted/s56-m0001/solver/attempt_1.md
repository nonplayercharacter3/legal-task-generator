## Issue 1 – Change of Control Triggers Incurable Breach and Fee Acceleration

**Where:** Agreement §15.2; Agreement §12.5; Amendment No. 1, Recital B.

**The Problem:**

Section 15.2 provides that *any direct or indirect change of control of Licensee*—including at the parent level—constitutes a deemed assignment requiring Draibav's prior written consent. Without consent, it is "a material breach of this Agreement that is not capable of cure," and Draibav may terminate, in which case §12.5 accelerates all Subscription Fees for the remainder of the then-current term.

Amendment No. 1, Recital B, confirms that Logith Holdings, Inc. (Logith's parent) signed a merger agreement with Ostrander Remarketing Partners, L.P. on April 20, 2026, with closing expected in Q4 2026. The Ostrander transaction is a change of control of Logith's parent, squarely within §15.2's "indirect" language.

At $78,000/month, the remaining Initial Subscription Term (through June 30, 2030) represents approximately **$1.87 million** in unaccrued fees. If the Agreement has already rolled into a Renewal Term (or if the termination notice is held invalid and the term continues), exposure could be substantially higher—up to **$4.68 million** for a full 60-month period.

The term sheet (§10) left change-of-control treatment to be "addressed in the Definitive Agreement" without signaling the severity of the provision that ultimately appeared.

**Why It Matters:** If the Ostrander merger closes without Draibav's consent, Draibav can terminate and accelerate all remaining fees. Logith would owe the full balance despite having done nothing wrong operationally. The breach is "not capable of cure"—Logith cannot undo the merger to fix it.

**Recommended Fix:**

- **Immediately** notify Draibav of the Ostrander transaction (if not already done informally) and request written consent under §15.2 before the merger closes. Frame it as a routine request; Draibav has already acknowledged the transaction in the Amendment recitals, so they are on notice.
- Negotiate a consent letter that (a) confirms no fee acceleration, (b) confirms the Agreement continues on existing terms, and (c) ideally carves out the Ostrander transaction from the "not capable of cure" language.
- If Logith's termination under SLS §5.2 is ultimately held valid (see Issue 2), the change-of-control issue becomes moot for the terminated sites, but we should still obtain consent to avoid any colorable claim.
- **Deadline:** Consent must be secured before the Ostrander merger closes (expected Q4 2026).

## Issue 2 – Validity of Logith's Termination Notice (Chronic Failure)

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recitals C and §6; Service Level Schedule §§5.1–5.2; Agreement §12.4.

**The Problem:**

Logith delivered a termination notice on **May 4, 2026**, asserting a Chronic Failure based on Monthly Availability of 98.1% (January), 97.6% (February), and 98.4% (March 2026)—all below the 99.0% threshold in SLS §5.1. Draibav disputed the notice on May 11, 2026. Amendment No. 1 §6.1 expressly reserves all rights on whether the notice was "timely and effective."

The timing issue: SLS §5.2 requires notice "within thirty (30) days after the end of the third affected month." The third affected month is March 2026, which ended March 31. Thirty days after March 31 is **April 30, 2026**. Logith's notice was delivered **May 4**—four days late on a plain reading.

**Counter-argument for Logith:** The monthly availability report under SLS §2.4 is due "within ten (10) days after the end of each calendar month," i.e., by April 10 for March. One could argue Logith could not have known of the Chronic Failure until it received the March report, and the 30-day window should run from actual knowledge. However, the contract language ties the deadline to "the end of the third affected month," not to receipt of the report.

**Why It Matters:** If the termination is invalid, Logith remains locked into the Agreement (potentially for the full 60-month Initial Term or a 60-month Renewal Term—see Issue 4). If valid, the Agreement terminates October 31, 2026, with no early termination fee (§12.4; SLS §5.2). This determines whether Logith is a customer of Draibav for the next 1–5 years.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Prepare a position paper arguing the notice was timely, emphasizing: (a) the report-based knowledge argument; (b) Draibav's own delay in providing the March report (if applicable—confirm the actual delivery date); and (c) the equitable argument that Draibav's May 11 dispute letter, followed by its willingness to negotiate an Amendment, constitutes a waiver or estoppel against the four-day lateness.
- In parallel, negotiate a resolution as part of the consent request under Issue 1. A practical outcome: Draibav consents to the Ostrander change of control *and* acknowledges the termination as effective (or agrees to a mutual early termination on specified terms). This converts a litigated dispute into a commercial resolution.
- If negotiation fails, be prepared to litigate in Wilmington, Delaware (Agreement §14.3). The four-day gap is factually close enough that a reasonable jury or judge could find Logith timely, particularly if the March report was delivered near the outside of the 10-day window.
- **Preserve evidence:** Secure the exact dates Logith received each monthly availability report (January, February, March 2026) and any internal communications regarding the termination decision.

## Issue 3 – Exclusivity Clause Far Broader Than Term Sheet

**Where:** Agreement §3.4; Term Sheet §§4.1–4.2.

**The Problem:**

| Dimension | Term Sheet | Agreement §3.4 |
|---|---|---|
| Scope of restriction | Simulcast bidding software only | "any online, simulcast or mobile vehicle auction software, bidding software or condition reporting software" |
| Geographic limit | Six Licensed Sites only | "anywhere in the United States or Canada" |
| Entities bound | Logith only | Logith **and its Affiliates** |
| Internally developed software | Permitted (implied by §4.2) | Prohibited |

The term sheet expressly stated: "Nothing in this Section 4 restricts Logith or its affiliates in respect of any other software or any other facility" (§4.2). The final agreement eliminated that protection entirely.

**Why It Matters:** Logith operates six sites but could expand. Under §3.4, Logith cannot use *any* competing auction technology—whether at its existing sites, at future sites, or anywhere in the U.S. or Canada—nor can it develop its own. Its Affiliates are equally bound. This is an extraordinary restriction for a 60-month (or longer) term and goes well beyond what the parties discussed.

If the Ostrander merger brings new facilities or operations into the Logith family, those would also be restricted.

**Recommended Fix:**

- If the termination (Issue 2) is successful, this becomes moot.
- If the Agreement continues (whether because the termination fails or as part of a negotiated resolution), seek an amendment narrowing §3.4 to: (a) the six Licensed Sites (plus any sites added by supplemental Order Form); (b) simulcast bidding software only (matching the term sheet); and (c) deleting the Affiliate restriction or limiting it to Affiliates that operate vehicle auction facilities.
- At minimum, obtain a written side letter confirming that the Ostrander transaction and any post-merger integration activities do not violate §3.4.

## Issue 4 – Renewal Term Is 60 Months, Not 12

**Where:** Agreement §12.2; Order Form §2.2; Term Sheet §3.2.

**The Problem:**

The term sheet provided for "successive **12-month** renewal terms." The Agreement provides that each Renewal Term is "equal in length to the Initial Subscription Term"—which the Order Form sets at **60 months** (§2.2). If Logith misses the non-renewal notice window (see Issue 5), it is locked in for five additional years, not one.

**Why It Matters:** A 60-month renewal with a 4% annual fee cap (§6.5) means Logith could be committed through 2035 or beyond on a platform that has already demonstrated chronic availability failures. The commercial leverage shifts dramatically to Draibav.

**Recommended Fix:**

- If the Agreement continues, negotiate an amendment to §12.2 reducing the Renewal Term to 12 months (as per the term sheet) or adding a right to terminate for convenience at the end of any 12-month period within a Renewal Term.
- Calendar the non-renewal notice deadline immediately (see Issue 5).

## Issue 5 – Non-Renewal Notice Window Is Narrow and Early

**Where:** Agreement §12.2; Term Sheet §3.2.

**The Problem:**

The term sheet required non-renewal notice "at least ninety (90) days before the end of the then-current term"—a simple, long lead time. The Agreement requires notice "during the **thirty (30) day period ending 180 days before** the expiration." For the Initial Subscription Term ending June 30, 2030, the window is approximately **December 2, 2029 through January 1, 2030**. Miss that 30-day window and Logith is bound for another 60 months.

**Why It Matters:** The window is both earlier (180 days out vs. 90) and narrower (30 days vs. any time up to 90 days before). A single administrative oversight locks Logith in for five years.

**Recommended Fix:**

- **Calendar the deadline now** with multiple reminders (12 months out, 6 months out, 3 months out, and 30 days before the window opens).
- If the Agreement continues past the Initial Term, consider negotiating an amendment to restore the term sheet's 90-day notice standard.

## Issue 6 – Conflicting Definitions of "Licensee Data" Undermine Ownership and Enable Draibav Commercialization

**Where:** Order Form §4.1; Agreement §1.9; Agreement §2.3 (order of precedence); Agreement §§7.1, 7.4; Term Sheet §7.

**The Problem:**

The Agreement defines "Licensee Data" broadly (§1.9): all data "submitted to, collected by, entered into, stored in or **generated by** the Platform," including bid histories, hammer prices, sale results, condition reports, and derivatives.

The Order Form defines it narrowly (§4.1): only "data and information **uploaded to** the Platform **by Licensee's Authorized Users**."

Under the order of precedence (§2.3), the **Order Form controls**. The operative definition is therefore the narrow one.

Consequences:

1. **Ownership gap (§7.1):** Logith owns "all right, title and interest in and to the Licensee Data." Data *generated by* the Platform (e.g., bid timestamps, system-generated sale reports, analytics derivatives) is not "uploaded by Authorized Users" and may fall outside the definition. Logith may not own its own auction results data.

2. **Draibav commercialization (§7.4):** Draibav may "collect, use, aggregate, analyze and **commercialize**" data "other than Licensee Data," including "to develop and sell **analytics products, market reports and market pricing benchmarks to third parties**." Because Platform-generated data (auction results, pricing, volumes) likely falls outside the narrow Order Form definition, Draibav can sell Logith's auction pricing data to competitors.

The term sheet (§7.2) was more protective: "Draibav may use Logith's data only to provide the Platform and services to Logith. Draibav may use Platform operational and performance data that **does not identify Logith, its sites, bidders or consignors**."

**Why It Matters:** Logith's auction results, pricing data, and volume information are commercially sensitive. Competitors could purchase market pricing benchmarks from Draibav that are derived from Logith's actual transaction data. This is a direct competitive harm.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Amend Order Form §4.1 to conform to Agreement §1.9 (the broader definition), or add language expressly including Platform-generated data in the definition.
- Amend Agreement §7.4 to add the term sheet's restriction: Draibav may not use or commercialize any data that identifies Logith, its Licensed Sites, its Bidders, or its Consignors, and may not sell pricing benchmarks derived from Logith's transactions without Logith's written consent.
- If the termination is successful, ensure the data return/export under §7.3 captures all Platform-generated data, not just uploaded data.

## Issue 7 – "Best Efforts" Standard for Auction Hours Availability

**Where:** Service Level Schedule §2.2.

**The Problem:**

SLS §2.1 commits to 99.9% Monthly Availability (measured across all hours). But §2.2 provides only that Draibav "shall use its **best efforts** to ensure that no Downtime occurs during Auction Hours." "Best efforts" is the lowest standard of commercial diligence in contract law—short of "reasonable efforts" and well short of a firm commitment.

Auction Hours (6:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. local) are when Logith's revenue is generated. A complete outage during a live auction means lost sales, angry bidders, and reputational damage. The contract's primary financial remedy for Auction Hours downtime is an additional 5% service credit (§4.2)—on $78,000/month, that is $3,900, regardless of how many millions in vehicle sales were disrupted.

**Why It Matters:** The standard is mismatched to the business risk. Logith is running live auctions with real-time bidding; an outage during Auction Hours is an existential event for that sale, not a minor service degradation.

**Recommended Fix:**

- If the Agreement continues, negotiate to replace "best efforts" in §2.2 with a firm commitment (e.g., "Licensor shall use commercially reasonable efforts" or a specific Auction Hours availability target of 99.95%).
- Increase the §4.2 service credit for Auction Hours Severity 1 incidents from 5% to a figure that more meaningfully compensates for lost auction revenue (e.g., 15–20%).
- At minimum, ensure the termination right in §5.3 (three Severity 1 incidents >2 hours during Auction Hours in 90 days) is clearly communicated to Draibav as a live threat.

## Issue 8 – Service Credit Cap Limits Remedy to 50% of Monthly Fees

**Where:** Service Level Schedule §4.3.

**The Problem:**

"Service Credits for any month may not exceed in total 50% of the Subscription Fees for that month." Even if the Platform is entirely unavailable for a month (0% availability), Logith recovers only $39,000 (50% of $78,000) in credits. The base credit schedule (§4.1) caps at 30% for availability below 98%, and the Auction Hours add-on (§4.2) adds 5%, for a maximum of 35%—well below the 50% cap, but the cap still limits recovery in extreme scenarios.

**Why It Matters:** The cap means Draibav's maximum financial exposure for a catastrophic month is $39,000—less than half a month's fees. For a business generating seven-figure auction volumes, this is inadequate compensation.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Negotiate to raise or eliminate the 50% cap, or at least increase it to 100% for months with availability below 95%.
- Clarify that the cap does not limit Logith's right to terminate under SLS §5 or to claim damages for Draibav's breach (though §10.1 excludes consequential damages, and §10.3 caps liability at 12 months' fees—see below).

## Issue 9 – Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for a Platform Outage

**Where:** Agreement §§10.1, 10.3, 10.4.

**The Problem:**

- §10.1 excludes all indirect, consequential, and lost-profits damages.
- §10.3 caps total liability at "Fees paid or payable in the 12 months preceding the claim"—approximately **$936,000** (12 × $78,000).
- §10.4(b) carves out breach of Section 8 (confidentiality/data security) from the cap, but a general availability failure is not a Section 8 breach.

If the Platform is down for an extended period and Logith loses auction revenue, bidder relationships, and consignor confidence, Logith's recovery is limited to $936,000 in direct damages (and in practice, to service credits of $39,000/month under the SLS cap). The lost-profits exclusion means Logith cannot recover the actual revenue it failed to collect.

**Why It Matters:** For a wholesale auction business, the Platform *is* the revenue-generating infrastructure. A multi-day outage during peak auction season could cost millions in lost sales. The liability framework provides minimal financial protection.

**Recommended Fix:**

- This is difficult to change post-signing, but if negotiating any amendment (e.g., for the change-of-control consent), seek to add an exclusion from the §10.1 consequential damages bar for "lost auction revenue directly attributable to Platform unavailability during Auction Hours," or at least raise the cap for availability-related claims.
- In the interim, ensure Logith's own business interruption insurance covers Platform outages (confirm with the risk team).

## Issue 10 – Downtime Measurement Ambiguity

**Where:** Service Level Schedule §1.4.

**The Problem:**

"Downtime" is measured "by Licensor's monitoring tools **and confirmed by** Licensee's reasonable records." The conjunction "and" creates ambiguity: does Logith need to independently confirm each Downtime event with its own records, or is Draibav's monitoring the primary measure with Logith's records serving as a secondary check? If Draibav's tools show no downtime but Logith's bidders were locked out, who decides?

**Why It Matters:** In a dispute over whether a Chronic Failure occurred (as is now the case), the measurement methodology is critical. If Draibav's monitoring tools show 99.9% but Logith's records show otherwise, the outcome depends on how this clause is interpreted.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Confirm what Logith's "reasonable records" actually capture. Do Logith's systems log failed connections, bidder complaints, or auction disruptions independently of the Platform?
- If the termination dispute proceeds to litigation, argue that "confirmed by" means Logith's records serve as a verification mechanism (i.e., they can confirm or dispute Draibav's numbers), not an independent requirement that Logith must separately measure downtime.
- In any amendment, clarify that Downtime is measured by the Platform's actual availability to Bidders and Consignors, not solely by Draibav's internal monitoring.

## Issue 11 – Claims-Made Insurance Policies and Long-Term Coverage

**Where:** Certificate of Insurance, §5.2 (TEO and Cyber policies); Agreement §11.1.

**The Problem:**

The Technology E&O and Cyber Liability policies are **claims-made** forms with a retroactive date of March 15, 2016. The Agreement requires Draibav to maintain insurance "during the Term and for two (2) years afterwards" (§11.1). The Initial Term runs to June 30, 2030; plus two years = June 30, 2032. The current certificate shows policy periods of May 1, 2026 to May 1, 2027.

Claims-made policies only cover claims *made during the policy period* (or during an extended reporting period, if purchased). If Draibav lets a policy lapse or fails to purchase a tail/extended reporting period at expiration, a claim made in 2028 relating to a 2026 security incident or service failure could be uninsured.

**Why It Matters:** If a major data breach or platform failure occurs in 2026 and the claim is not made until 2028 (after policy expiration), Draibav may have no insurance coverage, leaving Logith to pursue an unsecured judgment.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Require Draibav to provide updated certificates of insurance annually (the Agreement already permits this under §11.2).
- In any amendment, add a requirement that Draibav maintain continuous claims-made coverage (no gaps) for the full period required by §11.1, and purchase an extended reporting period (tail) of at least two years at the end of the Agreement.
- Confirm that the A.M. Best ratings of all three insurers (A+ XV, A XIII, A X) meet the "A- VII or better" requirement in §11.1. They do, but monitor for any downgrade.

## Issue 13 – Order Form Licensee Data Definition Creates Implementation Risk

**Where:** Order Form §4.1; Agreement §7.3.

**The Problem (related to Issue 6 but with a practical angle):**

Section 7.3 requires Draibav to make "all Licensee Data" available for export for 60 days post-termination. If the narrow Order Form definition controls, Platform-generated data (bid timestamps, system-generated run lists, auction result logs) may not be exportable. Logith could lose its historical auction data upon termination.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Before any termination takes effect, request a full data export from Draibav *now*, expressly including all Platform-generated data, and confirm in writing that Draibav will include it under §7.3.
- If the narrow definition is held to control, argue that §7.3's reference to "all Licensee Data" should be read in context with §1.9's broader definition (which describes what data the Platform actually holds), or seek a specific data export agreement as a condition of any negotiated resolution.
