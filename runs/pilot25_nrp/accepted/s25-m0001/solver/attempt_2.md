## Issue 1 – Change of Control: Kestrova Transaction Triggers Immediate Termination Right Without Cure

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 17.2 (Change of Control of Distributor)
- SLS § 8.1 (Pending Transaction), § 8.3 (Dealer Portal Transition)
- DA § 16.1 (Termination for Breach)

**Explanation:**

DA § 17.2 provides that any Change of Control of Distributor "shall be deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Distributor requiring Supplier's prior written consent under Section 17.1." It further states: "A Change of Control without Supplier's prior written consent is a material breach of this Agreement, and Supplier may, on written notice, immediately terminate this Agreement and suspend shipments of Products under any Purchase Order, without opportunity to cure."

The definition of Change of Control in § 17.2(a) captures "any transaction or series of related transactions by which any person or group acquires, directly or indirectly, more than fifty percent (50%) of the voting equity of Distributor or of any entity Controlling Distributor."

SLS § 8.1 confirms that Sterin Holdings, Inc. (the parent of Distributor) entered into a Stock Purchase Agreement dated September 22, 2026 with Kestrova Agritech, Inc., under which Sterin Holdings will sell 100% of the equity of Distributor to Kestrova. Closing is expected January 15, 2027.

Critically, SLS § 8.3 states: "Nothing in this Section 8 constitutes Supplier's consent under Section 17.2 of the Distribution Agreement." By signing the SLS (dated October 20, 2026), both parties acknowledged the transaction but Supplier expressly disclaimed consent.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

On the expected closing date (January 15, 2027), the Kestrova transaction will constitute a Change of Control. Because Supplier has not given written consent (and has affirmatively disclaimed it), the closing will be a material breach. Supplier will then have the right to (a) terminate the Agreement immediately and (b) suspend all shipments under any Purchase Order, without giving Sterin the 30-day cure period that § 16.1 otherwise provides for material breaches. Sterin would lose its exclusive distribution rights, its inventory repurchase right under § 16.3(c) (which applies "on termination or expiration" but is triggered by Supplier's termination for breach, meaning Supplier would not be obligated to fulfill accepted Purchase Orders under § 16.3(a)), and its ongoing supply relationship—potentially overnight, with no cure period.

This is the highest-exposure issue because it is time-certain (closing is ~5 weeks away as of the SLS Restatement Date) and the consequence is total loss of the commercial relationship.

**Recommended Fix:**

Before closing, Sterin must obtain Supplier's written consent to the Change of Control under § 17.2. We recommend:

1. Open a formal consent request with Pridort immediately, referencing § 17.2 and the SLS § 8 acknowledgment.
2. Negotiate the consent as part of a broader package that may include: (a) a commitment from Kestrova to assume Sterin's obligations under the DA (mirroring the affiliate-assignment carve-out in § 17.1, which permits Supplier to assign to an Affiliate or successor without consent—Sterin should seek reciprocal treatment); (b) a transition period during which exclusivity is maintained; and (c) confirmation that the Parent Guaranty remains in effect (see Issue 5).
3. If Pridort refuses consent, evaluate whether to (a) restructure the transaction so that Sterin Holdings retains >50% (unlikely given the 100% sale), (b) seek a negotiated exit with buyout of inventory under § 16.3(c), or (c) assess the commercial value of litigating the reasonableness of the consent denial (though the contract language is unambiguous).

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- Has Sterin approached Pridort for consent, and what has Pridort's response been to date?
- Are there any side letters, emails, or oral assurances from Pridort regarding the Kestrova transaction?
- What are the conditions precedent in the Stock Purchase Agreement? Does the Kestrova deal require maintenance of the Pridort distribution as a closing condition?
- What is Sterin's current inventory position and 12-month purchase volume (relevant to quantifying exposure if the relationship terminates)?

## Issue 2 – Recall Cost Cap: Direct Conflict Between DA and QA Resolved Against Sterin

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 12.1 (Liability Cap), § 12.3(d) (Recall Costs Excluded from Cap)
- QA § 9.4 (Recall Cost Reimbursement – Subject to Cap)
- DA § 20.4 (Order of Precedence)
- Guaranty § 2.2 (Extent of Guaranteed Obligations)

**Explanation:**

DA § 12.3(d) states: "Supplier's recall cost reimbursement under Section 9.5, which is excluded from the Section 12.1 cap." This means recall costs are uncapped under the DA.

QA § 9.4 states: "Supplier's aggregate liability for Recall Costs is subject to the limitation set forth in Section 12.1 of the Distribution Agreement, and all amounts paid or payable by Supplier in respect of Recall Costs shall be counted toward that limitation." This means recall costs ARE capped under the QA.

These provisions directly contradict each other. DA § 20.4 provides the order of precedence: "In the event of any conflict or inconsistency among them, they shall prevail in the following order of precedence: Quality Agreement (quality, nonconformance and recall matters only), then Distribution Agreement, then Service Level Schedule, then Purchase Orders."

Because recall is a "recall matter," the QA prevails. Therefore, **recall costs are subject to the 12-month purchase cap in DA § 12.1**, despite the DA's own text saying they are excluded.

Furthermore, Guaranty § 2.2 provides that "Guarantor's liability under this Guaranty shall not exceed the liability that Supplier would have to Distributor under the Guaranteed Documents for the same matter" and that "[e]very limitation, exclusion and defense available to Supplier under the Guaranteed Documents is available to Guarantor." The QA is a "Guaranteed Document" (Guaranty § 1.2(b)). So the guaranty is also subject to the recall cost cap.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

The Territory covers 12 Midwestern states. The installed base of agricultural spray drones (AX-60, AX-40) and mapping drones (AX-M2) could be substantial. A recall triggered by a Critical Nonconformity (e.g., nozzle flow-controller failure leading to uncontrolled chemical discharge, or battery thermal event) could involve thousands of units. Recall Costs under DA § 9.5 include Customer notification, retrieval, shipping, field labor, replacement Products and parts, and refunds or credits to Customers. In a worst-case scenario, recall costs could far exceed 12 months of Sterin's purchases from Pridort, leaving Sterin bearing the excess out of pocket with no recourse against Pridort or its parent guarantor.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Immediate:** Negotiate an amendment to QA § 9.4 to conform it to DA § 12.3(d), removing the cap on recall costs. Given the precedence rule, this requires amending the QA (not the DA).
2. **Alternative:** If Pridort resists uncapping recall costs entirely, negotiate a higher sub-cap for recall costs (e.g., 3x the 12-month purchase amount) or a carve-out that recall costs are excluded from the cap but subject to the product recall insurance policy limits (DA § 13.1 requires $2M product recall insurance).
3. **Supplemental:** Confirm that Pridort's product recall insurance (minimum $2M per DA § 13.1) is in force and that Sterin is named as an additional insured or loss payee on the recall policy. Request a certificate.
4. **Guaranty:** If the recall cap is negotiated upward or removed in the QA, confirm the Guaranty automatically benefits (it should, per § 2.2, since it tracks the "Guaranteed Documents read together").

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What is Sterin's trailing 12-month purchase amount from Pridort (i.e., the current cap value)?
- What is the approximate installed base of AX-series products in the Territory?
- Does Sterin have its own product recall insurance or contingency budget?
- Has Pridort provided a certificate of its product recall insurance policy?

## Issue 3 – Incoming Inspection Deemed Acceptance and the OM-01 Dispute (Lot SR-4417)

**Documents and Sections:**
- QA § 6.2 (Rejection Notice; Deemed Acceptance; Waiver of Claims)
- QA Schedule 2, Part A (Incoming Inspection Functional Checks)
- SLS Annex B, OM-01 (Open Matter)
- DA § 9.1 (Product Warranty)

**Explanation:**

QA § 6.2 provides that Distributor must reject any Product or Lot having a Nonconformity "discoverable at incoming inspection" by written notice within **ten (10) Business Days after delivery**. If Sterin fails to reject within that window, the Product is "deemed accepted, and Distributor waives all claims with respect to any Nonconformity in that Product that was discoverable at incoming inspection, **including warranty claims under Section 9 of the Distribution Agreement**."

OM-01 (SLS Annex B) states: Lot SR-4417 (140 AX-60 spray drones) was delivered August 4, 2026. Sterin delivered written notice of a nozzle flow-controller Nonconformity on August 25, 2026. Pridort rejected the notice as untimely on September 3, 2026. Sterin disputes and reserves all rights.

Counting 10 Business Days from August 4, 2026 (a Tuesday): August 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18. The deadline was **August 18, 2026**. Sterin's notice on August 25 was **5 Business Days late**.

The incoming inspection protocol for AX-60 (QA Schedule 2, Part A) includes "nozzle flow-controller static flow test on sampled units." The word "sampled" is key: the inspection does not test every unit. If the nonconformity was present in units that were not sampled, an argument exists that it was not "discoverable at incoming inspection" within the meaning of § 6.2. However, Pridort's position is that the static flow test was part of the protocol and the nonconformity was therefore discoverable.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

If Pridort prevails, Sterin has waived all claims—including the 12-month product warranty under DA § 9.1—on 140 AX-60 units with a nozzle flow-controller defect. Nozzle flow controllers are critical to spray application accuracy; a defect could lead to improper pesticide application, which carries regulatory (EPA/state) and customer liability exposure. Sterin, as the party providing first-line service and warranty administration (DA § 7.2), would bear the field service costs, customer complaints, and potential regulatory issues for these 140 units with no recourse against Pridort.

More broadly, the 10-Business-Day window with full waiver is an aggressive term that creates ongoing operational risk: any defect that is "discoverable" through the sampling protocol but not caught within 10 days results in a permanent waiver of warranty.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **For OM-01 specifically:** Develop the factual record on whether the nonconformity was truly "discoverable" under the sampling plan. Key questions: What was the sampling rate? Were the affected units in the sample? Did the static flow test protocol have sensitivity sufficient to detect the specific failure mode? If the sampling rate was low (e.g., 1 in 20) and the affected units were not sampled, Sterin has a strong argument that the nonconformity was not "discoverable at incoming inspection" as a matter of fact.
2. **For the contract term going forward:** Negotiate an amendment to QA § 6.2 to (a) extend the rejection window to 20 or 30 Business Days, (b) limit the waiver to only those units actually tested under the sampling plan (i.e., the waiver applies only to sampled units, not the entire Lot), or (c) carve out Critical Nonconformities from the deemed-acceptance waiver entirely (a Critical Nonconformity by definition involves safety risk, and it would be unreasonable to waive claims for a safety defect simply because it wasn't caught in a 10-day sampling window).
3. **Operational:** Ensure Sterin's receiving team is trained and resourced to complete all Schedule 2, Part A checks within 10 Business Days of every delivery, and document the inspection thoroughly (photos, test results, serial numbers checked) to preserve the ability to prove timeliness in future disputes.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What was the sampling rate for Lot SR-4417? How many of the 140 units were actually subjected to the nozzle flow-controller static flow test?
- Were any of the sampled units found to be nonconforming, or was the nonconformity identified only in field use?
- What is the specific nature of the nozzle flow-controller defect (e.g., flow rate out of spec, intermittent failure, complete blockage)?
- Has Sterin received any field failures or customer complaints related to the 140 units in Lot SR-4417?
- What is the current status of discussions with Pridort on OM-01? Has Pridort offered any partial remedy?

## Issue 4 – Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for Sterin's Downstream Exposure

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 12.1 (Cap: 12 months of amounts paid or payable)
- DA § 12.2 (Consequential Damages Exclusion)
- DA § 11.2 (Distributor's Indemnification to Supplier)
- DA § 7.2 (Distributor's Service and Support Obligations)
- DA § 9.4 (Pass-Through Warranty)

**Explanation:**

DA § 12.1 caps each party's aggregate liability at "the amounts paid or payable by Distributor in the 12 months preceding the claim." This is a mutual cap, but it operates asymmetrically in practice: Sterin's exposure to Pridort is largely limited to its purchase obligations (which it controls), while Pridort's exposure to Sterin includes warranty, recall, indemnification, and supply failures that could generate costs well in excess of 12 months of purchases.

DA § 12.2 excludes consequential damages and lost profits for both parties. However, DA § 1.5 defines "Losses" (used in the indemnification section) to "include lost profits and direct losses whether or not arising out of a third-party claim," and § 12.3(a) excludes indemnification obligations from both the cap and the consequential damages bar. So the indemnification section is uncapped and includes lost profits—but only for the specific triggers in §§ 11.1 and 11.2.

The practical concern: If a Product defect causes a Customer (grower, cooperative, or custom applicator) to suffer crop loss, personal injury, or regulatory fines, the Customer will look to Sterin (the local distributor providing first-line service and warranty administration under § 7.2, and the party that may have extended the pass-through warranty under § 9.4). Sterin's recourse against Pridort for a third-party product liability claim would be under § 11.1 (IP indemnity—limited to IP claims) or § 9.1/9.3 (warranty—repair/replace/credit). Notably, **there is no general product liability indemnity from Supplier to Distributor** for third-party personal injury or property damage claims arising from the Products. The only indemnity running from Supplier to Distributor is for IP infringement (§ 11.1). For product liability claims (the most likely serious third-party claims for agricultural drones), Sterin's remedy is limited to the warranty provisions (§ 9.1–9.3), which provide repair, replacement, or credit—but do not indemnify Sterin against the Customer's damages.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

Sterin is the local face of the product. It provides first-line technical support, field service, and warranty administration (§ 7.2). It may extend the end-user warranty to Customers (§ 9.4). If a drone malfunctions in a grower's field and causes crop damage, chemical contamination, or personal injury, the grower will sue Sterin (and possibly Pridort). Sterin has no contractual indemnity from Pridort for product liability claims—only a warranty remedy (repair/replace/credit). Sterin's own CGL insurance ($2M per § 13.1) may be insufficient for a serious drone accident, and Sterin would have no contractual basis to recover from Pridort beyond the warranty credit.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Add a product liability indemnity from Supplier to Distributor.** Negotiate a new § 11.1(b) (or restructure § 11) providing that Supplier shall indemnify Distributor against third-party claims for personal injury, death, or property damage to the extent caused by a defect in the Products or by Supplier's negligence. This is standard in distribution agreements for products with inherent risk (drones, batteries, pesticide application equipment).
2. **Increase the liability cap** or add a super-cap for product liability and recall (e.g., 3x trailing 12-month purchases, or aligned with the insurance limits in § 13.1).
3. **Confirm insurance adequacy:** DA § 13.1 requires Pridort to carry $5M CGL (products-completed operations), $5M aviation liability, and $2M product recall. Request current certificates and confirm Sterin is named as additional insured on the CGL and aviation policies. If the product liability indemnity gap is not contractually fixed, the insurance is Sterin's primary protection—ensure the limits are adequate for the installed base.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- Has Sterin received any product liability claims, customer complaints involving injury or property damage, or regulatory inquiries related to the AX-series products?
- What are Sterin's current CGL insurance limits and does the policy cover product liability for distributed (non-manufactured) products?
- What is the approximate installed base of AX-series products in the Territory?
- Has Sterin extended the pass-through warranty (§ 9.4) to any Customers, and if so, what are the terms?

## Issue 5 – Parent Guaranty Is Only as Strong as the Underlying Caps and Lacks Ongoing Financial Covenants

**Documents and Sections:**
- Guaranty § 2.2 (Extent of Guaranteed Obligations – Subject to Same Limitations)
- Guaranty § 5(f) (One-Time Solvency Representation)
- Guaranty § 6.1 (Financial Statements "On Request")
- DA § 14.1 (Parent Guaranty Requirement)

**Explanation:**

The Guaranty is structured as a guaranty of payment and performance (Guaranty § 2.1, 2.3), which is favorable to Sterin: it is a primary obligation, not merely surety, and Sterin need not exhaust remedies against Supplier first. However, § 2.2 provides that "Guarantor's liability under this Guaranty shall not exceed the liability that Supplier would have to Distributor under the Guaranteed Documents for the same matter" and that "[e]very limitation, exclusion and defense available to Supplier under the Guaranteed Documents is available to Guarantor."

This means:
- The 12-month purchase cap (DA § 12.1) limits the Guaranty.
- The consequential damages exclusion (DA § 12.2) limits the Guaranty.
- The recall cost cap (as resolved in Issue 2, via QA § 9.4) limits the Guaranty.
- The product liability indemnity gap (Issue 4) means the Guaranty does not cover what the underlying documents do not require Supplier to pay.

Additionally, the Guaranty contains only a one-time solvency representation (§ 5(f)) as of the signing date (March 3, 2025). There is no ongoing financial maintenance covenant, no minimum tangible net worth requirement, no restriction on Guarantor's indebtedness, and no requirement that Guarantor maintain the financial capacity to honor the Guaranty over time. Financial statements are deliverable only "on request" (§ 6.1), not automatically.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

The entire purpose of the Parent Guaranty (DA § 14.1) is to provide Sterin with a creditworthy backstop if Pridort Aerial Robotics, Inc. (the operating subsidiary) fails to perform. If the subsidiary is undercapitalized or the parent's financial condition deteriorates, the Guaranty may be worthless in practice. Given that the Guaranty is subject to the same caps as the underlying documents, its real value is limited to ensuring that Sterin can collect from the parent what the subsidiary owes—capped at 12 months of purchases. For a distributor that has invested in inventory, trained staff, and customer relationships in reliance on the exclusive appointment, this is thin protection.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Negotiate a standalone cap on the Guaranty** that is higher than the DA § 12.1 cap (e.g., 24 months of purchases, or a fixed dollar amount), or at minimum carve out recall costs and the product liability indemnity (if added per Issue 4) from the Guaranty's mirroring of the underlying caps.
2. **Add ongoing financial covenants:** Require Guarantor to deliver annual (not just "on request") audited financial statements, maintain a minimum tangible net worth or debt-to-equity ratio, and provide notice of any material adverse change in financial condition.
3. **Add a springing additional collateral or letter of credit requirement** if Guarantor's financial condition falls below a specified threshold.
4. **Confirm the Change of Control protection:** Guaranty § 4.1(c) provides that the Guaranty is not affected by "any change in the ownership, corporate structure or existence of Supplier or Guarantor, including any assignment by Supplier permitted under Section 17.1." This is good—it means the Guaranty survives even if Pridort restructures. However, we should confirm that a Change of Control of *Guarantor* (Pridort Holdings) itself does not trigger any issue. The Guaranty is silent on a Change of Control of Guarantor. If Pridort Holdings were to be acquired, the new parent would step into the Guaranty by operation of § 8.3 ("binds Guarantor and its successors and assigns"), but we would want to confirm the acquirer has sufficient creditworthiness.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What is Pridort Holdings, Inc.'s current financial condition? Has Sterin requested and received financial statements under Guaranty § 6.1?
- Is Pridort Holdings, Inc. publicly traded or privately held? Is there any public information about its financial health?
- Has there been any change in Pridort Holdings' ownership or capital structure since March 2025?

## Issue 6 – Minimum Purchase Commitment and Risk of Loss of Exclusivity

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 4.3 (Minimum Purchase Commitment; Conversion to Non-Exclusive)
- DA § 2.1–2.2 (Exclusive Appointment; Supplier Restrictions)
- SLS § 3.3 (Forecast Reliance – 125% Threshold)

**Explanation:**

DA § 4.3 requires Sterin to purchase minimum quantities (set in Exhibit D) each contract year. If Sterin misses the minimum, Supplier may "convert Distributor's appointment to non-exclusive for the remainder of the Term" as its "sole right" under that section. The notice window is 90 days after the end of the contract year.

The exclusivity in § 2.2 is a core economic benefit of the arrangement: it prevents Pridort from selling directly or through other distributors in the 12-state Territory (except reserved national accounts in Exhibit C). Loss of exclusivity would fundamentally alter Sterin's business model and competitive position.

Compounding the risk: SLS § 3.3 excludes from the fill-rate measure any orders exceeding 125% of the forecast. This means if Sterin's actual demand surges (e.g., due to market growth or a competitor's product failure), Pridort is not measured on those excess units for service credit purposes. While this does not directly affect the minimum purchase commitment, it means Sterin could face a scenario where demand exceeds supply (because Pridort is not incentivized to fill above-forecast orders) and Sterin cannot meet its minimum purchase commitment because Pridort cannot deliver—yet the minimum is still measured on purchases actually made.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

If the Kestrova transition (Issue 1) disrupts operations, or if Pridort's delivery performance is poor (as suggested by OM-02 backorders), Sterin may miss its minimum purchase commitment. The consequence—loss of exclusivity for the remainder of the Term (potentially 3+ years)—is severe and disproportionate to the breach.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Negotiate a supply-failure carve-out to the minimum purchase commitment:** If Sterin misses the minimum because Pridort failed to deliver ordered Products (i.e., the shortfall is attributable to Pridort's supply failure, not Sterin's demand failure), the minimum should be reduced by the undelivered quantity.
2. **Negotiate a cure period or alternative remedy:** Instead of automatic conversion to non-exclusive, provide for a 6-month cure period or a monetary payment in lieu (e.g., Sterin pays the difference between actual and minimum purchases at a reduced price).
3. **Confirm Exhibit D minimums:** Review the actual minimum quantities to assess whether they are achievable given current market conditions and the pending Kestrova transition.
4. **Coordinate with Issue 1:** If Sterin obtains consent to the Kestrova Change of Control, negotiate a corresponding adjustment to the minimum purchase commitment for the transition year.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What are the actual minimum purchase quantities in Exhibit D for the current contract year (2026–2027)?
- What has Sterin's actual purchase volume been to date in the current contract year?
- Has Sterin been meeting its rolling forecasts under DA § 4.2?
- Is there any correlation between Pridort's delivery failures (OM-02 backorders) and Sterin's ability to meet minimums?

## Issue 7 – Service Credit Cap Limits Recovery for Chronic Delivery Failures

**Documents and Sections:**
- SLS § 5.3 (Monthly Maximum: 10% of amounts invoiced)
- SLS § 5.4 (Service Credits Do Not Limit Other Rights, But Offset Damages)
- SLS § 5.5 (Chronic Failure = Material Breach)
- SLS Annex A, Part 2 (Service Credit Schedule)
- SLS Annex B, OM-02 (AX-B60 Backorders)

**Explanation:**

The service credit structure (Annex A, Part 2) provides modest credits: 2% of amounts invoiced for late units if fill rate is 90–95%, 5% if 85–90%, 8% if below 85%, and 10% for spare parts below 98%. The monthly cap in § 5.3 limits total credits to 10% of amounts invoiced for Products shipped in that month.

§ 5.4 preserves Sterin's other rights (including termination for chronic failure under § 5.5) but provides that "any damages recovered for a late delivery shall be reduced by the service credits issued for it." This means service credits are an offset, not a floor—Sterin can still claim additional damages for late delivery (subject to the DA § 12.1 cap and § 12.2 consequential damages exclusion), but the credits reduce the recovery.

OM-02 shows 220 AX-B60 batteries backordered as of September 30, 2026, with shipment promised by November 30, 2026. Batteries are critical: without them, spray drones are grounded. A 2-month battery shortage during peak season (which runs through July 31, but spring planning and pre-season stocking occur in winter/early spring) could have significant downstream effects on Sterin's ability to service Customers.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

The 10% monthly cap means that even in a month of severe delivery failure, Sterin's maximum service credit recovery is limited. If the failure persists, Sterin's primary remedy is the chronic failure termination right (§ 5.5)—but termination is a nuclear option that Sterin may not want to exercise (it ends the relationship). The offset provision in § 5.4 further reduces any damages claim. In practice, service credits may be the only regularly available remedy for delivery shortfalls, and they are modest.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Increase the service credit percentages** in Annex A, Part 2, particularly for the below-85% tier (currently 8%—arguably should be higher given the operational impact of drone/battery shortages on agricultural customers).
2. **Raise or eliminate the monthly cap** in § 5.3, or at minimum increase it to 15–20%.
3. **Add a peak-season multiplier:** Service credits during Peak Season (March 1–July 31) should be doubled or tripled, given the criticality of timely delivery during the planting/spraying window.
4. **Clarify the interaction with the DA cap:** Confirm that service credits (as price adjustments) do not count against the DA § 12.1 liability cap, or that the cap is calculated net of credits already issued.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What has Sterin's actual experience been with Pridort's on-time fill rate over the past 12 months?
- Has Sterin claimed service credits, and if so, what amounts were issued?
- What is the operational impact of the OM-02 battery backorder on Sterin's Customer service levels?
- Are there any Customers who have threatened to terminate or reduce orders due to product unavailability?

## Issue 8 – No General Product Liability Indemnity from Supplier (Addressed in Issue 4, but Flagged Separately for Clarity)

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 11.1 (Supplier Indemnity – IP Only)
- DA § 11.2 (Distributor Indemnity – Broad)
- DA § 9.1–9.3 (Warranty – Repair/Replace/Credit Only)

**Explanation:**

The indemnification structure is heavily one-sided in Pridort's favor. Supplier indemnifies Distributor only for third-party IP infringement claims (§ 11.1). Distributor indemnifies Supplier for (a) any breach of the Agreement, (b) Distributor's marketing, promotion and resale (including unauthorized representations to Customers), and (c) Distributor's negligence or willful misconduct (§ 11.2).

There is no provision under which Supplier indemnifies Distributor for third-party product liability claims (personal injury, property damage, environmental contamination) arising from a defect in the Products. Sterin's only contractual recourse for a defective product is the warranty remedy in § 9.3 (repair, replace, or credit the price paid). If a drone crashes into a grower's crop or injures a worker, the grower's damages (crop loss, medical expenses, regulatory fines) far exceed the price of the drone, and Sterin has no contractual mechanism to pass those costs to Pridort.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

This is the same issue identified in Issue 4, but it warrants separate emphasis because the asymmetry is stark: Sterin indemnifies Pridort broadly (any breach, any marketing representation, any negligence), while Pridort indemnifies Sterin only for IP claims. For a product category involving lithium batteries, pesticide application, and unmanned aircraft operation, product liability is the most probable and most severe risk.

**Recommended Fix:**

See Issue 4. The primary fix is to add a product liability indemnity from Supplier. In the alternative, if Pridort refuses, Sterin should (a) ensure its own CGL policy has adequate product liability coverage for distributed products, (b) negotiate a higher CGL limit (currently $2M per DA § 13.1—arguably low for drone product liability), and (c) require Pridort's product liability insurance to name Sterin as additional insured with a higher limit.

## Issue 9 – NDA and Distribution Agreement Confidentiality Provisions Create Overlap and Ambiguity

**Documents and Sections:**
- NDA § 9.1 (Disclosure Period: 2 years from November 18, 2024 → ends November 18, 2026)
- NDA § 9.2 (Obligations: 3 years after disclosure period ends → November 18, 2029; indefinite for trade secrets)
- DA § 15.3 (Confidentiality Survival: 3 years after termination or expiration; indefinite for trade secrets)
- DA § 20.4 (Order of Precedence – NDA not listed)

**Explanation:**

The NDA was signed November 18, 2024, before the DA (March 3, 2025). The NDA's disclosure period ended November 18, 2026. Its confidentiality obligations run until November 18, 2029 (3 years post-disclosure-period). The DA's confidentiality obligations (§ 15) run for 3 years after DA termination or expiration (the DA has a 5-year initial term from March 3, 2025, so earliest expiration is March 3, 2030, plus 3 years = March 3, 2033; or earlier if terminated).

The NDA is not listed in the DA's order of precedence (§ 20.4), which covers only the DA, QA, SLS, and Purchase Orders. This creates ambiguity about which agreement governs confidentiality for information disclosed during the pre-DA negotiation period (November 2024–March 2025) versus information disclosed during the DA term.

Practically, the NDA's obligations will expire in November 2029, while the DA's confidentiality obligations will likely run until at least 2033. For information disclosed during the NDA period, the NDA's 3-year post-disclosure-period limit is shorter than the DA's 3-year post-termination limit. If the DA is still in effect in 2029, one could argue the DA's confidentiality provision (which covers "non-public business, technical and financial information disclosed in connection with this Agreement") would continue to protect that information. But the NDA's scope is broader in some respects (it covers information disclosed "in connection with the Purpose," i.e., evaluating and negotiating the distribution relationship).

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

This is a lower-exposure issue, but it becomes relevant in the context of the Kestrova transaction. When Kestrova acquires Sterin, it will gain access to all of Sterin's files, including Pridort's Confidential Information. The NDA's assignment provision (§ 12.3) permits assignment to "a successor to all or substantially all of its business," so the NDA obligations would transfer to Kestrova. But the DA's confidentiality provision (§ 15.1) permits disclosure only to "employees, advisers and financing sources who need to know it and are bound by obligations of confidentiality no less protective." Kestrova as successor would need to be bound by the DA's confidentiality terms (which it would be, if it steps into the DA via the § 17.2 consent process). The overlap creates a question about which set of rules governs and for how long.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. Add a clause to the DA (or a side letter) stating that the NDA is superseded by the DA's confidentiality provisions for all information disclosed on or after the DA Effective Date, and that the NDA continues to apply only to information disclosed before the DA Effective Date.
2. In the Kestrova consent negotiation (Issue 1), include a confidentiality acknowledgment from Kestrova covering all Pridort Confidential Information under both the NDA and the DA.

## Issue 10 – DA § 16.3(a) Carve-Out for Supplier-Initiated Termination Limits Inventory Repurchase

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 16.3(a) (Supplier to Fulfill Accepted POs "unless terminated by Supplier under Section 16.1, 16.2 or 17.2")
- DA § 16.3(c) (Supplier to Repurchase Unsold Inventory at Distributor's Option)

**Explanation:**

DA § 16.3(a) provides that on termination or expiration, "Supplier shall fulfill all Purchase Orders accepted before the effective date of termination **unless terminated by Supplier under Section 16.1, 16.2 or 17.2**." This means if Supplier terminates for Sterin's material breach (§ 16.1), for insolvency (§ 16.2), or for Change of Control without consent (§ 17.2), Supplier is **not** obligated to fulfill accepted Purchase Orders.

However, § 16.3(c) (repurchase of unsold inventory) is not subject to the same carve-out. It states: "Supplier shall, at Distributor's option, repurchase Distributor's unsold, undamaged inventory at the price paid." This appears to apply on any "termination or expiration."

The practical question: If Supplier terminates under § 17.2 (Change of Control without consent), is Sterin entitled to the § 16.3(c) repurchase? The text of § 16.3(c) does not contain the carve-out that § 16.3(a) has. Read literally, yes—Sterin can demand repurchase even if Supplier terminated for Sterin's breach. But Supplier will likely argue that the repurchase obligation is part of the "effect of termination" package and should not apply when termination is for Distributor's material breach. This is a potential dispute.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

If the Kestrova transaction closes without consent (Issue 1), Supplier could terminate under § 17.2. Sterin would then want to invoke § 16.3(c) to have Pridort buy back its inventory. If Pridort refuses, Sterin is left with unsold inventory it cannot sell (because the exclusive appointment—and the trademark license under § 10.1—has ended). The inventory repurchase right is Sterin's primary protection against being stranded with product it cannot sell post-termination.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Clarify in the consent negotiation (Issue 1):** If Sterin obtains consent to the Change of Control, this issue is moot. If it does not, negotiate a termination agreement that expressly preserves § 16.3(c).
2. **Amend § 16.3(c)** to state explicitly that the repurchase obligation applies regardless of the grounds for termination, or to state that it applies on any termination other than Distributor's insolvency.
3. **Quantify inventory exposure now:** Determine the cost of Sterin's current unsold inventory so the commercial team understands the stakes.

**Facts We Need from the Client:**

- What is the current value of Sterin's unsold Pridort inventory?
- How much of that inventory is time-sensitive (e.g., batteries with shelf life, firmware-dependent units that may become obsolete)?

## Issue 11 – Order of Precedence Creates Ambiguity for Non-Quality Matters

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 20.4 (Order of Precedence)

**Explanation:**

DA § 20.4 provides: "In the event of any conflict or inconsistency among them, they shall prevail in the following order of precedence: Quality Agreement (quality, nonconformance and recall matters only), then Distribution Agreement, then Service Level Schedule, then Purchase Orders."

The QA prevails only for "quality, nonconformance and recall matters." For all other matters, the DA prevails over the SLS. This is generally sensible, but it creates a gray area: Where does a "recall matter" end and a "commercial matter" begin? For example, the recall cost cap conflict (Issue 2) was resolved by treating it as a "recall matter" (QA prevails). But what about the interaction between QA § 9.4 (recall cost reimbursement timing: 30 days after invoice) and DA § 5.3 (payment terms: 45 days for undisputed invoices)? Is the timing of recall cost reimbursement a "recall matter" (QA governs) or a "payment matter" (DA governs)? The answer is probably that QA governs (30 days), but the ambiguity could be exploited in a dispute.

Similarly, the SLS sets out operational terms (lead times, support hours, training) that interact with DA § 7.2 (Distributor's service obligations) and DA § 4.4 (Supplier's supply obligation). If the SLS and DA conflict on, say, what constitutes "commercially reasonable efforts" in supply, the DA prevails (since it's not a quality/recall matter). But the SLS was specifically negotiated to operationalize the DA's supply obligations, so one could argue the SLS should prevail for the specific metrics it sets.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

In a dispute, Pridort could argue that a particular issue is (or is not) a "quality, nonconformance or recall matter" to invoke (or avoid) the QA's precedence. The recall cost cap issue (Issue 2) is the clearest example of this working against Sterin.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Amend DA § 20.4** to provide a more precise delineation. For example: "The Quality Agreement prevails over the Distribution Agreement solely with respect to the procedures and standards for incoming inspection, nonconformance handling, corrective action, traceability, and recall conduct. All other matters, including the financial terms of recall cost reimbursement, are governed by the Distribution Agreement."
2. **Alternatively,** amend QA § 9.4 to conform to DA § 12.3(d) (see Issue 2), which would eliminate the most significant conflict.

## Issue 12 – Guaranty Does Not Cover a Change of Control of Guarantor

**Documents and Sections:**
- Guaranty § 4.1(c) (Not Affected by Change in Ownership of Supplier or Guarantor)
- Guaranty § 6.2 (Notice if Guarantor Cessates to Control Supplier)
- Guaranty § 8.3 (Binds Successors and Assigns)

**Explanation:**

Guaranty § 4.1(c) states that the Guaranty is not affected by "any change in the ownership, corporate structure or existence of Supplier or Guarantor." This appears to protect Sterin if Pridort Holdings is acquired—the Guaranty continues. § 8.3 confirms the Guaranty "binds Guarantor and its successors and assigns."

However, § 6.2 requires Guarantor to give notice "within thirty (30) days after it ceases to Control Supplier." This is a narrow notice obligation (only if Pridort Holdings stops controlling Pridort Aerial Robotics). It does not require notice of a Change of Control of Pridort Holdings itself.

The practical concern: If Pridort Holdings is acquired by a distressed or less-creditworthy entity, Sterin would have no contractual right to be notified (beyond the 30-day notice if the acquirer ceases to "Control" Pridort Aerial Robotics—which it likely would, through the Pridort Holdings intermediate). The Guaranty would continue to bind the new parent, but its value would depend on the new parent's creditworthiness. Sterin has no right to demand additional security or to terminate the Guaranty if the parent's credit deteriorates.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

This is a secondary concern compared to Issues 1–4, but it becomes relevant if Pridort Holdings is in a position to be sold or restructured. Given that Pridort Holdings is the guarantor of all of Pridort Aerial Robotics' obligations to Sterin, a change in its ownership or financial condition directly affects Sterin's credit protection.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Broaden § 6.2** to require notice of any Change of Control of Guarantor (not just cessation of Control over Supplier), using the same definition as DA § 17.2.
2. **Add a Sterin termination right or additional security requirement** if Guarantor undergoes a Change of Control to an entity that does not meet a specified credit threshold.
3. **Monitor Pridort Holdings' public filings** (if any) or request updated financial statements annually (see Issue 5).

## Issue 13 – SLS § 8.2 Notice Obligation Creates Ongoing Disclosure Duty

**Documents and Sections:**
- SLS § 8.2 (Information – Keep Supplier "Reasonably Informed")

**Explanation:**

SLS § 8.2 requires Distributor to "keep Supplier reasonably informed of the status of the transaction described in Section 8.1, including any change to the expected closing date, and shall give Supplier written notice of closing within two (2) Business Days after it occurs."

This is a continuing obligation that runs until closing. If the Kestrova transaction is delayed, amended, or terminated, Sterin must notify Pridort. The "reasonably informed" standard is vague and could be invoked by Pridort as a basis to claim Sterin breached the SLS if Pridort feels it was not given sufficient detail.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

While not a high-exposure issue on its own, this obligation interacts with Issue 1. If Sterin is negotiating for consent to the Change of Control, it is simultaneously obligated to keep Pridort "reasonably informed" of the transaction's status. This could create tension: Sterin may not want to disclose certain deal terms (e.g., Kestrova's strategic plans for the distribution business, pricing terms in the Stock Purchase Agreement) while also complying with the "reasonably informed" obligation.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Clarify the scope of "reasonably informed"** in the consent negotiation: Confirm that the obligation extends to material changes in timing or structure, but does not require disclosure of the Stock Purchase Agreement's financial terms or Kestrova's internal strategic plans.
2. **Coordinate communications:** All updates to Pridort regarding the Kestrova transaction should go through counsel to ensure consistency and to avoid inadvertently waiving rights or making admissions.

## Issue 14 – DA § 4.1 Deemed Acceptance of Purchase Orders Favors Sterin but Creates Supply Commitments

**Documents and Sections:**
- DA § 4.1 (Purchase Orders Deemed Accepted if Not Rejected in 5 Business Days)
- DA § 4.4 (Supply Obligation)
- SLS § 3.1–3.2 (Lead Times; Committed Ship Date)

**Explanation:**

DA § 4.1 provides that a Purchase Order "not rejected within [5 Business Days] after receipt is deemed accepted." Once accepted (or deemed accepted), Supplier is obligated to supply the Products under § 4.4, and the lead times and fill-rate commitments in the SLS apply.

This is favorable to Sterin in that it prevents Pridort from sitting on orders and creating uncertainty. However, it also means that if Sterin's ordering team issues a Purchase Order in error (wrong quantity, wrong product, wrong delivery date), Pridort is deemed to have accepted it within 5 Business Days, and Sterin is committed to purchase. There is no provision for Sterin to cancel a Purchase Order after it has been deemed accepted.

**Why It Matters to Sterin:**

This is a lower-exposure operational risk, but in the context of the Kestrova transition (where ordering processes may be disrupted), the risk of erroneous Purchase Orders increases. Each erroneous order that is deemed accepted becomes a binding purchase commitment.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Add a mutual cancellation right** for Purchase Orders within a specified period after deemed acceptance (e.g., either party may cancel a PO within 10 Business Days of deemed acceptance, with the cancelling party bearing reasonable costs incurred).
2. **Implement internal controls** to ensure Purchase Orders are reviewed and approved before transmission, particularly during the Kestrova transition period.
