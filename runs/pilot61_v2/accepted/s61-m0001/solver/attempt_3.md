## 1. Survival Period Drastically Shortened from LOI and Prior Draft

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 4.1; LOI § 4.1; Prior Draft SPA § 9.1

**Issue:** The final SPA § 9.1 provides that all non-fundamental representations and warranties survive only "until the Escrow Release Date." The Escrow Release Date is defined in Escrow Agreement § 4.1 as **six (6) months** after the Closing Date.

The LOI § 4.1 contemplated 18 months for general representations and **24 months for product safety and recall representations** (§ 4.14). The Prior Draft SPA § 9.1 implemented exactly that: "The representations and warranties in Section 4.14 shall survive the Closing until 24 months after the Closing Date. All other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement shall survive the Closing until 18 months after the Closing Date."

The final SPA eliminated both the 18-month general survival and the 24-month product safety survival, replacing them with a uniform 6-month period tied to the escrow release.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Ostbrook Kibble is a pet food manufacturer. Product safety issues (Salmonella, Listeria, aflatoxin contamination, misbranding) frequently surface months or years after production. A 6-month survival period means Peinol loses its indemnification remedy for most representation breaches—including the critical § 4.14 product safety reps—just six months after closing. The $4.2M escrow also releases at that same date (Escrow Agreement § 4.2), leaving Peinol to pursue Seller directly for any claims not filed within the window.

**Recommendation:** Restore the Prior Draft / LOI structure: 18 months for general reps, 24 months for § 4.14 (product safety and recalls). Alternatively, if the 6-month escrow release is non-negotiable, decouple the survival period from the Escrow Release Date and provide independent survival periods. At minimum, extend § 4.14 survival to 24 months.

## 2. Pawford Change-of-Control Consent Not a Closing Condition

**Documents:** SPA § 7.2(c); SPA § 3.1(f); Disclosure Schedule 4.6 (Parts A and B); Disclosure Schedule 4.8, Part A, Item 1(d); LOI § 5(c)

**Issue:** Disclosure Schedule 4.8, Part A, Item 1(d) discloses that the Pawford Agreement—the Company's **largest customer** at approximately $31.6M in FY2025 (nearly 38% of the $84M purchase price)—contains a provision allowing Pawford to terminate on 60 days' written notice if "any transfer of more than 50% of the voting equity of the Company occurs, whether directly or indirectly and whether in one transaction or a series of related transactions, **without Pawford's prior written consent**."

Despite this, Disclosure Schedule 4.6, Part B states: "None. No consent of any third party is required in connection with the execution, delivery and performance by Seller and the Company of the Agreement or the consummation of the Transactions."

SPA § 7.2(c) makes it a closing condition only that "each of the consents listed on Schedule 4.6 shall have been obtained." Since Schedule 4.6 lists no consents, this condition is automatically satisfied. SPA § 3.1(f) likewise requires delivery of "the third-party consents listed on Schedule 4.6"—an empty set.

The LOI § 5(c) expressly contemplated "receipt of all required third-party and governmental consents, **including consents under the Company's material customer and supply contracts**" as a closing condition. The final SPA narrowed this to only Schedule 4.6 consents.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Peinol could close the transaction and, within 60 days, have its largest revenue stream terminated by Pawford. The $31.6M in annual Pawford revenue represents a material portion of the value underlying the $84M purchase price. Because the risk is disclosed in Schedule 4.8, Seller's representation in § 4.5(c) (no conflicts with Material Contracts) is technically accurate—the transfer doesn't "breach" the Pawford Agreement, it merely triggers a termination right. Peinol would have no indemnification claim for the loss of the Pawford relationship.

**Recommendation:** (a) Add the Pawford consent to Schedule 4.6, Part B, and make it a closing condition under § 7.2(c); or (b) add a specific closing condition requiring delivery of Pawford's written consent or waiver of its termination right; or (c) if consent cannot be obtained pre-closing, negotiate a specific indemnity from Seller for loss of the Pawford Agreement attributable to the change of control, with survival beyond the general period. At a minimum, obtain a bring-down certificate from Seller at closing confirming Pawford has not exercised its termination right.

## 3. Set-Off Rights Against the Seller Note Significantly Restricted

**Documents:** SPA § 9.6(b); SPA § 11.9; Seller Note § 5.1–5.3; Prior Draft SPA § 9.6(b)

**Issue:** Three interlocking provisions substantially limit Peinol's ability to use the Seller Note as a source of recovery for indemnification claims:

**(a) Narrow "Losses" definition in the Note.** Seller Note § 5.2 defines "Losses" for set-off purposes as "out-of-pocket damages, liabilities, judgments, fines, penalties and reasonable attorneys' fees actually paid by a Buyer Indemnitee to a third party, **excluding costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery**, and **excluding any diminution in value, lost profits, or multiple-of-earnings or similar valuation-based damages**."

This is dramatically narrower than the SPA § 10.1 definition of "Losses," which expressly includes "all costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery, customer chargebacks and credits, product destruction and disposal, and regulatory compliance and remediation."

**(b) Precondition of agreement or final judgment.** Seller Note § 5.3 permits set-off only to the extent the amount "(a) has been agreed in writing by Holder; (b) is deemed accepted by Holder under the claims procedures of Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement; or (c) has been finally determined by a final, non-appealable judgment."

**(c) Order of precedence favors the Note.** SPA § 11.9 provides that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to its payment, subordination and **set-off** terms." The Prior Draft SPA § 9.6(b) did not include the qualifier "in accordance with the Seller Note"; the final SPA added it. Combined with § 11.9, the Note's restrictive set-off terms govern.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** The most likely significant claims against Seller in a pet food acquisition will be product recall costs, contamination remediation, and customer chargebacks—all expressly excluded from the Note's set-off definition. Peinol's practical recovery for these claims is limited to (i) the $4.2M escrow (available only for claims filed within 6 months) and (ii) direct action against Seller. The $12M Seller Note—Peinol's largest post-closing asset secured against Seller—cannot be used to offset the very losses most likely to arise in this business.

Additionally, subsection (b) above creates a circularity problem: SPA § 9.4 does not contain a "deemed acceptance" mechanism. The deemed acceptance provision is in Escrow Agreement § 5.3 (no Objection Notice within 30 days = deemed accepted). The Seller Note's cross-reference to "the claims procedures of Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement" for deemed acceptance is therefore to a section that does not provide for it. This could be read to mean set-off is available only with Seller's express written agreement or after a final court judgment—rendering it nearly useless as a practical remedy.

**Recommendation:** (a) Conform Seller Note § 5.2 to the SPA's definition of "Losses," or at minimum include recall, contamination, and chargeback costs; (b) fix the cross-reference in Seller Note § 5.3(b) to point to Escrow Agreement § 5.3 (or add a deemed-acceptance mechanism to SPA § 9.4); (c) delete "in accordance with the Seller Note" from SPA § 9.6(b) or amend SPA § 11.9 to provide that the SPA controls on set-off scope while the Note controls only on payment mechanics and subordination.

## 4. New Uncapped Buyer Indemnification for "Buyer Fundamental Obligations"

**Documents:** SPA § 9.3(c); SPA § 9.5(d); SPA § 10.1 (definition of "Buyer Fundamental Obligations"); Guaranty Side Letter § 2.3; Prior Draft SPA § 9.3

**Issue:** The Prior Draft SPA § 9.3 contained only subsections (a) and (b). The final SPA adds § 9.3(c): "any breach or non-performance of any Buyer Fundamental Obligation."

SPA § 9.5(d) then provides: "The limitations in Sections 9.5(a) and 9.5(b) shall not apply to Losses arising from the Fundamental Representations or Fraud. **Those limitations shall not apply to Buyer Fundamental Obligations.**"

"Buyer Fundamental Obligations" (SPA § 10.1) includes:
- (a) Buyer's obligations to pay the Purchase Price under Article II, including under the Seller Note;
- (b) Buyer's obligations under Section 2.5(d) (final working capital payment); and
- (c) any obligation of Buyer under an Ancillary Agreement that the Ancillary Agreement expressly designates as a Buyer Fundamental Obligation.

Guaranty Side Letter § 2.3 designates Buyer's indemnification obligations under the Side Letter (i.e., indemnifying Seller and Guarantor Members for all Guaranteed Obligations) as a "Buyer Fundamental Obligation."

**Why it matters for Peinol:** This creates an **uncapped, basket-free indemnification** obligation running from Peinol to Seller for: (i) non-payment of the Seller Note; (ii) non-payment of final working capital adjustments; and (iii) failure to indemnify Seller/Guarantor Members under the Side Letter. The Side Letter covers approximately $16.2M in annual contract obligations (equipment lease ~$1.38M, protein/fat supply ~$7.8M, grain supply ~$5.1M, premix supply ~$1.95M). If the Company defaults on any of these and the Guarantor Members are called, Peinol's indemnity to them is uncapped. Seller could then assert a separate indemnification claim under § 9.3(c) if Peinol disputes or fails to pay the Side Letter indemnity in full and on time.

While the obligation to pay the Note is obviously fundamental, layering a separate uncapped indemnification claim on top of the Note's own default remedies (acceleration, default interest, costs of collection under Seller Note § 7) gives Seller a redundant and potentially more aggressive remedy.

**Recommendation:** (a) Delete § 9.3(c) or limit it to willful non-payment only; (b) if retained, clarify that the Note's own remedies (acceleration, default rate) are the exclusive remedy for non-payment of the Note, and § 9.3(c) applies only to the Side Letter obligations; (c) in the Side Letter, consider capping the indemnification or providing that it is subject to the Company's insurance proceeds first (consistent with SPA § 9.5(e)).

## 5. Guaranty Side Letter: Guarantor Members Not Identified

**Documents:** Guaranty Side Letter, Annex A

**Issue:** Annex A lists four Guaranteed Contracts and the form of each Guaranty, but the "Guarantor Member(s)" column contains only the placeholder "[Member name]" for all entries. The actual identities of the individuals who gave the personal guaranties are not stated.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Peinol is agreeing in § 2.1 to indemnify "each Guarantor Member" and in § 3.1 to use "commercially reasonable efforts to obtain from each counterparty to a Guaranteed Contract a written release of each Guarantor Member." Without knowing who the Guarantor Members are, Peinol cannot: (a) assess the scope of its indemnification obligation; (b) verify that the individuals listed are actually members of Seller; (c) confirm that the guaranties described match the actual instruments; or (d) effectively pursue releases. Section 4 makes each Guarantor Member an express third-party beneficiary who "may enforce [Sections 2 and 3.3] directly against Buyer"—but a person who is not identified cannot be a beneficiary.

**Recommendation:** Require Seller to complete Annex A with the full legal names of each Guarantor Member before signing. Add a representation from Seller that the individuals named are in fact members of Seller and that the described guaranties are true and complete. Consider adding a covenant that Seller will notify Peinol promptly if any Guarantor Member's status changes (e.g., death, withdrawal from Seller).

## 6. Missing Schedule 4.11 (Permits)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule 4.6, Part A (second paragraph); SPA § 4.11

**Issue:** Disclosure Schedule 4.6, Part A states that following Closing, the Company will "update its commercial feed licenses with the Wisconsin Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection and the **other state feed control officials listed in Schedule 4.11**." However, the Disclosure Schedule contains no Schedule 4.11. The schedules included are 4.6, 4.8, 4.10, 4.16, 4.17, and 4.18 only.

SPA § 4.11 represents that the Company "holds all Permits material to the conduct of its business, including all registrations and licenses required by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the U.S. Department of Agriculture and state feed control officials." Without a schedule identifying which specific permits are held (and in which states), the representation is unqualified by any disclosure, and Peinol has no independent verification of the permit inventory.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** If the Company operates in multiple states (the Disclosure Schedule references a distribution agreement covering the "Upper Midwest" and a warehouse in Oshkosh, Wisconsin), it likely holds feed licenses in several states. A missing schedule means Peinol cannot confirm the scope of regulatory approvals or identify which jurisdictions require post-closing notifications. If a permit is missing or lapsed, the Company could face regulatory action, and Peinol's ability to claim indemnification is undermined by the absence of a baseline disclosure.

**Recommendation:** Require Seller to deliver a completed Schedule 4.11 listing all Permits (FDA registration number, state feed licenses, USDA approvals, and any other regulatory authorizations) before or at signing. The FDA registration number is also redacted in Schedule 4.6 ("FDA Registration No. [●]")—require that to be completed.

## 7. Governing Law and Jurisdiction Mismatch Among Transaction Documents

**Documents:** SPA § 11.7–11.8; Seller Note § 12.1–12.2; Escrow Agreement § 12.1–12.2; Guaranty Side Letter § 5.4

**Issue:** The SPA, Seller Note, and Guaranty Side Letter are all governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The Escrow Agreement is governed by **Delaware law** with exclusive jurisdiction in the **Court of Chancery of Delaware** (or the Superior Court / District of Delaware as fallback).

**Why it matters for Peinol:** A dispute involving the escrow (e.g., whether a claim was timely filed, whether the Escrow Agent should disburse) will be litigated in Delaware under Delaware law, while the underlying indemnification claim (e.g., whether a representation was breached) will be litigated in New York under New York law. The Escrow Agent's disbursement decisions under the Escrow Agreement may turn on interpretations of SPA definitions (e.g., "Losses," "Fundamental Representations") that would be construed differently under Delaware vs. New York law. This creates the risk of inconsistent rulings and forces Peinol to litigate in two forums.

**Recommendation:** Amend Escrow Agreement § 12.1 to New York law and § 12.2 to New York County jurisdiction, conforming to the SPA and Note. If the Escrow Agent (Cobalt Harbor Trust Company, N.A.) requires Delaware governing law as a condition of its engagement, add a provision that the Escrow Agent shall apply New York law to any question of interpretation of SPA-defined terms.

## 8. Seller Note § 5.3(b): Defective Cross-Reference to "Deemed Acceptance"

**Documents:** Seller Note § 5.3(b); SPA § 9.4; Escrow Agreement § 5.3

**Issue:** Seller Note § 5.3(b) permits set-off for amounts that "are deemed accepted by Holder under the claims procedures of Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement." However, SPA § 9.4 contains no deemed-acceptance mechanism. It provides only for (a) notice of claims and (b) third-party claim defense procedures. The deemed-acceptance provision (no Objection Notice within 30 days = claim deemed accepted) is in **Escrow Agreement § 5.3**, which applies only to claims against the escrow, not to set-off against the Note.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** As drafted, the only reliable paths to set-off under Seller Note § 5.3 are (a) Seller's express written agreement or (c) a final, non-appealable court judgment. Path (b) is effectively unavailable because the referenced procedure does not exist in the referenced section. This means Peinol cannot set off against the Note based on a claim that Seller simply failed to respond to—Peinol would need to litigate to a final judgment first. Given the 6-month escrow release and the narrow "Losses" definition in the Note, this makes the set-off remedy largely theoretical.

**Recommendation:** Amend Seller Note § 5.3(b) to reference "the claims procedures of Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement **or Section 5.3 of the Escrow Agreement**," or add a standalone deemed-acceptance provision to the Note (e.g., if Seller fails to respond to a set-off notice within 30 days, the amount is deemed accepted for set-off purposes).

## 10. Product Safety Risk Concentration: Short Survival + Narrow Set-Off + Limited Escrow

**Documents:** SPA § 4.14; SPA § 9.1; SPA § 10.1 ("Losses"); Seller Note § 5.2; Escrow Agreement § 4.1–4.2; LOI § 4.1

**Issue:** This is a composite risk. The pet food business presents a specific hazard profile—contamination events (Salmonella, Listeria, aflatoxin) can cause recalls that surface well after the sale, and the financial impact can be severe (product destruction, customer chargebacks, regulatory penalties, brand damage). The transaction documents, as finalized, create a structural gap for this risk:

- **Survival:** 6 months (SPA § 9.1 tied to Escrow Release Date in Escrow Agreement § 4.1), vs. 24 months contemplated in the LOI.
- **Escrow:** $4.2M, released at 6 months (Escrow Agreement § 4.2).
- **Set-off:** Recall costs expressly excluded from the Note's "Losses" definition (Seller Note § 5.2).
- **Direct recovery:** Available but requires litigating against Seller, whose post-closing financial capacity is unsecured.

The SPA § 10.1 definition of "Losses" (which includes recall costs) governs the indemnification claim itself, but the recovery mechanisms are all constrained. Peinol could have a valid $10M+ recall claim under § 9.2(a), but: the escrow is gone after 6 months; set-off against the Note is unavailable for recall costs; and direct recovery depends on Seller's solvency.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** This is the single most significant economic risk in the transaction. A major recall at a pet food company can cost tens of millions (product destruction, logistics, customer credits, regulatory fines, litigation). The current structure leaves Peinol largely self-insured for this risk after month 6.

**Recommendation:** This issue is addressed by the recommendations in Issues 1 (extend survival), 3 (broaden set-off "Losses"), and 8 (fix deemed acceptance). Additionally: (a) consider increasing the escrow amount or extending the Escrow Release Date for § 4.14 claims specifically; (b) require Seller to maintain a minimum net worth or provide additional security for the tail period; (c) confirm that the Company's product contamination and recall insurance ($5M aggregate per Disclosure Schedule 4.18) will be renewed post-closing and that SPA § 9.5(e) (insurance offset) will apply.
