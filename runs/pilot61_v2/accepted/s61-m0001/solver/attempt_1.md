## 1. Survival Period Reduced from 18/24 Months to Six Months

**Documents:** Stock Purchase Agreement § 9.1; Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.1; Letter of Intent § 4.1; Prior Draft SPA § 9.1

**Issue.** The final SPA provides that "[a]ll other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement shall survive until the Escrow Release Date" (SPA § 9.1). The Escrow Release Date is defined as six months after the Closing Date (Escrow Agreement § 4.1). By contrast, the LOI called for 18 months for general representations and 24 months for the product safety and recall representations in Section 4.14 (LOI § 4.1). The prior draft, which was prepared by our own firm (header: "Buyer's Counsel Draft of February 18, 2026"), tracked the LOI: Section 4.14 reps survived 24 months; all other non-fundamental reps survived 18 months (Prior Draft § 9.1).

**Why it matters for Peinol.** Ostbrook Kibble is a pet food manufacturer. The principal risks—Salmonella or Listeria contamination, aflatoxin, misbranding, regulatory inspection findings, customer chargebacks, and product recalls—frequently surface well after closing. A six-month survival period means that if a contamination event is discovered in month seven, Peinol has no indemnification remedy against Seller for breach of the Section 4.14 product safety representation. The LOI and our own prior draft both recognized this by providing 24 months for those specific reps. The final SPA appears to have inadvertently (or adversely) tied all non-fundamental survival to the escrow release date rather than to a fixed period.

**Recommendation.** Restore the prior draft's survival structure in SPA § 9.1: Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations; Section 4.14 (Product Safety and Recalls) survives 24 months after the Closing Date; all other non-fundamental representations survive 18 months after the Closing Date. The Escrow Release Date should remain the date on which undisputed escrow funds are released to Seller, but it should not define the outer limit of indemnification rights. Peinol's right to set off against the Seller Note (SPA § 9.6(b)) and to seek direct indemnification from Seller (SPA § 9.6(c)) should remain available for the full survival period, not just while the escrow is funded.

## 2. Pawford Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Schedule 4.6

**Documents:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6 (Parts A and B); Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8 Part A, Item 1(d); Stock Purchase Agreement §§ 4.5(c), 4.6, 7.2(c)

**Issue.** The Disclosure Schedule states that no third-party consent is required (Schedule 4.6, Part B: "None"). However, Schedule 4.8 Part A, Item 1(d) discloses that the Pawford Agreement—the Company's largest customer at approximately $31.6 million in annual purchases (roughly 37.5% of revenue)—permits Pawford to terminate on 60 days' notice if more than 50% of the Company's voting equity transfers without Pawford's prior written consent. The transaction is a 100% change of control.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** Two problems arise. First, Seller's representation in SPA § 4.6 ("no consent … is required … except as set forth on Schedule 4.6") is inaccurate as delivered, because the Pawford consent is in fact required but not disclosed on Schedule 4.6. Second, SPA § 7.2(c) makes it a condition to Buyer's closing obligation only that "each of the consents listed on Schedule 4.6 shall have been obtained." Because Pawford's consent is not listed, it is not a closing condition. Peinol could close the transaction without having obtained Pawford's consent, and Pawford would then have an immediate right to terminate, eliminating the Company's largest revenue stream.

**Recommendation.** Require Seller to add the Pawford consent to Schedule 4.6, Part B before signing. This will (a) make the representation in § 4.6 accurate, and (b) make Pawford's consent a condition to closing under § 7.2(c), giving Peinol the right to walk if the consent is not obtained. As a practical matter, Peinol should also seek to obtain the Pawford consent as a closing deliverable under § 3.1(f). If Seller resists listing the consent (perhaps because Pawford has not yet been asked), Peinol should not close without it.

## 3. Seller Note Set-Off "Losses" Definition Excludes Recall Costs

**Documents:** Subordinated Seller Note § 5.2; Stock Purchase Agreement § 10.1 (definition of "Losses"); Stock Purchase Agreement § 11.9

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Losses" broadly to include "all costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery, customer chargebacks and credits, product destruction and disposal, and regulatory compliance and remediation" (SPA § 10.1). The Seller Note, however, defines "Losses" for set-off purposes as "out-of-pocket damages, liabilities, judgments, fines, penalties and reasonable attorneys' fees actually paid by a Buyer Indemnitee to a third party, **excluding costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery**, and excluding any diminution in value, lost profits, or multiple-of-earnings or similar valuation-based damages" (Note § 5.2). SPA § 11.9 provides that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to its payment, subordination and set-off terms."

**Why it matters for Peinol.** The set-off right against the $12 million Seller Note is Peinol's second source of recovery after the escrow (SPA § 9.6). Because the Note controls set-off terms, the narrower definition in Note § 5.2 applies. This means that if a product recall occurs after closing and Seller is liable under the Section 4.14 representation, Peinol can recover from the escrow (if funds remain) and directly from Seller, but it **cannot** set off the recall costs against the Note. For a pet food company, recall costs are the single largest potential indemnification exposure. This provision effectively strips Peinol of its most practical collection mechanism for the most likely type of claim.

**Recommendation.** Amend Note § 5.2 to conform to the SPA's definition of "Losses." At minimum, delete the express exclusion of "costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery." If Seller's counsel resists on the ground that the senior lenders required the narrower definition, consider whether the senior lenders' concern (preventing set-off from reducing cash available to service senior debt) can be addressed through the subordination mechanics in Note § 6 rather than through a substantive exclusion of recall costs from the set-off definition. Note that § 6.9 already provides that a set-off "is not a payment to Holder for purposes of this Section 6," so the senior lenders' subordination protection is already preserved.

## 4. Uncapped Buyer Exposure Under the Guaranty Side Letter

**Documents:** Guaranty Side Letter §§ 2.1, 2.3; Stock Purchase Agreement §§ 9.3(c), 9.5(a)–(d), 10.1 (definition of "Buyer Fundamental Obligations")

**Issue.** The Side Letter requires Buyer to indemnify Seller and the Guarantor Members for all Losses arising from the Guaranteed Obligations (Side Letter § 2.1). Section 2.3 designates this obligation as a "Buyer Fundamental Obligation." Under the SPA, Buyer Fundamental Obligations are (a) carved out from the indemnification cap of $8.4 million (SPA § 9.5(d): "Those limitations shall not apply to Buyer Fundamental Obligations") and (b) subject to Buyer's indemnification obligation in SPA § 9.3(c). The guaranteed contracts include the equipment lease (~$1.38M/year), the protein supply agreement (~$7.8M/year with take-or-pay minimums), the grain supply agreement (~$5.1M/year), and the premix supply agreement (~$1.95M/year with minimum-volume commitments). Total annual contract value is approximately $16.2 million, and the remaining terms extend to 2030 for the equipment lease.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** If the Company defaults on any of these contracts after closing, the Guarantor Members can demand indemnification from Buyer for the full amount of their exposure—potentially tens of millions of dollars over the remaining contract terms—with no cap and no basket. This is an unbounded contingent liability that was not present in the LOI (which merely stated that the parties "would address" the guaranties "on terms to be negotiated," LOI § 8) and was not flagged in the prior draft.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate a cap on Buyer's indemnification under the Side Letter, tied to the remaining undischarged obligations under the Guaranteed Contracts as of the Closing Date (or a fixed dollar amount). Alternatively, require that the releases be obtained as a condition to closing (or within a short post-closing period), with the indemnity applying only to obligations that arise before a release is obtained. At minimum, clarify that the indemnity does not cover obligations that the Company voluntarily incurs after closing in the ordinary course (i.e., limit it to pre-existing contract obligations that accelerate or are demanded upon a change of control).

## 5. Escrow Agreement Governed by Delaware Law; SPA and All Other Documents Governed by New York Law

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 12.1 (Delaware); Stock Purchase Agreement § 11.7 (New York); Subordinated Seller Note § 12.1 (New York); Guaranty Side Letter § 5.4 (New York)

**Issue.** The Escrow Agreement is governed by Delaware law and subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of Delaware (Escrow Agreement §§ 12.1–12.2). Every other transaction document is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** A dispute over whether a Claim Notice is valid, whether a claim falls within the scope of Article IX, or whether the Escrow Release Date has been properly calculated will implicate both the SPA (New York law) and the Escrow Agreement (Delaware law). The Court of Chancery is a court of limited jurisdiction (primarily equity and corporate governance matters); it may decline to hear a dispute that is essentially a contract interpretation question under the SPA. The fallback provision (Superior Court or federal court in Delaware) creates a second forum. This split increases litigation cost and creates the risk of inconsistent interpretations of the same defined terms (e.g., "Losses," "Material Adverse Effect") across two bodies of law.

**Recommendation.** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and jurisdiction to New York, consistent with the SPA, the Seller Note, and the Side Letter. If the Escrow Agent (Cobalt Harbor Trust Company, N.A.) insists on Delaware law for internal policy reasons, at minimum add a provision stating that in interpreting the scope of indemnifiable claims, the Escrow Agent shall apply the SPA's definitions and New York law as the substantive standard, with Delaware law governing only the ministerial mechanics of the escrow.

## 6. New Buyer Indemnification for Breach of Buyer Fundamental Obligations

**Documents:** Stock Purchase Agreement § 9.3(c); Prior Draft SPA § 9.3 (no subsection (c)); Stock Purchase Agreement § 9.5(d) (second sentence)

**Issue.** The final SPA adds § 9.3(c), under which Buyer indemnifies Seller for "any breach or non-performance of any Buyer Fundamental Obligation." The prior draft had no such provision. Additionally, § 9.5(d) in the final SPA adds the sentence: "Those limitations shall not apply to Buyer Fundamental Obligations." This means the $8.4 million cap and $420,000 basket do not protect Buyer against Seller's indemnification claims for non-payment of the Purchase Price or the Seller Note.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** While it is obvious that Buyer must pay the Note (it is a direct obligation), the indemnification provision creates a separate, uncapped cause of action for Seller. If a dispute arises over whether a set-off was properly exercised (see Issue 3) or whether a payment was "prohibited" under the subordination provisions (Note § 6), Seller could argue that Buyer breached a Buyer Fundamental Obligation and claim uncapped indemnification on top of the underlying payment obligation. This gives Seller an additional lever in any payment dispute.

**Recommendation.** Delete § 9.3(c) and the second sentence of § 9.5(d). Buyer's obligation to pay the Purchase Price and the Seller Note is already a direct contractual obligation enforceable by Seller; it does not need to be restated as an indemnification trigger. If Seller insists on the provision for the benefit of its senior lenders' comfort, limit it to willful non-payment and make it subject to the same cap and basket as other indemnification claims.

## 7. Seller Note Set-Off Cross-Reference to Inoperative Deemed Acceptance Provision

**Documents:** Subordinated Seller Note § 5.3(b); Stock Purchase Agreement § 9.4; Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 5.3

**Issue.** Note § 5.3(b) permits set-off of an amount that "is deemed accepted by Holder under the claims procedures of Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement." However, SPA § 9.4 contains no deemed acceptance mechanism. The deemed acceptance provision is in the Escrow Agreement § 5.3 (if Seller does not deliver an Objection Notice within 30 days, the claim is deemed accepted and the Escrow Agent disburses to Buyer). The Note's cross-reference is therefore inoperative as drafted.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** In practice, this means Peinol may not be able to set off a claim that Seller simply ignored (did not agree to and did not contest) unless Peinol can point to a valid deemed acceptance under some other provision. The ambiguity could allow Seller to argue that the set-off conditions in Note § 5.3 were not met, blocking Peinol from using the Note as a collection mechanism for uncontested claims.

**Recommendation.** Amend Note § 5.3(b) to reference the correct provision: "is deemed accepted by Holder under Section 5.3 of the Escrow Agreement" or add a standalone deemed acceptance provision to the Note itself (e.g., "if Holder does not dispute the claim in writing within 30 days after receiving Maker's set-off notice under Section 5.5, the claim is deemed accepted").

## 8. SPA § 9.6(b) Now Subordinates Set-Off to the Seller Note's Terms

**Documents:** Stock Purchase Agreement § 9.6(b); Prior Draft SPA § 9.6(b)

**Issue.** The final SPA provides that set-off is exercised "in accordance with the Seller Note" (SPA § 9.6(b)). The prior draft had no such qualifier—it simply stated "by set-off against principal and accrued interest otherwise payable under the Seller Note." Combined with SPA § 11.9 (which gives the Note control over "set-off terms"), this means every procedural and substantive limitation in Note § 5 (narrow "Losses" definition, three-prong determination requirement, withholding limits) applies to the set-off right.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** This is the structural provision that makes Issue 3 (recall cost exclusion) and Issue 7 (inoperative cross-reference) operative. Without the "in accordance with the Seller Note" language, the SPA's broader definition of "Losses" and its general indemnification framework would govern the set-off. The addition of this phrase, together with § 11.9, effectively imports all of the Note's limitations into the SPA's indemnification recovery mechanism.

**Recommendation.** If Issues 3 and 7 are resolved by amending the Note, this provision can remain. If the Note is not amended, consider deleting "in accordance with the Seller Note" from § 9.6(b) and instead providing that set-off is exercised in accordance with the SPA's Article IX, with the Note's subordination provisions (Section 6) being the only Note terms that apply.

## 9. Target Working Capital Was a Placeholder in the Prior Draft—Confirm It Was Properly Set

**Documents:** Prior Draft SPA § 10.1 ("Target Working Capital" means $[●], with buyer's note "to be set following completion of Buyer's financial due diligence"); Final SPA § 10.1 ("Target Working Capital" means $9,750,000)

**Issue.** The prior draft left the Target Working Capital as a placeholder. The final SPA sets it at $9,750,000. I cannot verify from the documents in the folder whether this figure was derived from Buyer's financial due diligence or whether it was negotiated. The Closing Cash Payment of $67,800,000 is calculated as the Purchase Price minus the Note and Escrow, and is then adjusted up or down based on the difference between actual Closing Working Capital and the Target.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** If the Target is set too low, Peinol overpays at closing (the Estimated Adjustment increases the cash payment when actual working capital exceeds the target). If set too high, Peinol underpays and may owe Seller a true-up after closing. The $9,750,000 figure should be validated against the Company's historical working capital levels in the Financial Statements (referenced in SPA § 4.7 but not included in the folder).

**Recommendation.** Confirm with the deal team that $9,750,000 was set based on Buyer's financial due diligence and is consistent with the Company's trailing 12-month average working capital. If the audited FY2025 financials show a materially different level, the Target should be revisited before signing.

## 10. Insurance Policy Timing Risk

**Documents:** Seller Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.18; Stock Purchase Agreement § 8.1(b)

**Issue.** Most of the Company's insurance policies (CGL, Umbrella, Product Contamination and Recall, Commercial Property, Auto, D&O) expire on July 1, 2026. The outside date for closing is June 30, 2026 (SPA § 8.1(b)). This leaves no margin: if closing occurs on June 30, the policies are in effect for only one day after closing before they expire. SPA § 6.1(b) requires Seller to maintain insurance until closing, but says nothing about post-closing renewal.

**Why it matters for Peinol.** The Product Contamination and Recall policy ($5 million aggregate, $100,000 retention) is critical for a pet food company. If it lapses on July 1 and a recall event occurs in early July, the Company would have no coverage. SPA § 9.5(e) provides that Losses are calculated net of insurance proceeds actually received—so a coverage gap directly increases Peinol's out-of-pocket exposure.

**Recommendation.** Add a closing condition or post-closing covenant requiring that the Company's product contamination and recall insurance (and the CGL/umbrella tower) be renewed or replaced before the current policies expire. Alternatively, require Seller to deliver evidence of bound renewal coverage as a closing deliverable under § 3.1.
