## Issue 1: Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations Dramatically Shortened

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 4.1; LOI § 4.1; Prior Draft SPA § 9.1

**Description:** The final SPA § 9.1 provides that "[a]ll other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement shall survive until the Escrow Release Date." The Escrow Release Date is defined in Escrow Agreement § 4.1 as **six (6) months after the Closing Date**.

By contrast:
- LOI § 4.1 provided for **18 months** survival for general reps and **24 months** for product safety and recall reps (§ 4.14).
- Prior Draft SPA § 9.1 implemented those periods: "The representations and warranties in Section 4.14 shall survive the Closing until 24 months after the Closing Date. All other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement shall survive the Closing until 18 months after the Closing Date."

**Why it matters for Peinol:** For a pet food manufacturer, six months is an inadequate period to discover latent product safety issues (e.g., contamination that manifests in consumers weeks or months after purchase), tax exposures from prior periods, or environmental problems. The product safety rep (§ 4.14) was specifically negotiated for a 24-month tail in the LOI and our own prior draft. Collapsing it to six months eliminates Peinol's indemnification recourse for the most material category of risk in this deal. After six months, Peinol would have no escrow, no survival for most reps, and would be limited to Fundamental Representations (which do not include product safety) and covenants.

**Recommendation:** Restore the survival periods from the Prior Draft: 18 months for general reps and 24 months for § 4.14 (product safety and recalls). Alternatively, if Seller insists on a shorter escrow period, decouple the survival period from the Escrow Release Date and provide for a longer survival with recovery directly from Seller (and via set-off under the Seller Note) after the escrow is released.

## Issue 2: Seller Note Set-Off "Losses" Definition Excludes Product Recall Costs and Other Key Damages

**Documents:** Seller Note § 5.2; SPA § 10.1 (definition of "Losses"); SPA § 11.9; LOI § 2.3

**Description:** The SPA defines "Losses" broadly (SPA § 10.1): "all losses, damages, liabilities, deficiencies, judgments, fines, penalties, costs and expenses, including reasonable attorneys' and consultants' fees. Losses include all costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery, customer chargebacks and credits, product destruction and disposal, and regulatory compliance and remediation."

However, Seller Note § 5.2 redefines "Losses" for set-off purposes as only: "out-of-pocket damages, liabilities, judgments, fines, penalties and reasonable attorneys' fees **actually paid by a Buyer Indemnitee to a third party**, **excluding costs of any product recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery**, and **excluding any diminution in value, lost profits, or multiple-of-earnings or similar valuation-based damages**."

SPA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence) provides that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to its payment, subordination and set-off terms." The LOI § 2.3 contemplated that "Buyer would have the right to set off amounts finally determined to be owed to Buyer under the indemnification provisions of the Definitive Agreement against amounts payable under the Seller Note"—i.e., the full scope of indemnifiable losses.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Product recalls are the single greatest financial risk in the pet food industry. If a recall occurs post-closing, Peinol can claim indemnification from Seller under SPA Article IX (which includes recall costs in "Losses"), but it **cannot** set off those recall costs against the $12 million Seller Note. Peinol's recovery would be limited to (a) the $4.2 million escrow (which releases in 6 months) and (b) a direct claim against Seller. Given that Seller is a family holding company that will have received $67.8 million in cash at closing, collecting a direct judgment may be difficult. The exclusion of diminution in value and lost profits further narrows the set-off to a small fraction of actual economic harm.

**Recommendation:** Amend Seller Note § 5.2 to use the SPA's definition of "Losses" (or at minimum to include product recall, market withdrawal, and stock recovery costs). If Seller resists, consider whether the SPA § 11.9 carve-out for the Seller Note's "set-off terms" can be narrowed so that the SPA's "Losses" definition controls for set-off purposes.

## Issue 3: Pawford Change-of-Control Consent Not Disclosed; Closing Condition Gap

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6 (Part B); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8 (Part A, Item 1(d)); SPA § 7.2(c); SPA § 3.1(f)

**Description:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6, Part B states: "None. No consent of any third party is required in connection with the execution, delivery and performance by Seller and the Company of the Agreement or the consummation of the Transactions."

However, Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8, Part A, Item 1(d) discloses that the Pawford Agreement (the Company's largest customer, with approximately $31,600,000 in annual purchases in FY2025) provides: "Pawford may terminate the Pawford Agreement on 60 days' written notice if any transfer of more than 50% of the voting equity of the Company occurs, whether directly or indirectly and whether in one transaction or a series of related transactions, **without Pawford's prior written consent**."

The transaction transfers 100% of the Shares. Pawford's prior written consent is therefore required to avoid giving Pawford an immediate termination right.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Pawford represents roughly 37% of the Company's disclosed customer revenue. If Pawford terminates post-closing, the revenue loss would likely constitute a Material Adverse Effect and would devastate the deal economics. Because Schedule 4.6 lists no consents, SPA § 7.2(c) (which conditions Closing on "each of the consents listed on Schedule 4.6" being obtained) is technically satisfied without Pawford's consent. SPA § 3.1(f) (requiring delivery of "the third-party consents listed on Schedule 4.6") is likewise empty. Peinol could close the transaction without Pawford's consent and face an immediate termination notice.

Additionally, the failure to disclose the Pawford consent requirement on Schedule 4.6 is itself a breach of Seller's representation in SPA § 4.6, which could give Peinol a post-closing indemnification claim—but only if the survival period (see Issue 1) is long enough and the claim fits within the cap.

**Recommendation:** (1) Require Seller to add the Pawford consent to Schedule 4.6 before signing. (2) Make Pawford's written consent (or a written confirmation that it will not exercise its termination right) a condition to Closing under SPA § 7.2 and a required delivery under SPA § 3.1(f). (3) Consider requiring the consent as a condition in the form of a joinder or estoppel certificate from Pawford.

## Issue 4: No Financing Condition in Final SPA

**Documents:** SPA Article VII (Conditions to Closing); LOI § 5(e); SPA § 5.4

**Description:** LOI § 5(e) provided that Closing would be subject to "Buyer's receipt of its senior acquisition financing on terms satisfactory to Buyer." The final SPA contains no financing condition. SPA § 7.2 lists six conditions to Buyer's obligations (reps bring-down, covenant performance, consents, officer certificate, no MAE, Seller deliveries), and § 7.3 lists three conditions to Seller's obligations. None references Buyer's financing.

Meanwhile, SPA § 5.4 contains a representation by Buyer that "At the Closing, Buyer will have sufficient immediately available funds to pay the Closing Cash Payment and the Indemnity Escrow Amount."

**Why it matters for Peinol:** Peinol is obligated to close and to pay $67.8 million in cash (plus or minus the working capital adjustment) and $4.2 million into escrow regardless of whether its senior acquisition financing is in place. If the financing falls through or is delayed past the June 30, 2026 outside date (SPA § 8.1(b)), Peinol faces either (a) specific performance or damages for failure to close, or (b) a breach of the § 5.4 sufficiency-of-funds representation (which is not a Fundamental Representation, so it would be subject to the cap and basket—though the basket is only $420,000). The Board Resolutions § 3.1 authorize entry into a senior credit agreement "on or about the Closing Date," confirming that the financing is not yet committed.

**Recommendation:** Add a financing condition to SPA § 7.2 (e.g., "Buyer shall have received a commitment for, and funding under, the Senior Credit Agreement on terms not materially adverse to Buyer"). If Seller resists a full "satisfactory to Buyer" standard (which is buyer-favorable), a condition that the financing is "in place and capable of funding at Closing" should be acceptable. At minimum, the outside date should be extended to provide adequate time to close financing.

## Issue 5: Guaranty Side Letter Creates Uncapped, Open-Ended Indemnification Exposure

**Documents:** Guaranty Side Letter §§ 2.1, 2.3; SPA § 9.5(d); SPA § 10.1 (definition of "Buyer Fundamental Obligations"); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8 (Part B, Items 1–4; Part C, Items 2–5)

**Description:** Side Letter § 2.1 requires Buyer to indemnify Seller and each Guarantor Member for "all Losses incurred by any of them arising out of or relating to the Guaranteed Obligations or any Guaranty." Section 2.3 designates this obligation as a "Buyer Fundamental Obligation." Under SPA § 9.5(d) (final version), the cap and basket "shall not apply to Buyer Fundamental Obligations."

The Guaranteed Contracts (per Side Letter Annex A and Disclosure Schedule Schedule 4.8) include:
- Master Equipment Lease: ~$1,380,000 annual rent (term to 2027–2030)
- Protein Meal and Rendered Fat Supply: ~$7,800,000 annual purchases (term to Dec. 2026)
- Grain Supply: ~$5,100,000 annual purchases (year-to-year)
- Premix Supply: ~$1,950,000 annual purchases (term to May 2026, with renewal option)

Total annual guaranteed obligations: approximately **$16.2 million**.

Side Letter § 3.1 requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain releases but does not guarantee they will be obtained. If a counterparty refuses to release the guarantors, Buyer's uncapped indemnification continues for the life of the underlying contract.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** This is an uncapped, potentially multi-year exposure. If the Company defaults on any of these contracts (e.g., fails to meet take-or-pay minimums under the Eastmere protein supply agreement), the guarantor members can demand indemnification from Peinol for the full amount. Because it is a Buyer Fundamental Obligation, it is not subject to the $8.4 million cap or the $420,000 basket. The exposure could easily exceed the entire Purchase Price in a worst-case scenario (e.g., simultaneous defaults on multiple supply contracts).

**Recommendation:** (1) Cap Buyer's aggregate indemnification obligation under the Side Letter (e.g., at the outstanding principal/rent balance of the Guaranteed Contracts as of the Closing Date). (2) Add a sunset date (e.g., the Side Letter indemnity terminates when all Guaranties are released or expire). (3) Require that releases be obtained as a condition to Closing, or at minimum that Seller use its relationships to facilitate releases pre-closing. (4) Confirm whether the Side Letter indemnity should be subject to the same insurance offset as SPA Article IX (currently it is not expressly addressed).

## Issue 6: New Buyer Indemnification for Buyer Fundamental Obligations (Not in Prior Draft)

**Documents:** SPA § 9.3(c); SPA § 9.5(d); Prior Draft SPA § 9.3

**Description:** The final SPA § 9.3 adds subsection (c): "any breach or non-performance of any Buyer Fundamental Obligation." The Prior Draft § 9.3 contained only (a) rep breaches and (b) covenant breaches. Additionally, final SPA § 9.5(d) adds: "Those limitations shall not apply to Buyer Fundamental Obligations," confirming that the cap and basket do not protect Buyer for these claims.

"Buyer Fundamental Obligations" (SPA § 10.1) include: (a) payment of the Purchase Price including under the Seller Note; (b) payment under the working capital adjustment (§ 2.5(d)); and (c) any obligation in an Ancillary Agreement expressly designated as a Buyer Fundamental Obligation (which now includes the Side Letter indemnity per Issue 5).

**Why it matters for Peinol:** This creates a separate indemnification pathway for Seller to recover from Buyer for non-payment, in addition to Seller's ordinary contractual rights under the Seller Note (acceleration, default interest, etc.). While it seems fair that Seller should be able to recover if Buyer doesn't pay, the uncapped nature (no cap, no basket) combined with the broad definition of Buyer Fundamental Obligations (which now sweeps in the Side Letter) means any dispute about whether an obligation is a "Buyer Fundamental Obligation" could expose Peinol to uncapped indemnification.

**Recommendation:** (1) Confirm that § 9.3(c) is limited to actual non-payment (not, e.g., a disputed working capital adjustment that is later resolved in Buyer's favor). (2) Ensure the definition of "Buyer Fundamental Obligations" does not inadvertently capture obligations from the Side Letter that should have their own cap (see Issue 5). (3) Consider whether the § 9.7 exclusive remedy provision should apply to § 9.3(c) claims or whether Seller retains all remedies (including specific performance under § 11.11).

## Issue 7: Escrow Agreement Governing Law and Jurisdiction Conflict with SPA

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 12.1–12.2; SPA § 11.7–11.8; SPA § 11.9

**Description:** The Escrow Agreement is governed by Delaware law (§ 12.1) and submits disputes to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of Delaware (§ 12.2). The SPA is governed by New York law (§ 11.7) and submits disputes to the exclusive jurisdiction of state or federal courts in New York County (§ 11.8). SPA § 11.9 provides that the SPA controls over Ancillary Agreements in the event of conflict, except that the Seller Note controls on payment, subordination, and set-off terms. The Escrow Agreement's jurisdiction clause is not within the Seller Note carve-out.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** If a dispute arises that implicates both the escrow mechanics and the SPA's indemnification provisions (e.g., whether a claim was properly noticed, whether the Escrow Release Date has passed, or whether a disbursement was proper), there is a conflict over which court has jurisdiction. The SPA's order of precedence suggests New York, but the Escrow Agent (Cobalt Harbor Trust Company, N.A.) is a party only to the Escrow Agreement and may not have validly consented to New York jurisdiction. This could lead to threshold litigation over venue before the merits are addressed.

**Recommendation:** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and jurisdiction to New York (matching the SPA), or add a provision to SPA § 11.9 that carves out the Escrow Agreement's forum selection for disputes to which the Escrow Agent is a party. The cleaner solution is to change the Escrow Agreement to New York law and jurisdiction.

## Issue 8: Seller Note § 5.3 Requires Final Determination Before Set-Off (Limited Withholding Relief)

**Documents:** Seller Note § 5.3, § 5.4

**Description:** Seller Note § 5.3 permits set-off only for amounts that (a) have been agreed in writing by Holder, (b) are deemed accepted under the SPA claims procedures, or (c) have been "finally determined by a final, non-appealable judgment." Section 5.4 provides a partial mitigation: if a claim has been noticed but not resolved when a payment is due, Maker may **withhold** (not set off) an amount equal to the claimed amount, "not exceeding the payment then due."

**Why it matters for Peinol:** The withholding right in § 5.4 is limited to "the payment then due." Interest payments are quarterly (approximately $210,000 per quarter on $12M at 7%). If Peinol has a $5 million product recall claim that is disputed, it can withhold only the ~$210,000 quarterly interest payment—not the full $5 million. The withheld amount must be paid over (with interest) if the claim is resolved in Seller's favor. This provides minimal practical protection for large claims.

**Recommendation:** Expand § 5.4 to permit withholding up to the full amount of the pending claim (not just the payment then due), or permit Peinol to escrow the disputed amount with a neutral third party pending resolution. Alternatively, tie the withholding right to the escrow claims procedure so that amounts properly claimed against the escrow are also withheld from the Note.

## Issue 10: Audited Financial Statements for FY2025 May Not Be Available

**Documents:** SPA § 4.7; SPA dated March 4, 2026

**Description:** SPA § 4.7 represents that "Seller has delivered to Buyer the audited balance sheets of the Company as of December 31, 2024 and December 31, 2025, together with the related statements of income and cash flows for the years then ended." The SPA is dated March 4, 2026—only two days after the FY2025 year-end.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** A full audit of FY2025 financial statements typically requires 90–120 days after year-end. It is highly unlikely that truly *audited* FY2025 financials exist as of March 4, 2026. If the financials delivered are in fact unaudited or only reviewed, the representation in § 4.7 is inaccurate as of the signing date. While this would be a breach of a non-fundamental rep (subject to the cap and basket), it undermines the reliability of the financial baseline against which the working capital target ($9,750,000) was set.

**Recommendation:** (1) Confirm with the client whether the FY2025 financials are actually audited. If not, amend § 4.7 to state "audited" for FY2024 and "unaudited" (or "reviewed") for FY2025, with a covenant that Seller will deliver audited FY2025 financials within a specified period post-closing. (2) Confirm that the Target Working Capital was set based on the same financials and is not affected by the audit status.

## Issue 11: SPA § 9.1 Adds "Buyer Fundamental Obligations" to Covenant Survival (Redundant but Expansive)

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1; Prior Draft SPA § 9.1

**Description:** Final SPA § 9.1: "The covenants and agreements of the Parties, **and the Buyer Fundamental Obligations**, shall survive the Closing until fully performed in accordance with their terms." Prior Draft § 9.1 omitted the reference to Buyer Fundamental Obligations.

**Why it matters for Peinol:** While Buyer Fundamental Obligations are already "covenants and agreements" (and thus already survive until performed), the express addition signals intent that these obligations have a distinct, potentially longer survival. Combined with § 9.3(c) (Issue 6) and § 9.5(d) (no cap or basket), this reinforces that Seller has a perpetual, uncapped indemnification claim against Buyer for any alleged non-performance of a Buyer Fundamental Obligation. The practical risk is low (payment obligations are either performed or not), but it adds another layer of exposure.

**Recommendation:** This is a minor point. If the client is comfortable with the substance of Issues 5 and 6, this language can be accepted. If not, it should be addressed as part of the broader negotiation on Buyer Fundamental Obligations.

## Issue 12: SPA § 11.9 Order of Precedence is New and Favors the Narrower Seller Note Set-Off

**Documents:** SPA § 11.9; Prior Draft SPA (no equivalent provision)

**Description:** The Prior Draft contained no order-of-precedence clause. The final SPA § 11.9 provides: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, this Agreement shall control, **except that the Seller Note shall control with respect to its payment, subordination and set-off terms**."

**Why it matters for Peinol:** On its face, this provision favors Buyer (the SPA controls over ancillary agreements). However, the carve-out for the Seller Note's "set-off terms" specifically preserves the narrow "Losses" definition in Note § 5.2 (Issue 2). Without this carve-out, one could argue that the SPA's broader "Losses" definition should control under the general rule. The carve-out eliminates that argument.

**Recommendation:** This provision should be addressed in conjunction with Issue 2. If the Note's "Losses" definition is broadened, the carve-out becomes less problematic. If it is not broadened, consider removing "set-off terms" from the carve-out so that the SPA's definition controls.
