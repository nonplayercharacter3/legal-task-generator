## 1. Environmental Indemnity Is Gutted by a Cross-Reference Error

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 (definitions of "Facility" and "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities"); TSA § 1.1 (definition of "Facility"); SPA § 9.2(d) (indemnity for Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities).

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Facility" by cross-reference to the Transition Services Agreement. In the TSA, "Facility" means *only* Stounaisk's retained property at 2200 Sandusky Pike, Fremont, Ohio (the coating and warehousing site). "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" is then defined as Losses arising from Hazardous Materials "at or from any Facility" or any Environmental Law violation "in connection with any Facility." As drafted, the special environmental indemnity in § 9.2(d) covers *only* the Fremont, Ohio site — not the Company's own plants in Erie, Pennsylvania or Rochester, New York, which are the assets Votox is actually buying.

The LOI (§ 4(c)) expressly contemplated a "special environmental indemnity" for "conditions existing or activities conducted on or prior to the Closing Date at the Company's current and former facilities." The SPA as drafted does not deliver that.

**Why it matters.** The Erie and Rochester plants are glass-fabrication facilities with batch houses, coating lines, sealant application, and solvent use — exactly the operations that generate the Hazardous Materials enumerated in the SPA's definition (lead, arsenic, antimony, batch-house residues, coating and cleaning solvents, asbestos, PCBs). If there is a pre-Closing environmental condition at either plant, Votox would have only the general environmental representation (§ 4.15), which (as noted in Issue 2 below) survives just 90 days and is subject to the cap and deductible. The uncapped (up-to-Purchase-Price) environmental indemnity that the LOI negotiated would be unavailable.

**Recommendation.** Delete the cross-reference in SPA § 1.1 and define "Facility" in the SPA to mean "any real property currently or formerly owned, leased, or operated by the Company, including the plants in Erie, Pennsylvania and Rochester, New York, and any Facility as defined in the Transition Services Agreement." Alternatively, add a separate definition of "Company Facility" and use it in the "Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities" definition. Confirm the LOI's intent that the special indemnity covers the Company's own sites.

## 2. General Representation Survival Is 90 Days, Not 18 Months

**Where:** SPA § 9.1 (Survival); Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Release Date"); LOI § 4(b).

**Issue.** SPA § 9.1 provides that representations and warranties "shall survive the Closing until the Release Date (as defined in the Escrow Agreement)." The Escrow Agreement defines "Release Date" as *ninety (90) days after the Closing Date*. The LOI, by contrast, states that "Seller's general representations and warranties would survive Closing for 18 months after closing."

The Escrow Agreement also defines a separate "Indemnity Release Date" (18 months), which governs when the escrow funds are released to Seller. It appears the SPA drafter conflated the two dates.

**Why it matters.** Ninety days is far too short to discover many representation breaches — tax deficiencies, product warranty claims (IGU seal failures have long latent periods), undisclosed liabilities, or environmental conditions. After 90 days, Votox would have no indemnification recourse for general rep breaches even though the escrow remains funded for another 15 months. This effectively converts the 10% escrow from a security for 18 months of rep risk into a security for only 90 days.

**Recommendation.** Amend SPA § 9.1 to state that general representations and warranties survive until the *Indemnity Release Date* (18 months), not the Release Date (90 days). Reserve the 90-day Release Date for its actual purpose in the Escrow Agreement (release of the Adjustment Escrow). Confirm that the Fundamental Reps and Tax reps continue to survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations, as both the SPA and LOI already provide.

## 3. Pre-Closing Taxes Are Not Carved Out from the Cap and Deductible

**Where:** SPA § 9.5(c) (cap and deductible carve-outs); LOI § 4(d).

**Issue.** SPA § 9.5(c) provides that the cap (§ 9.5(a)) and deductible (§ 9.5(b)) do not apply to Losses arising from (i) Fraud, (ii) Fundamental Representations, or (iii) "the matters described in Section 9.2(d)" (Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities). Section 9.2(c) (Pre-Closing Taxes) is *not* listed. The LOI, however, states: "Neither the deductible nor the cap would apply to the following cap carve-outs: Fraud, fundamental representations, **pre-closing taxes**, and the special environmental indemnity."

**Why it matters.** The Company operates multistate fabrication, supply, and installation contracts. The SPA's own definition of "Pre-Closing Taxes" (§ 1.1) specifically calls out "sales, use and similar Taxes imposed by any state or locality on or with respect to the Company's fabrication, supply and installation contracts" — acknowledging this is a known risk area. If a multistate sales/use tax deficiency emerges post-Closing (e.g., economic nexus in a state where the Company installed curtain-wall systems), Votox would be subject to the $630,000 deductible and the $6.3 million cap. Given that a single multistate sales tax exposure for a glass-fabrication business with installation contracts could easily exceed $6.3 million, this is a material gap.

**Recommendation.** Add "the matters described in Section 9.2(c)" (Pre-Closing Taxes) to the list of carve-outs in SPA § 9.5(c), consistent with the LOI.

## 4. Third-Party Consent Condition Missing from SPA

**Where:** LOI § 8(c); SPA Article VIII (Conditions to Closing).

**Issue.** The LOI lists "receipt of material third-party consents" as a condition to Closing. The SPA's conditions to Buyer's obligation (§ 8.2) do not include any third-party consent condition. The SPA does require Seller to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain consents (§ 6.3), but that is an efforts covenant, not a closing condition.

**Why it matters.** The Company has Material Contracts (SPA § 4.9) that may contain change-of-control or assignment provisions requiring counterparty consent. The Kestrelline forklift lease (§ 9.1) requires Lessor consent for assignment, though a stock purchase likely does not trigger it. More critically, any of the Company's large fabrication or installation contracts (those over $500,000 per the § 4.9 threshold) may have consent requirements. Without a closing condition, Votox could be compelled to close even if a key customer or supplier has not consented, potentially losing a material contract immediately post-Closing.

**Recommendation.** Add to SPA § 8.2 a condition that "all consents, approvals or waivers required under any Material Contract in connection with the transactions contemplated hereby have been obtained and are in full force and effect." Alternatively, if the parties prefer not to make this a hard condition, at minimum add it to the "absence of Material Adverse Effect" analysis or require Seller to deliver a consent matrix as a closing deliverable.

## 5. MCA Does Not Protect Phase II Environmental Sampling

**Where:** MCA § 4.1 (Site Visits); SPA § 6.2 (Access to Information); LOI § 7 (Due Diligence).

**Issue.** MCA § 4.1 provides that no sampling of soil, groundwater, building materials or other media may be performed without Stounaisk's prior written consent, which "shall not be unreasonably withheld for a sampling plan recommended in a Phase I environmental site assessment." The "not unreasonably withheld" standard applies *only* to Phase I-recommended sampling. If the Phase I at Erie or Rochester recommends a Phase II investigation (e.g., soil gas sampling, groundwater monitoring wells), Stounaisk can withhold consent without any stated standard.

The LOI (§ 7) contemplates "Phase I (and, where warranted, Phase II) environmental site assessments." SPA § 6.2 grants access "for the purpose of conducting environmental assessments" but is subject to the MCA.

**Why it matters.** Given the Company's use of batch-house residues, coating solvents, and potential asbestos/PCBs (all enumerated in the SPA's "Hazardous Materials" definition), a Phase II is a realistic possibility. If Stounaisk can block Phase II sampling, Votox may close without full knowledge of subsurface conditions at the plants it is acquiring.

**Recommendation.** Amend MCA § 4.1 to extend the "not unreasonably withheld" standard to any sampling plan recommended in a Phase I *or* Phase II environmental site assessment. Alternatively, add language to SPA § 6.2 that Seller shall not unreasonably withhold consent to any environmental sampling recommended by Buyer's environmental consultant.

## 6. Governing Law Inconsistency: SPA (New York) vs. Escrow Agreement (Delaware)

**Where:** SPA § 11.9(a)–(b); Escrow Agreement § 11.2.

**Issue.** The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County. The Escrow Agreement is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (or Superior Court / federal District Court if Chancery lacks jurisdiction).

**Why it matters.** If a dispute arises that implicates both agreements — for example, a fight over whether a claim was properly noticed under SPA § 9.4 and whether the Escrow Agent should release funds under Escrow § 5.3 — the parties could face inconsistent procedural rules, different standards for "manifest error," and the possibility of parallel or sequential litigation in two different jurisdictions. The Escrow Agent's interpleader right (Escrow § 6.4) would be exercised in Delaware, while the underlying SPA claim would be in New York.

**Recommendation.** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and jurisdiction to New York, matching the SPA and TSA. If the other side resists (Pellucid may prefer Delaware as a bank), at minimum add a provision that any dispute between Buyer and Seller regarding the interpretation or application of the Escrow Agreement shall be governed by the SPA's choice-of-law and forum provisions.

## 7. Buyer Bears Tax on Escrow Earnings

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 10.1 (Tax Reporting); Escrow Agreement § 10.4 (Tax Distributions).

**Issue.** The Escrow Agreement provides that "Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds for income tax purposes until they are disbursed" and that "all interest and other earnings on the Escrow Funds shall be reported as income of Buyer." Section 10.4 requires the Escrow Agent to distribute 40% of quarterly earnings to Buyer (presumably to fund Buyer's tax liability), but the remaining 60% stays in the account and may ultimately be released to Seller.

**Why it matters.** Votox will receive a 1099 for interest earned on $7.05 million in escrow (the combined indemnity and adjustment amounts) and will owe income tax on that interest, even though a portion of the underlying principal may be released to Seller. The 40% quarterly distribution may not fully cover Votox's tax liability depending on its marginal rate and the timing of disbursements.

**Recommendation.** This is a standard escrow tax allocation and difficult to change unilaterally. However, consider negotiating: (a) that the Escrow Agent distribute 100% of earnings to Buyer quarterly (with Buyer accounting to Seller for Seller's share if the funds are ultimately released to Seller), or (b) a gross-up provision requiring Seller to reimburse Buyer for any tax on earnings ultimately attributable to Seller's share. At minimum, model the tax cost and confirm it is reflected in the deal economics.

## 8. Ambiguous Dividend Restriction in Conduct of Business Covenant

**Where:** SPA § 6.1 (Conduct of Business).

**Issue.** The covenant provides that without Buyer's consent, the Company shall not "declare any dividend other than cash dividends paid prior to the Closing." As drafted, this is circular: a dividend that has already been "paid prior to the Closing" has necessarily already been "declared" before Closing, so the exception covers nothing that could occur between signing and Closing. The apparent intent is to prohibit *new* dividend declarations between signing and Closing, while perhaps carving out dividends that were declared before signing but will be paid after signing.

**Why it matters.** If the Company declares a dividend between signing and Closing, Votox could argue the covenant was breached, but the ambiguous carve-out gives Seller room to argue the dividend was permissible. More practically, if the Company has a declared-but-unpaid dividend at signing, the parties need clarity on whether it reduces the Purchase Price (via the working capital or indebtedness adjustment) or is simply paid out of Company cash (reducing Closing Working Capital).

**Recommendation.** Rewrite the exception to: "other than cash dividends declared prior to the date hereof and paid in accordance with their declared terms." Confirm in the Estimated Closing Statement that any declared-but-unpaid dividends are treated as a reduction of Closing Working Capital (i.e., as a current liability).

## 9. Company's Execution of TSA Not a Specified Closing Deliverable

**Where:** SPA § 3.2 (Seller Deliverables); SPA § 6.5 (Transition Services covenant); TSA (parties are Provider and Company).

**Issue.** SPA § 6.5 obligates "Seller and the Company" to enter into the TSA at Closing. However, § 3.2 (Seller's closing deliverables) requires only that Seller deliver "the Escrow Agreement and the Transition Services Agreement, duly executed by Seller." The Company's execution of the TSA is not listed as a deliverable by either party.

**Why it matters.** The TSA is between Stounaisk (Provider) and Lumacrest (Recipient/Company). At Closing, the Company's stock transfers to Votox, so Votox (as new parent) would direct the Company to execute. But if the TSA is not a condition to Closing or a required deliverable, there is no contractual hook to compel the Company's signature. In practice this is unlikely to be disputed, but it is a drafting gap.

**Recommendation.** Add to SPA § 3.2: "(g) the Transition Services Agreement, duly executed by the Company." Alternatively, add to § 8.2 (Buyer's closing conditions): "the Transition Services Agreement having been duly executed by Seller and the Company."

## 10. Outside Date Is Blank

**Where:** SPA § 10.1(b).

**Issue.** The termination right for failure to close by the outside date references "[●], 2027" — the date has not been filled in.

**Why it matters.** Without an outside date, neither party has a defined deadline to walk away. For Votox, this cuts both ways: it preserves the option to close later if conditions are delayed, but it also means Seller could sit on the deal indefinitely while the Company's value changes.

**Recommendation.** Insert a specific outside date (e.g., 12 months from signing, or a date that aligns with any financing commitment expiry). Ensure the date is realistic given HSR filing timelines and any anticipated consent processes.

## 11. No Deadlock Mechanism for Working Capital Dispute

**Where:** SPA § 2.3(b).

**Issue.** If Buyer and Seller cannot agree on an "independent accounting firm mutually selected" to resolve a working capital dispute, the provision has no fallback. The section does not specify what happens if the parties cannot agree on the firm within a stated period.

**Why it matters.** A stalemate on the accounting firm would leave the purchase price adjustment in limbo, with the $750,000 Adjustment Escrow held indefinitely. While the Release Date (90 days) would trigger a release of the Adjustment Escrow to Seller absent a Buyer notice (Escrow § 5.1(b)), a genuine dispute that cannot be resolved for lack of a neutral party is a practical problem.

**Recommendation.** Add: "If the parties cannot agree on the selection of an independent accounting firm within fifteen (15) days after delivery of an objection, either party may request the President of the AICPA (or such other mutually acceptable body) to appoint such firm." Alternatively, name a default firm in the agreement.

## 12. Financial Statement Gap

**Where:** SPA § 4.6 (Financial Statements).

**Issue.** The most recent financial statements represented are the unaudited balance sheet as of September 30, 2026. If Closing occurs in, say, February or March 2027, there will be a 5- to 6-month gap between the last represented financials and Closing. The "Absence of Changes" rep (§ 4.8) looks back only to December 31, 2025, and the Conduct of Business covenant (§ 6.1) addresses interim operations, but there is no representation as to the financial condition at a date closer to Closing.

**Why it matters.** Votox is paying $63 million based in part on the Company's financial condition. A multi-month gap means Votox could be closing on a business that has deteriorated materially since September 30, 2026, with only the MAE condition (§ 8.2(d)) as protection — which is a high bar.

**Recommendation.** Require Seller to deliver interim financial statements (at minimum an unaudited balance sheet) as of a date no more than 30 days before Closing, and add a representation that such interim financials fairly present the Company's condition. Alternatively, tighten the MAE definition or add a specific condition that Closing Working Capital not deviate from the target by more than a stated percentage.

## 13. Kestrelline Forklift Lease – Change-of-Control and Operational Dependency

**Where:** Kestrelline Lease § 9.1 (Assignment); SPA § 4.9 (Material Contracts); SPA § 4.5 (No Conflicts; Consents).

**Issue.** The lease (§ 9.1) requires Lessor's consent for assignment but does not contain an express change-of-control provision. A stock purchase of the Lessee (Lumacrest) is not an assignment of the lease, so Lessor's consent should not be required. However, the lease is not obviously a "Material Contract" under the SPA's $250,000-per-year threshold (aggregate Monthly Rent is $11,880, or ~$142,560/year), so it may not appear on the § 4.9 Disclosure Schedule and may not be covered by the § 4.5 "No Conflicts" rep.

**Why it matters.** The Company depends on 8 forklifts across both plants for glass handling. If the lease were somehow invalidated or if Lessor asserted a technical default post-Closing, the Company's operations would be disrupted. The lease runs through approximately March 2029 (48 months from March 2025), so it will be in effect well past Closing.

**Recommendation.** Confirm in diligence that the lease is not in default and that no consent is required for the stock purchase. If it is not a Material Contract under the SPA's thresholds, consider adding it to the Disclosure Schedule voluntarily or lowering the Material Contract threshold to capture it. At minimum, ensure the § 4.5 "No Conflicts" rep covers all contracts, not just Material Contracts.

## 14. TSA Limitation of Liability May Be Inadequate for Coating Failures

**Where:** TSA § 8.1 (Limitation of Liability); TSA Schedule A (Service A-1: Low-E and reflective coating).

**Issue.** The TSA caps each party's aggregate liability at "the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement." If the monthly coating fee is, for example, $150,000, the 9-month cap would be roughly $1.35 million. A systematic coating defect (e.g., incorrect Low-E coating that fails in the field) could generate product warranty claims, customer claims, and re-coating costs far exceeding that amount.

The carve-outs from the cap are limited to gross negligence, willful misconduct, breach of the confidentiality/data provisions (Article 6), and the mutual indemnification in § 8.2 (which covers third-party claims caused by breach, negligence, or willful misconduct). A simple failure to meet the coating specifications in § 3.2 — short of gross negligence — would be subject to the cap.

**Why it matters.** Votox is relying on Stounaisk to coat glass to the Company's specifications for 9 months post-Closing. If the coating is defective and the units are already installed in buildings, the resulting warranty and liability exposure (broken lites, water infiltration, building damage) could be substantial and would be capped at a relatively modest amount.

**Recommendation.** Add to TSA § 8.1 a carve-out from the liability cap for "Losses arising from Provider's failure to perform the coating Services in accordance with Section 3.2 (Coating Specifications)." Alternatively, require Provider to maintain product liability or professional liability insurance specifically covering the coating Services, with the Company named as additional insured, and make the cap subject to the limit of such insurance.
