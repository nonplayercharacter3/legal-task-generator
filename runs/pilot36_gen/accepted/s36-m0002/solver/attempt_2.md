# MEMORANDUM

## Summary

I have reviewed the Asset Purchase Agreement (the "APA"), the agreed-form Escrow Agreement, the Key Employee Agreement, and the integration planning meeting notes. Below are the issues I identified, ordered by significance to Teivav. The three most consequential problems are: (1) a direct conflict between the APA's indemnification provisions that causes Teivav to assume uncapped pre-Closing customer contract liability; (2) a discrepancy between the APA and the Escrow Agreement on whether Excluded Liabilities are subject to the Cap, with the Escrow Agreement (which controls under APA § 11.9) imposing the Cap; and (3) the Environmental Representations surviving for only 90 days, tied to the Escrow Period, which is far too short for the types of environmental risks inherent in an ammonia-refrigerated logistics business.

---

## Issue 1 – Teivav Indemnifies Seller for Pre-Closing Customer Contract Losses (Uncapped)

**Where:** APA § 9.3(c) read against APA §§ 2.4(b) and 9.2(c); APA § 9.5(d).

**Explanation.** APA § 9.3(c) provides that Buyer (Teivav) shall indemnify Seller for all Losses arising from "the Transferred Customer Contracts, **whether arising before or after the Closing**." This language is unqualified and sweeps in pre-Closing performance failures—temperature excursions, spoiled cargo, service-level credits, chargebacks—all of which § 2.4(b) expressly identifies as Excluded Liabilities that Seller is supposed to retain and pay.

The internal conflict is direct:

- § 2.4(b) designates pre-Closing contract performance liabilities as Excluded Liabilities.
- § 9.2(c) obligates Seller to indemnify Buyer for any Excluded Liability.
- § 9.3(c) obligates Buyer to indemnify Seller for the same Transferred Customer Contracts, "whether arising before or after the Closing."
- § 9.5(d) states that Buyer's obligations under § 9.3 "shall not be subject to the Cap or any other limitation."

The practical effect is that if a customer brings a claim for a pre-Closing temperature excursion, Teivav is contractually obligated to indemnify Seller for that loss, with no cap and no basket. This is the opposite of what the Excluded Liabilities structure was supposed to accomplish.

**Why it matters.** In a refrigerated logistics business, pre-Closing cargo claims (spoilage, temperature deviations, late deliveries) are a known and recurring category of liability. Teivav is paying $42 million for the business and, under this drafting, is also taking on unlimited pre-Closing customer contract exposure. This could materially erode the value of the acquisition.

**Recommended fix.** Delete the words "whether arising before or after the Closing" from § 9.3(c) and replace with "arising from performance required after the Closing Date." Alternatively, add a carve-out: "provided that this Section 9.3(c) shall not apply to any Loss to the extent it constitutes an Excluded Liability under Section 2.4."

**Proposed language for § 9.3(c):**

> (c) the Transferred Customer Contracts, to the extent arising from performance or non-performance after the Closing Date; provided that nothing in this Section 9.3(c) shall obligate Buyer to indemnify Seller for any Excluded Liability.

## Issue 2 – Escrow Agreement Caps Excluded Liabilities, Overriding the APA

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 6.3 vs. APA § 9.5(b); APA § 11.9 (order of precedence).

**Explanation.** APA § 9.5(b) provides: "The Cap shall not apply to Losses arising from Fraud, breach of Fundamental Representations, **and Excluded Liabilities**." This means that under the APA, Excluded Liabilities (pre-Closing environmental, tax, wage, cargo, and contract performance liabilities) are uncapped.

Escrow Agreement § 6.3 provides: "The Cap shall apply to all claims under Section 9.2 of the Purchase Agreement other than claims for Fraud or breach of Fundamental Representations." The reference to Excluded Liabilities is **omitted**.

APA § 11.9 resolves the conflict: "the Escrow Agreement shall control on indemnification claims and limits." Because the Cap is a "limit," the Escrow Agreement's version governs. Under the Escrow Agreement, Excluded Liabilities are subject to the $4.2 million Cap.

**Why it matters.** Excluded Liabilities in a refrigerated logistics business can be substantial: a single ammonia release at a facility could generate seven- or eight-figure environmental remediation and third-party liability; a fleet of reefers generates ongoing cargo claims; pre-Closing payroll and tax liabilities can accumulate. Capping all of these at $4.2 million—the same amount as the escrow—means that once the escrow is exhausted, Teivav has no further contractual recourse against Seller for Excluded Liabilities, despite the APA's apparent intent to leave them uncapped.

**Recommended fix.** Amend Escrow Agreement § 6.3 to conform to APA § 9.5(b). Add "or Excluded Liabilities" to the list of carve-outs from the Cap.

**Proposed language for Escrow Agreement § 6.3 (second sentence):**

> The Cap shall apply to all claims under Section 9.2 of the Purchase Agreement other than claims for Fraud, breach of Fundamental Representations, or Excluded Liabilities.

## Issue 3 – Environmental Representations Survive Only 90 Days

**Where:** APA § 9.1(b); Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Escrow Period").

**Explanation.** APA § 9.1(b) provides: "The Environmental Representations shall survive the Closing until the expiration of the Escrow Period." The Escrow Agreement defines the Escrow Period as 90 days from the Closing Date (Escrow Agreement § 1.1). Therefore, Teivav has only 90 days post-Closing to discover and assert a claim under the Environmental Representations (APA § 4.12).

By contrast, general representations survive 12 months (APA § 9.1(a)), and Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations (APA § 9.1(c)).

**Why it matters.** The Business operates anhydrous ammonia refrigeration systems subject to EPA RMP and OSHA PSM regulations, and a fleet of reefer units subject to 40 C.F.R. Part 82, Subpart F leak-rate requirements. Environmental problems in this context—ammonia leaks, refrigerant releases, soil or groundwater contamination—frequently do not manifest or become reportable within 90 days. A slow ammonia seep, a latent refrigerant leak in a trailer that is only detected at the next scheduled inspection (which may be 6–12 months out), or a regulatory audit that identifies a historical violation can all surface well after the 90-day window. Once the Escrow Period expires, the Environmental Representations are dead, and Teivav has no indemnification claim for them.

Additionally, because the Escrow is released 90 days after Closing (Escrow Agreement § 7.1), Teivav loses its security for environmental claims at the same time the claims expire.

**Recommended fix.** Decouple the survival of Environmental Representations from the Escrow Period. Extend survival to at least 24 months, or tie it to the applicable statute of limitations for environmental claims (which in many jurisdictions is longer than general contract limitations). If Seller resists a longer survival, at minimum extend the Escrow Period to 180 days and add a specific environmental escrow holdback.

**Proposed language for APA § 9.1(b):**

> (b) The Environmental Representations shall survive the Closing until the earlier of (i) 24 months after the Closing Date, or (ii) the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations for the underlying claim.

## Issue 4 – Escrow Period (90 Days) Is Shorter Than General Rep Survival (12 Months): Security Gap

**Where:** APA § 9.1(a) (12-month survival); Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (90-day Escrow Period); Escrow Agreement § 7.1 (release at end of Escrow Period).

**Explanation.** General representations and warranties survive for 12 months post-Closing, but the escrow is released to Seller 90 days after Closing (less any Pending Claim Amount). For the 9-month gap between month 3 and month 12, Teivav has a contractual right to indemnification but no security for it. Recovery depends entirely on Seller's solvency and willingness to pay.

**Why it matters.** In a sale-of-business context, Seller may distribute the sale proceeds, wind down operations, or become difficult to locate after the escrow is released. If a material rep breach is discovered in month 6 or month 10 (e.g., a title defect in a reefer trailer, a hidden litigation, a financial statement inaccuracy), Teivav must pursue an unsecured claim against Seller. The risk of non-collection is real.

**Recommended fix.** Extend the Escrow Period to 12 months to align with the general rep survival period. If Seller resists, consider a tiered release: 50% at 90 days, 50% at 12 months.

**Proposed language for Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (Escrow Period definition):**

> "Escrow Period" means the period beginning on the Closing Date and ending at 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the date that is 12 months after the Closing Date.

## Issue 5 – No Purchase Price Adjustment for Non-Assignable Contracts

**Where:** APA § 2.7; Schedule 4.7, item 1 (Norrbeck Biologics).

**Explanation.** APA § 2.7 provides that if a Required Consent has not been obtained by Closing, the applicable Assigned Contract "shall be deemed an Excluded Asset for all purposes of this Agreement, **without adjustment to the Purchase Price**." Schedule 4.7, item 1 identifies the Norrbeck Biologics Master Temperature-Controlled Storage Agreement as a Material Contract (i.e., it generated revenue in excess of $500,000 in FY2025) for which consent is required and has not been obtained as of the signing date.

**Why it matters.** If Norrbeck Biologics consent is not obtained by Closing, Teivav pays the full $42 million but does not receive a contract that generated at least $500,000 in annual revenue. The "without adjustment" language means Teivav bears 100% of the economic loss from losing this customer. Given that the contract is for temperature-controlled storage (a core service line), the loss of this revenue stream could also have knock-on effects on facility utilization and fixed-cost absorption.

**Recommended fix.** Add a per-diem or contract-value-based purchase price reduction for each Assigned Contract that is not assigned due to a missing consent. At minimum, the reduction should equal the trailing-12-month revenue attributable to that contract.

**Proposed language for APA § 2.7 (second sentence):**

> If any consent to assignment of any Assigned Contract has not been obtained as of the Closing, such Assigned Contract shall not be assigned to Buyer at the Closing and shall be deemed an Excluded Asset for all purposes of this Agreement. The Purchase Price shall be reduced by an amount equal to the revenue generated under such Assigned Contract during the twelve (12) months preceding the Closing Date, as reflected in the financial statements delivered under Section 4.4.

## Issue 6 – Key Employee Agreement "Business" Definition Is Narrower Than the APA

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Business") vs. APA § 1.1 (definition of "Business").

**Explanation.** The APA defines "Business" as "temperature-controlled warehousing, refrigerated and frozen freight transportation, and cross-dock services." The Key Employee Agreement defines "Business" as "the operation of temperature-controlled warehouse facilities." The Key Employee Agreement's definition omits refrigerated/frozen freight transportation and cross-dock services entirely.

All of the restrictive covenants in Section 6 of the Key Employee Agreement are keyed to this narrower "Business" definition:

- § 6.1(a) (non-compete): Executive cannot engage in "the Business" — only warehouse facilities.
- § 6.1(b) (customer non-solicit): covers customers "of the Business" — only warehouse customers.
- § 6.1(c) (employee non-solicit): covers employees "engaged in the Business" — only warehouse employees.
- § 6.1(d) (vendor/landlord non-interference): covers landlords, suppliers, carriers, owner-operators "of the Business" — arguably only warehouse-related.

**Why it matters.** The Executive is the COO of Seller and will serve as SVP Operations at Teivav with a remit that explicitly includes "refrigerated fleet operations, transportation and dispatch, and cross-dock operations" (Key Employee Agreement § 2.3). He has deep relationships with carriers, owner-operators, and transportation customers. If he departs and joins a competitor in the refrigerated transportation space, the non-compete would not cover it because "refrigerated freight transportation" is not part of the Key Employee Agreement's "Business" definition. This is a significant gap in the protection Teivav is paying for through the retention bonus and equity grant.

**Recommended fix.** Conform the Key Employee Agreement's "Business" definition to the APA's definition.

**Proposed language for Key Employee Agreement § 1.1:**

> "Business" means temperature-controlled warehousing, refrigerated and frozen freight transportation, and cross-dock services, as conducted by Seller or the Company as of the Closing Date.

## Issue 7 – Cap Equals Escrow Amount: No Excess Recovery for Capped Claims

**Where:** APA § 9.5(a) (Cap = $4,200,000); APA § 3.3 (Escrow Amount = $4,200,000); APA § 9.6 (source of recovery).

**Explanation.** The Cap on Seller's indemnification liability is $4.2 million. The Escrow Amount is also $4.2 million. APA § 9.6 provides that recovery is "satisfied first from the Escrow Amount... and then directly by Seller." Because the Cap equals the Escrow, for any claim subject to the Cap, Teivav's maximum recovery is exactly the escrow. There is no scenario in which Teivav recovers more than the escrow for a capped claim, because the Cap prevents it. The "then directly by Seller" language in § 9.6 is effectively inoperative for capped claims.

**Why it matters.** This is not necessarily a drafting error—it may reflect the negotiated risk allocation. But it means that the $4.2 million escrow is Teivav's absolute ceiling for general rep breaches, covenant breaches, and (per Issue 2 above) Excluded Liabilities. If multiple general rep breaches aggregate to, say, $6 million, Teivav recovers only $4.2 million. For a $42 million acquisition, a 10% cap is at the low end of market (15–20% is more common for a business of this complexity and regulatory exposure).

**Recommended fix.** If the cap percentage is negotiable, seek to increase it to 15% ($6.3 million) with a corresponding increase in the Escrow Amount. If the cap percentage is fixed, at minimum ensure the Escrow Amount exceeds the Cap so that the "then directly by Seller" language in § 9.6 has meaning (e.g., Escrow of $5 million, Cap of $4.2 million—though this would be unusual). Alternatively, accept the structure but ensure Issues 1, 2, and 3 are fixed so that the most likely large claims (Excluded Liabilities, environmental) fall outside the Cap.

## Issue 8 – Insurance Gap Between Signing and Closing

**Where:** APA § 2.2(c) (insurance policies are Excluded Assets); APA § 6.1 (conduct of business covenant); Integration Notes § 9.1 (new coverage bound effective 12:01 a.m. on Closing Date).

**Explanation.** Seller's existing insurance policies are Excluded Assets and do not transfer to Teivav. Teivav's new insurance program is bound effective 12:01 a.m. on the Closing Date (Integration Notes § 9.1). Between signing (March 3, 2026) and Closing (targeted late May 2026), the Purchased Assets are presumably covered by Seller's existing policies. However, the APA does not contain an explicit covenant requiring Seller to maintain its existing insurance coverage (or equivalent) through the Closing Date. Section 6.1 requires Seller to "maintain the Purchased Assets in good operating condition" and conduct the Business "in the ordinary course," which arguably implies maintaining insurance, but this is not stated expressly.

**Why it matters.** If a loss occurs to the Purchased Assets (e.g., a reefer trailer is damaged in transit, an ammonia system leaks at a facility) during the pre-Closing period, and Seller's insurance has lapsed or is insufficient, the loss falls on Seller under § 6.1 (failure to maintain in good condition) but Teivav's remedy is an indemnification claim subject to the Cap, basket, and survival period. Teivav would prefer the loss be covered by insurance so that the assets arrive at Closing in the condition represented.

**Recommended fix.** Add an express covenant in § 6.1 requiring Seller to maintain its existing insurance programs (or substantially equivalent coverage) in full force and effect through the Closing Date.

**Proposed addition to APA § 6.1:**

> Seller shall maintain in full force and effect, through the Closing Date, all insurance policies covering the Business and the Purchased Assets that are in effect as of the date of this Agreement, in amounts and with coverages no less favorable than those in effect as of such date.

## Issue 9 – No Representation on Customer Concentration or Contract Renewals

**Where:** APA Article IV (Seller's representations and warranties); Schedule 4.7.

**Explanation.** The APA contains no representation regarding customer concentration risk, upcoming contract expirations, or known customer dissatisfaction or renewal risk. Schedule 4.7 lists Material Contracts by revenue threshold, but there is no rep that these contracts are expected to be renewed, that no customer has indicated an intention to leave, or that revenue is not disproportionately concentrated in a small number of accounts.

**Why it matters.** In a logistics business with a limited number of large customers (the Material Contracts threshold is only $500,000), the loss of even one or two key accounts can materially affect the business's value. Teivav is paying $42 million based in part on the revenue stream those contracts represent. Without a rep on renewals or customer retention risk, Teivav has no contractual recourse if, for example, a top-3 customer notifies Teivav within 30 days of Closing that it will not renew.

**Recommended fix.** Add a representation in Article IV that, to Seller's knowledge, (a) no Material Contract is subject to a pending or threatened non-renewal, termination, or material amendment by the counterparty; and (b) no single customer accounts for more than [X]% of Business revenue in the trailing 12 months.

## Issue 10 – Key Employee Agreement: Retention Bonus Not Forfeitable on Voluntary Resignation Without Good Reason

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 3.3 (Retention Bonus); § 4.2 (termination at will); § 4.3 (severance triggers).

**Explanation.** The $250,000 retention bonus is payable in two installments ($125,000 at each anniversary of the Effective Date), "subject to the Executive's continued employment through that date, except as provided in Section 4.3." Section 4.3 is triggered only by termination without Cause or resignation for Good Reason. If the Executive simply resigns without Good Reason before the first anniversary, he is not employed on the payment date and does not receive the first installment. However, if he resigns without Good Reason *after* the first anniversary but *before* the second, he has already received the first $125,000 and forfeits only the second installment.

More importantly, there is no clawback or forfeiture provision if the Executive resigns without Good Reason and immediately joins a competitor. The non-compete (Section 6.1) would be enforceable, and a breach would trigger the § 7.2 forfeiture of *severance* payments, but the retention bonus already paid is not subject to clawback.

**Why it matters.** The retention bonus is designed to keep the Executive through a critical integration period. If he collects $125,000 at month 12 and then resigns at month 13 to join a competitor, Teivav has lost the benefit of the retention without recovering the money. The non-compete remedy (injunction + forfeiture of future severance) may not be sufficient deterrence.

**Recommended fix.** Add a clawback provision for the retention bonus if the Executive resigns without Good Reason during the Restricted Period or materially breaches Section 6.

**Proposed language (new Key Employee Agreement § 3.5):**

> **3.5 Clawback of Retention Bonus.** If, during the Restricted Period, the Executive (a) resigns without Good Reason, or (b) materially breaches Section 6, the Executive shall repay to the Company an amount equal to the unamortized portion of the Retention Bonus, calculated on a straight-line basis over the 24-month Restricted Period.

## Issue 11 – Escrow Agent Fees and KYC: Administrative Gaps in Agreed Form

**Where:** Escrow Agreement Schedule 1 (Authorized Representatives and Call-Back Contacts); Schedule 2 (Fees); Integration Notes § 4.2 and Action Item 1.

**Explanation.** Schedule 1 of the Escrow Agreement is entirely placeholder ([●]). The call-back contacts are critical because § 8.6 requires telephone call-back confirmation before any wire disbursement. If the schedule is not completed before Closing, the Escrow Agent may be unable to process disbursements, potentially delaying payment of uncontested claims under § 5.3.

Additionally, the Escrow Agent's acceptance fee is $3,500 and the annual administration fee is $4,000 (Schedule 2), split equally between the parties (Escrow Agreement § 9.1; APA § 11.1). The "annual" administration fee is payable at Closing and "not prorated" (Schedule 2), even though the Escrow Period is only 90 days. Teivav is paying half of a full year's administration fee for a 3-month escrow.

**Why it matters.** The administrative issues are minor in dollar terms but could create operational friction at Closing. The unprorated annual fee is a small but unnecessary cost.

**Recommended fix.** (a) Ensure Schedule 1 is fully populated with named individuals and direct phone numbers before Closing. (b) Negotiate a prorated administration fee for a 90-day escrow period, or accept as de minimis.

## Issue 12 – APA § 4.6: "Neither Seller nor, to Seller's Knowledge, Any Landlord Is in Material Default"

**Where:** APA § 4.6 (Real Property).

**Explanation.** The representation regarding the Leases is qualified by "to Seller's knowledge" as to the landlord's performance. This means Seller is not warranting that the landlords are actually in compliance—only that Seller doesn't know of a default. If a landlord has been in default (e.g., failing to maintain the ammonia containment systems, roof leaks affecting refrigeration, or failing to provide required utilities), Teivav may not have a clean rep to rely on.

**Why it matters.** The Facilities are leased, not owned (APA § 4.6: "Seller owns no real property"). The value of the Business depends heavily on the condition and availability of the facilities. If a landlord is in default on its maintenance obligations and Seller simply didn't know, Teivav inherits the problem with limited recourse.

**Recommended fix.** Remove the knowledge qualifier as to landlord default, or at minimum expand due diligence to include landlord compliance certificates. If Seller will not remove the qualifier, add a specific rep that Seller has received no notice of landlord default and that all landlord-maintained systems (roof, HVAC, ammonia containment infrastructure) are in compliance.

## Open Questions

The following items require facts from the client or further document review before I can opine:

1. **Disclosure Schedules.** The APA representations are all subject to "except as set forth in the Disclosure Schedules delivered by Seller concurrently with this Agreement." I have not seen the Disclosure Schedules. Material exceptions in the schedules (particularly to §§ 4.8, 4.10, 4.12, and 4.13) could significantly alter the risk picture. **Request the Disclosure Schedules for review.**

2. **Norrbeck Biologics revenue and contract term.** Schedule 4.7 identifies the Norrbeck contract as a Material Contract (>$500K revenue in FY2025) but does not state the actual revenue figure or the contract term/renewal date. **Request the contract from the data room to assess the financial impact if consent is not obtained.**

3. **Status of landlord consents (Schedule 7.2(c)).** The integration notes (Section 8.1) indicate the team is checking on status weekly, but there is no confirmation that either consent has been obtained. **Request current status from the deal team.** If either consent is not obtained by Closing, the applicable Lease becomes an Excluded Asset under § 2.7 (no price adjustment), and Teivav would lose the facility without a corresponding reduction in Purchase Price.

4. **Schedule 10.1 (Transferred Employees).** I have not seen the list of employees to be offered employment. The scope of this list affects the wage and benefit assumptions in the integration plan. **Request Schedule 10.1.**

5. **Schedule 2.1(b) (Tangible Personal Property).** The integration notes reference this schedule for fleet and equipment, but I have not seen it. Confirming the scope of equipment (particularly the age and condition of reefer units and ammonia systems) is relevant to the adequacy of the § 4.8 condition rep. **Request Schedule 2.1(b).**

6. **Key Employee equity grant.** Key Employee Agreement § 3.4 provides for an equity award "subject to approval by the Board." The integration notes (Action Item 7.3) reference "equipment-grant paperwork for Board approval" (likely a typo for "equity-grant"). **Confirm whether Board approval has been obtained or is scheduled before Closing.** If the equity award is a material inducement to the Executive (as Recital D suggests), its uncertainty could affect his commitment.
