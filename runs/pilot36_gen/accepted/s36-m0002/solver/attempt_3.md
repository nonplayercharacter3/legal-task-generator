## Summary

Review of the Asset Purchase Agreement ("**APA**"), the agreed-form Escrow Agreement ("**Escrow Agreement**"), the Key Employee Agreement ("**KEA**"), and the integration planning meeting notes ("**Integration Notes**") identifies ten issues that expose Teivav to material financial or operational risk, or that create internal conflicts among the deal documents. The five most significant are: (1) the Norrbeck Biologics contract may be lost at Closing with no price adjustment and no walk-right; (2) the Environmental Representations survive for only 90 days—shorter than general reps; (3) a direct conflict between the APA and the Escrow Agreement on whether Excluded Liabilities are subject to the indemnification cap, with the Escrow Agreement controlling; (4) a circular indemnity trap in which Teivav indemnifies Seller for pre-Closing customer contract losses that the APA simultaneously designates as Excluded Liabilities; and (5) the KEA's non-compete is keyed to a narrower definition of "Business" than the APA, leaving the transportation and cross-dock operations unprotected.

## Issue 1 – Norrbeck Biologics Contract: No Consent, No Walk-Right, No Price Adjustment

**Where:** APA Schedule 4.7 (item 1); APA § 2.7; APA Schedule 7.2(c); APA § 7.2(c)

**Problem.** The Norrbeck Biologics Master Temperature-Controlled Storage Agreement is a Material Contract (revenue exceeded $500,000 in FY 2025) and is listed on Schedule 4.7 as an Assigned Contract. Schedule 4.7 states that consent to assignment is required and that "Consent has not been obtained as of the date of this Agreement." Critically, the Norrbeck consent is **not** listed on Schedule 7.2(c) as a Required Consent. The only Required Consents are the two landlord consents (Linden, NJ and Allentown, PA).

This creates a three-part exposure:

- **No condition to close.** Teivav's obligation to close is conditioned on Seller obtaining the Required Consents (APA § 7.2(c)), but Norrbeck is not one of them. Teivav cannot walk if the Norrbeck consent is not obtained.
- **No price adjustment.** APA § 2.7 provides that if a consent to assignment of any Assigned Contract has not been obtained at Closing, the contract "shall be deemed an Excluded Asset for all purposes of this Agreement, **without adjustment to the Purchase Price**." Teivav pays the full $42,000,000 even if it loses the Norrbeck contract.
- **Counterparty termination right.** Schedule 4.7 notes that Norrbeck "may terminate the agreement upon any assignment made without such consent." Even if the assignment is attempted, Norrbeck can terminate, and Teivav would have no contract at all.

**Why it matters.** The Norrbeck contract is one of only five Material Contracts and is the largest customer relationship by the revenue threshold. Losing it undermines the revenue base Teivav is paying $42M to acquire. The integration team is planning Day One operations assuming continuity of all customer contracts (Integration Notes § 3.1).

**Recommended fix.**

Before going back to Seller, Teivav should demand:

1. Addition of the Norrbeck consent to Schedule 7.2(c) as a Required Consent, making it a condition to Teivav's closing obligation; **or**
2. A purchase price reduction mechanism if the Norrbeck consent is not obtained (e.g., a per-dollar reduction based on the contract's trailing twelve-month revenue, or a fixed reduction agreed in the amendment); **or**
3. A specific closing condition that the Norrbeck contract be assigned and in full force at Closing, with Teivav's right to terminate if it is not.

Proposed language for option 2 (price adjustment):

> *"Notwithstanding Section 2.7, if the consent of Norrbeck Biologics, Inc. to the assignment of the Master Temperature-Controlled Storage Agreement dated September 1, 2022 (as amended) has not been obtained as of the Closing Date, the Purchase Price shall be reduced by $[●] (the 'Norrbeck Adjustment'), and the Escrow Amount shall be reduced proportionately."*

**Open question.** What is the trailing twelve-month revenue under the Norrbeck contract, and what is its remaining term? This will drive the appropriate price adjustment. Please confirm from the data room.

## Issue 2 – Environmental Representations Survive Only 90 Days

**Where:** APA § 9.1(b); Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Escrow Period")

**Problem.** APA § 9.1(b) provides that the Environmental Representations (APA § 4.12) "shall survive the Closing until the expiration of the Escrow Period." The Escrow Period is defined in the Escrow Agreement as **90 days after the Closing Date**. By contrast, general representations survive for **12 months** (APA § 9.1(a)), and Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations (APA § 9.1(c)).

The Environmental Representations thus have a **shorter** survival period than ordinary reps. For a business operating anhydrous ammonia refrigeration systems subject to RMP and PSM regulations, managing refrigerant leak rates under 40 C.F.R. Part 82 Subpart F, and handling temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical and food cargo, environmental and safety issues frequently have long latency. An ammonia leak that contaminates soil may not be detected for months. A refrigerant system that is under-maintained may fail after the 90-day window.

**Why it matters.** If Teivav discovers an environmental violation or a refrigeration system defect at month four, the Environmental Representations have expired, the escrow has been released to Seller, and Teivav has no indemnification claim (except for Fraud or a Fundamental Rep breach, neither of which is likely to apply). Teivav would bear the full cost of remediation or equipment replacement.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend APA § 9.1(b) to provide a longer survival period for Environmental Representations. A market-standard approach for environmental reps in asset deals involving regulated facilities is the applicable statute of limitations, or a minimum of 24–36 months.

Proposed language:

> *"Section 9.1(b) is hereby amended and restated as follows: The Environmental Representations shall survive the Closing until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, but in no event less than 24 months after the Closing Date."*

If Seller resists a statute-of-limitations period, a fallback is 18 months with a corresponding extension of the Escrow Period for environmental claims (see Issue 3 below regarding the escrow release).

**Open question.** Did Teivav's environmental diligence identify any specific concerns (e.g., aging ammonia systems, past leak events, missing RMP filings) that would justify a longer survival period as a business matter? If so, that strengthens the negotiating position.

## Issue 3 – Conflict Between APA and Escrow Agreement on the Cap: Excluded Liabilities

**Where:** APA § 9.5(b); Escrow Agreement § 6.3; APA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence)

**Problem.** APA § 9.5(b) states:

> *"The Cap shall not apply to Losses arising from Fraud, breach of Fundamental Representations, **and Excluded Liabilities**."*

Escrow Agreement § 6.3 states:

> *"The Cap shall apply to all claims under Section 9.2 of the Purchase Agreement other than claims for Fraud or breach of Fundamental Representations."*

The Escrow Agreement **omits Excluded Liabilities** from the cap carve-out. Because Excluded Liabilities are indemnified under APA § 9.2(c), they are "claims under Section 9.2" and would be subject to the $4,200,000 Cap under the Escrow Agreement's language.

APA § 11.9 provides the order of precedence:

> *"In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, this Agreement shall prevail over the Ancillary Agreements, **except that the Escrow Agreement shall control on indemnification claims and limits**."*

The Cap is an "indemnification limit." Therefore, the Escrow Agreement's narrower carve-out controls, and Excluded Liabilities are **capped at $4,200,000**—contrary to the APA's express language.

**Why it matters.** Excluded Liabilities include all pre-Closing operational liabilities: environmental liabilities (APA § 2.4(f)), pre-Closing cargo claims and temperature excursions (§ 2.4(b), (e)), pre-Closing taxes (§ 2.4(c)), and pre-Closing wages (§ 2.4(d)). In a refrigerated logistics business, a single major cargo spoilage claim, an ammonia release, or a back-tax assessment could easily exceed $4.2M. Capping Excluded Liabilities at the same amount as the general indemnification cap effectively eliminates the protection APA § 2.4 was designed to provide.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend Escrow Agreement § 6.3 to conform to APA § 9.5(b):

> *"Section 6.3 is hereby amended and restated as follows: The aggregate liability of Seller for indemnification under the Purchase Agreement shall not exceed $4,200,000 (10% of the Purchase Price) (the 'Cap'). The Cap shall not apply to claims for Fraud, breach of Fundamental Representations, or Excluded Liabilities."*

This is a non-negotiable fix. The APA's intent is clear, and the Escrow Agreement's omission appears to be a drafting error.

## Issue 4 – Circular Indemnity: Buyer Indemnifies Seller for Pre-Closing Customer Contract Losses

**Where:** APA § 9.3(c); APA § 2.4(b); APA § 9.2(c)

**Problem.** APA § 9.3(c) provides that Buyer (Teivav) shall indemnify Seller for:

> *"the Transferred Customer Contracts, **whether arising before or after the Closing**."*

Simultaneously, APA § 2.4(b) designates as Excluded Liabilities:

> *"all liabilities under the Assigned Contracts arising from Seller's performance or non-performance before the Closing, including claims for temperature excursions, spoiled, damaged or lost product, service-level credits and chargebacks."*

And APA § 9.2(c) requires Seller to indemnify Buyer for "any Excluded Liability."

The result: if a customer brings a claim for a pre-Closing temperature excursion (a classic Excluded Liability under § 2.4(b)):

- Seller must indemnify Teivav under § 9.2(c) (because it is an Excluded Liability); **and**
- Teivav must indemnify Seller under § 9.3(c) (because it arises from a Transferred Customer Contract "before the Closing").

The two indemnities point in opposite directions for the same loss. In practice, this creates a circular offset that likely results in neither party recovering anything, or—worse—Teivav's counsel arguing that § 9.3(c) is the more specific provision (it expressly covers "before or after the Closing") and therefore controls, leaving Teivav on the hook for pre-Closing contract claims it was supposed to be protected from under § 2.4(b).

**Why it matters.** In a refrigerated logistics business, pre-Closing customer claims (spoiled product, temperature excursions, service-level credits) are among the most common and costly post-Closing disputes. The Excluded Liability structure in § 2.4 was designed to keep these with Seller. Section 9.3(c) undermines that protection.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend APA § 9.3(c) to exclude pre-Closing losses:

> *"Section 9.3(c) is hereby amended and restated as follows: Buyer shall indemnify Seller from and against all Losses arising out of or resulting from the Transferred Customer Contracts to the extent arising from Buyer's performance or non-performance **after the Closing**."*

Alternatively, add an express carve-out:

> *"Notwithstanding the foregoing, Section 9.3(c) shall not apply to any Excluded Liability."*

**Open question.** Confirm with the deal team whether § 9.3(c) was negotiated as a Seller protection for post-Closing customer disputes (e.g., if Teivav fails to meet service levels after Closing, Seller wants indemnification). If so, the fix above preserves that protection while eliminating the circularity for pre-Closing claims.

## Issue 5 – Key Employee Non-Compete Covers Only Warehousing, Not Transportation or Cross-Dock

**Where:** KEA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); APA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); KEA § 6.1(a)

**Problem.** The KEA defines "Business" as:

> *"the operation of temperature-controlled warehouse facilities."*

The APA defines "Business" as:

> *"temperature-controlled warehousing, refrigerated and frozen freight transportation, and cross-dock services, as conducted by Seller as of the date of this Agreement."*

The non-compete in KEA § 6.1(a) prohibits the Executive from engaging in "the Business" within the Restricted Territory. Because the KEA's definition of "Business" is limited to warehousing, the non-compete does **not** restrict the Executive from competing in refrigerated transportation or cross-dock services—two of the three core service lines Teivav is acquiring.

The non-solicitation provisions in § 6.1(b)–(d) reference "the Business" and "customers of the Business," so they are similarly narrowed.

**Why it matters.** The Key Employee is the COO of Seller and will be SVP of Operations at Teivav, with a remit that explicitly includes "refrigerated fleet operations, transportation and dispatch, and cross-dock operations" (KEA § 2.3; Integration Notes § 7.3). He possesses detailed knowledge of all three service lines. If he departs and starts a refrigerated transportation or cross-dock business, the non-compete provides no protection. Teivav is paying a $250,000 retention bonus and a $310,000 base salary in part for this protection (KEA Recital D: the covenants are "a material inducement to the Company to enter into the Purchase Agreement").

**Recommended fix.**

Amend KEA § 1.1 to conform the definition of "Business" to the APA:

> *"'Business' means temperature-controlled warehousing, refrigerated and frozen freight transportation, and cross-dock services, as conducted by Seller as of the date of the Purchase Agreement or by the Company or its Affiliates at any time during the Executive's employment."*

This should be a straightforward conforming edit. The Executive's actual role spans all three service lines, so the broader definition accurately reflects the goodwill Teivav is protecting.

## Issue 6 – Key Employee Non-Compete Expires on a Fixed Date Regardless of Employment Duration

**Where:** KEA § 6.1 (definition of "Restricted Period")

**Problem.** The Restricted Period is defined as "until 24 months after the Closing Date." The non-compete applies "During the Executive's employment and until 24 months after the Closing Date." If the Executive remains employed beyond 24 months post-Closing, the restrictive covenants **expire** even though he is still in the role and still possesses current Confidential Information. He could leave at month 25 and immediately compete without restriction.

**Why it matters.** For a key operations leader in a specialized industry, 24 months from Closing may not be sufficient protection if he stays employed. The standard market approach is a post-employment restriction (e.g., 12–24 months after separation from service), not a fixed date from Closing.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend KEA § 6.1 to tie the post-employment restriction to separation from service:

> *"During the Executive's employment and for 18 months after the Executive's employment terminates for any reason (the 'Restricted Period'), the Executive shall not…"*

This ensures protection runs for a meaningful period after he actually leaves, regardless of when that occurs.

**Open question.** What is Teivav's business expectation for the Key Employee's tenure? If the plan is to retain him for 3+ years, a fixed 24-month-from-Closing restriction leaves a gap. If the plan is 2 years, the current structure is adequate.

## Issue 7 – WMS Software License May Not Be Transferable; Data Without Software Is of Limited Value

**Where:** APA § 2.1(f); APA § 4.8; Integration Notes § 6.1–6.2

**Problem.** APA § 2.1(f) includes "all intellectual property, software licenses and warehouse management system data used in the Business" as Purchased Assets. However, the Integration Notes acknowledge that "IT will confirm which WMS and routing software licenses are transferable" and that "any non-transferable license will need a replacement license that takes effect at Closing" (Integration Notes § 6.2).

If the WMS is licensed (not owned) and the license is non-transferable, Teivav receives the data (which is useful for historical records and migration) but not the software platform on which the Business currently operates. The integration plan contemplates a 90-day transition period running the "existing WMS instance on Teivav's infrastructure" (Integration Notes § 6.1(a)), which presupposes the license is transferable or that Seller will continue to provide access.

APA § 4.8 represents that "The Purchased Assets constitute all of the assets necessary to conduct the Business as currently conducted." If the WMS license is not transferable, this representation is inaccurate at signing—but it is not a Fundamental Representation and survives only 12 months (APA § 9.1(a)).

**Why it matters.** Without the WMS, Teivav cannot operate the facilities on Day One as planned. A 90-day transition on Seller's infrastructure requires Seller's continued cooperation post-Closing, which the APA does not expressly obligate. If the license is non-transferable and Seller withdraws access, Teivav faces an operational disruption at the worst possible time.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Before Closing, confirm the transferability status of the WMS and routing software licenses (Action Item 4 in the Integration Notes, due April 10, 2026).
2. If non-transferable, add a post-Closing covenant requiring Seller to provide access to the WMS for a transition period (e.g., 90 days) on agreed terms, or require Seller to assign the license if the licensor's consent can be obtained.
3. Consider adding the WMS license (or a transition-services arrangement) to the list of Closing deliverables or conditions.

Proposed language for a transition-services covenant:

> *"Section 6.5 is supplemented as follows: For a period of 90 days after the Closing Date, Seller shall provide Buyer and its designees with continued access to the [●] warehouse management system and related software, solely to the extent necessary for Buyer to migrate the Business's operations to a replacement system, on the terms set forth in Exhibit [●] (Transition Services Agreement)."*

## Issue 8 – Permits Transfer Only "To the Extent Transferable"

**Where:** APA § 2.1(d)

**Problem.** APA § 2.1(d) includes "all permits, licenses and registrations held for the Business, **to the extent transferable**" as Purchased Assets. For a business subject to FMCSA operating authority, state motor carrier permits, EPA RMP registrations, and food-safety-related licenses, some permits are personal to the holder and require re-application by the new operator. The Integration Notes confirm that Teivav will operate under its own USDOT number and MC authority from Day One (Integration Notes § 5.2), implying the FMCSA authority does not transfer.

The "to the extent transferable" qualifier means Teivav has no claim if a permit is non-transferable—it simply doesn't receive it. There is no representation that all necessary permits are in place or that re-application will be timely.

**Why it matters.** If a state or local permit required to operate the Facilities (e.g., a refrigeration system permit, a health department permit, or a local business license) is non-transferable and Teivav cannot obtain a replacement before Closing, there is a regulatory gap on Day One. The APA does not make permit transferability a condition to Closing.

**Recommended fix.**

1. Confirm through diligence which permits are transferable and which require re-application.
2. Add a representation (or expand § 4.10) that all permits necessary to conduct the Business at the Facilities are in full force and effect, and that Seller has provided Buyer with a list identifying which are transferable and which require re-application, together with an estimate of the re-application timeline.
3. If any critical permit has a re-application timeline that extends beyond the target Closing date, consider making it a condition to Closing or requiring Seller to file the re-application pre-Closing.

**Open question.** Please confirm from the diligence team which specific permits are at issue and their transferability status. The Integration Notes do not identify any permit gaps, but the "to the extent transferable" language in the APA suggests the parties were aware of at least some non-transferable items.

## Issue 9 – Key Employee Title Change: Potential Good Reason Trigger

**Where:** KEA Recital A; KEA § 2.2; KEA § 1.1 (definition of "Good Reason"); Integration Notes § 7.3

**Problem.** Recital A of the KEA identifies the Executive as "the Chief Operating Officer of Vaipith Refrigerated Logistics, Inc." Section 2.2 provides that Teivav shall employ him as "Senior Vice President, Operations." This is a title demotion (COO → SVP). The "Good Reason" definition includes "a material diminution in the Executive's title, authority or duties" (KEA § 1.1).

While the Executive is signing the KEA with knowledge of his new title, the Good Reason provision could be triggered post-Closing if his actual authority or duties are materially less than those of a COO. For example, if he reports to a CEO who micromanages operations, or if his budgetary authority is reduced, he could argue a "material diminution in authority or duties" and resign for Good Reason, triggering 12 months of base salary severance plus the full unpaid retention bonus (KEA § 4.3).

**Why it matters.** The severance package for a Good Reason resignation includes: 12 months base salary ($310,000), any earned but unpaid annual bonus (up to ~$124,000 at target), the full unpaid retention bonus (up to $250,000), and 12 months of COBRA premiums. Total exposure: approximately $684,000+ plus benefits.

**Recommended fix.**

This is more of a risk to monitor than a document to amend (the Executive has already agreed to the SVP title). However, to reduce ambiguity:

1. In the KEA or a side letter, include an express acknowledgment that the change in title from COO to SVP, Operations is agreed and does not constitute a diminution of title for purposes of the Good Reason definition.
2. Ensure that the Executive's actual authority and duties at Teivav are consistent with the role described in KEA § 2.3 (oversight of facility operations, fleet operations, transportation and dispatch, and cross-dock operations).

Proposed side-letter language:

> *"The Executive acknowledges and agrees that his title at the Company shall be Senior Vice President, Operations, and that this title, together with the duties and authority described in Section 2.3 of the Agreement, does not constitute a material diminution in title, authority or duties for purposes of the definition of 'Good Reason' in Section 1.1 of the Agreement."*

## Issue 10 – Escrow Release at 90 Days May Preclude Timely Assertion of Claims

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 7.1; APA § 9.1(a)–(b); APA § 9.4

**Problem.** The Escrow Agreement provides that within five Business Days after the 90-day Escrow Period expires, the Escrow Agent releases the Escrow Funds to Seller (less any Pending Claim Amount) (Escrow Agreement § 7.1). A claim becomes "pending" only if a Claim Notice is delivered before the Escrow Period ends (Escrow Agreement § 1.1, definition of "Pending Claim Amount").

For general representations (12-month survival under APA § 9.1(a)), Teivav has 12 months to discover a breach—but only 90 days to file a Claim Notice against the escrow. After 90 days, the escrow is gone. Teivav can still bring a claim against Seller directly under APA § 9.6 ("and then directly by Seller"), but:

- Seller may have distributed the escrow funds to its shareholders or used them for other purposes;
- Collection risk increases significantly;
- The practical deterrent effect of the escrow is lost.

For a $42M acquisition, the escrow is Teivav's primary security. If it is released at 90 days while general reps survive for 12 months, there is a 9-month window in which Teivav has a valid claim but no secured source of recovery.

**Why it matters.** The mismatch between the survival period (12 months) and the escrow release (90 days) means the escrow does not fully secure the indemnification obligations it was created to back. This is particularly acute for claims that require investigation (e.g., financial statement accuracy, asset condition, compliance with laws) where the facts may not be fully apparent within 90 days.

**Recommended fix.**

Align the Escrow Period with the general survival period. Amend the Escrow Agreement:

> *"'Escrow Period' means the period beginning on the Closing Date and ending at 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the date that is 12 months after the Closing Date."*

If Seller resists a full 12-month escrow (which is common), a compromise is a staggered release:

> *"Section 7.1 is amended as follows: Within five Business Days after the expiration of the Escrow Period, the Escrow Agent shall disburse to Seller seventy-five percent (75%) of the Escrow Funds, less the Pending Claim Amount. The remaining twenty-five percent (25%) shall be held until the first anniversary of the Closing Date and shall be released to Seller, less the Pending Claim Amount, within five Business Days thereafter."*

This gives Teivav a meaningful security period for the bulk of the escrow while providing Seller with partial liquidity.
