## 1. Pellam Ground Lease — Missing Consent (Critical)

**Documents:** SPA § 4.4; Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.4 ("None"); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9 item 3 (Pellam Ground Lease, § 14.2); SPA § 7.2(c); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 7.2(c) (lists only liquor licenses).

**Issue.** Section 14.2 of the Pellam Ground Lease provides that a transfer of more than 50% of the stock of Tenant (the Company) "is deemed an assignment of the lease" requiring Landlord's prior written consent under § 14.1. This transaction is a 100% stock sale. Yet:

- Schedule 4.4 (No Conflicts) states "None," meaning Seller has represented that no consent is required under any Material Contract.
- Schedule 7.2(c) (Required Consents) lists only liquor license approvals. The ground lease consent is absent.
- Section 7.2(c) of the SPA makes each Required Consent a condition to Buyer's closing obligation. Because the ground lease consent is not a Required Consent, Buyer can close without it.

**Why it matters.** Closing without Landlord's consent would put the Company in breach of the ground lease on day one. Hotel Pellam is the portfolio's flagship (142 rooms, Baltimore) and sits on a ground lease running to 2067. The Landlord could assert a default, demand cure, or (depending on the lease's termination provisions) terminate. This is the largest and most revenue-producing asset in the deal.

**Recommendation.**
- Add the Pellam Ground Lease consent to Schedule 7.2(c) as a Required Consent, making it a condition to closing.
- Correct Schedule 4.4 to disclose the consent requirement (or confirm with Seller's counsel that they intend to obtain it and add it to the disclosure).
- Begin the consent request process immediately; ground-lease consents can take 30–60 days.
- **Flag for client call:** Confirm whether Seller has already approached the Landlord (Ashcombe Quay Land Trust) and what the Landlord's posture is. If the Landlord is difficult, this could become a deal risk.

## 2. Bill of Sale Fails to Convey Domain Names, Guest Data, and Software Licenses (Critical)

**Documents:** SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Hotel Operating Assets," clauses (c)–(e)); Bill of Sale § 1.2 (redefined "Hotel Operating Assets"); Bill of Sale, Annex A (lists only FF&E and inventory); SPA § 5.8; SPA § 11.10.

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Hotel Operating Assets" to include five categories: (a) FF&E; (b) inventories; (c) PMS/CRS software licenses, subscriptions, related hardware and service agreements; (d) Guest Data; and (e) Hotel Domain Names. Section 5.8 requires the Service Affiliate to convey all Hotel Operating Assets "by executing and delivering to the Company the Bill of Sale."

But the Bill of Sale redefines "Hotel Operating Assets" in § 1.2 as only "all furniture, fixtures, equipment and inventory owned by Transferor that are located at the Hotels and listed on Annex A." Annex A contains FF&E and inventory only. The domain names (all registered in the Service Affiliate's name per Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 1.1(b)), the Guest Data, and the PMS/CRS licenses are nowhere in the Bill of Sale.

SPA § 11.10 provides that the SPA prevails over conflicts with Ancillary Agreements, "except that each Ancillary Agreement shall govern the conveyance of the assets transferred under it." Because the Bill of Sale does not purport to transfer the domain names, Guest Data, or software licenses, there is no operative mechanism for their conveyance. And § 5.8 deems Seller's obligation satisfied "upon such delivery" of the Bill of Sale.

**Why it matters.** Without the domain names, the Company loses sablewick.com, hotelpellam.com, and eight other domains plus all social-media handles — the primary customer acquisition channels. Without Guest Data, the Company cannot access guest profiles, loyalty records, reservation histories, or e-mail lists. Without PMS/CRS licenses, the hotels may lose their operational backbone. These are not nice-to-haves; they are core operating assets.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend the Bill of Sale to use the SPA's full definition of "Hotel Operating Assets" (or add a separate schedule covering domain names, Guest Data, and software licenses with specific transfer mechanics).
- Add a data-transfer protocol for Guest Data (format, timing, security requirements).
- Confirm with the Service Affiliate which PMS/CRS platform is in use and whether the licenses are assignable or require a new agreement with the vendor.
- **Flag for client call:** Confirm the business criticality of the Guest Data and domain names and whether Draibeir's IT team has a transition plan. If the PMS is proprietary to the Bruneis group, a replacement may be needed, which has cost and timeline implications.

## 3. General Representation Survival Effectively Limited to Six Months (High)

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1 (Survival); SPA § 2.5(b) (Escrow Release at 6 months).

**Issue.** Section 9.1 provides that General Representations survive "until the earlier of (a) the date that is eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date and (b) the date on which the Escrow Amount is released pursuant to Section 2.5." The Escrow Amount is released six months after Closing (§ 2.5(b)). The "earlier of" language means the effective survival period for all General Reps — financial statements, real property, IP, material contracts, permits, compliance with laws, environmental, insurance, employees, litigation — is **six months**, not eighteen.

**Why it matters.** Many issues (e.g., a latent environmental condition, a hidden defect in a material contract, a permit that was about to be revoked) may not surface within six months. After the escrow releases, Buyer's only recourse is a direct claim against Seller, subject to the 10% Cap (§ 9.4(a)) and the 0.5% Deductible (§ 9.4(b)). In practice, chasing a seller for a modest indemnification claim after the escrow is gone is costly and often unrecoverable.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend § 9.1 to delete clause (b) or change "earlier of" to "later of," so General Reps survive for the full 18 months regardless of escrow release.
- Alternatively, extend the escrow release to 18 months to align with the stated survival period.
- If Seller resists, consider a compromise: extend survival to 12 months and keep the 6-month escrow release, accepting that claims between months 6 and 12 are unsecured.
- **Flag for client call:** This is a negotiating point. Seller will likely resist extending the escrow. The client should decide how much risk it is willing to accept on the financial statement and compliance reps with only 6 months of secured coverage.

## 4. Escrow Release Does Not Protect the Working Capital Adjustment (High)

**Documents:** SPA § 2.5(a) (escrow secures both Art. IX indemnification and § 2.4(d) obligations); SPA § 2.5(b) (release mechanism carves out only Art. IX claims); SPA § 2.4(b)–(c) (Closing Statement due 90 days post-closing; 45-day review; 30-day negotiation; 30-day Independent Accountant determination); Escrow Agreement § 5.2(a).

**Issue.** The escrow is expressly designated as security for both indemnification claims and the purchase price adjustment under § 2.4(d) (SPA § 2.5(a)). But the release mechanism in § 2.5(b) only withholds amounts for "claims for indemnification set forth in any claim notice delivered by Buyer in accordance with Article IX." A working capital true-up under § 2.4 is not an Article IX claim.

The § 2.4 process can take up to 195 days after Closing (90 + 45 + 30 + 30). The escrow releases at 180 days (6 months). If the working capital adjustment is still in dispute at the Release Date, the escrow will be released to Seller without securing the § 2.4(d) payment obligation.

**Why it matters.** The Working Capital Target is $2.3M. If actual working capital is below target, Seller owes Buyer the difference. If the escrow is released before the adjustment is finalized, Buyer is an unsecured creditor for that amount.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend SPA § 2.5(b) to also withhold from the escrow release any amount that is the subject of a pending § 2.4 dispute as of the Release Date.
- Alternatively, amend the Escrow Agreement § 5.2(a) to include § 2.4(d) obligations in the Reserved Amount calculation.
- As a practical matter, confirm with the client's accounting team that the Closing Statement can be prepared well within the 90-day window so the dispute (if any) is resolved before the Release Date.

## 5. Draibeir's Primary Guarantee Under the IP Assignment (Moderate)

**Documents:** IP Assignment Agreement § 6.2 (Assignee indemnification to Assignor); IP Assignment Agreement § 6.4 (Guarantor guarantee); SPA § 9.4 (Cap and Deductible).

**Issue.** Draibeir, as Guarantor, has given an irrevocable, unconditional, primary guarantee of the Company's (Assignee's) indemnification obligations to the Brand Affiliate under IP Assignment § 6.2. Those obligations cover post-closing third-party infringement claims related to the Hotel Marks, post-closing operation of the Hotels under the marks, and any breach of the Company's covenants in the IP Assignment.

Section 6.2 is "subject to the limitations set forth in Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement" (Cap and Deductible). However, the guarantee in § 6.4 does not independently reference those limitations. Section 6.4(b) makes the guarantee one of payment, not collection — the Brand Affiliate can go directly after Draibeir without first proceeding against the Company. Section 6.4(d) provides the guarantee survives changes in the Company's ownership, insolvency, or transfer of the marks.

**Why it matters.** If a third party asserts an infringement claim against the Hotel Marks post-closing, the Brand Affiliate can demand payment directly from Draibeir (the parent) without exhausting remedies against the Company. The practical exposure is limited by the SPA Cap (10% of Purchase Price = $14.85M) if the § 6.2 cross-reference to § 9.4 is read to flow through to the guarantee, but that reading is not explicit.

**Recommendation.**
- Add express language to IP Assignment § 6.4 confirming that the Guaranteed Obligations are subject to the Cap and Deductible in SPA § 9.4.
- Confirm with the client that it is comfortable with a primary (not secondary) guarantee structure. If not, negotiate to make it a guarantee of collection (i.e., Brand Affiliate must proceed against the Company first).
- **Flag for client call:** The client should understand that this guarantee is a direct, unconditional obligation of Draibeir (not just the Company) and survives the Company's insolvency or any future sale of the marks.

## 6. Lisset Hotel Liquor License Expiration Timing (Moderate)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.10, Part 1, item 3 (Lisset Hotel license CHT-ALC-20-0988, expires December 31, 2026); Press Release § 1.5 and § 7.1(b) (expected Q4 2026 closing).

**Issue.** The Lisset Hotel's liquor license expires December 31, 2026. The parties expect closing in Q4 2026. If closing occurs in late November or December 2026, the license could expire before or around the change-of-ownership approval. The Required Consents (Schedule 7.2(c)) address the change-of-ownership approval but do not separately address renewal of the expiring license.

**Why it matters.** If the license lapses, the Lisset Hotel cannot serve alcohol until a new license is issued. For a hotel whose rooftop bar and restaurant are described in the press release as central to the guest experience (§ 4.3), a lapse in liquor service would be operationally disruptive and could affect revenue during the transition.

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the Savannah licensing authority whether the change-of-ownership application and the renewal application can be filed simultaneously and whether an interim operating permit is available.
- If closing is expected after December 31, 2026, require Seller to renew the license pre-closing as a condition or covenant.
- Add the Lisset Hotel license renewal to the Required Consents if it is not subsumed by the change-of-ownership approval.

## 7. Insurance Program Transition (Moderate)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.16 (note: "Policies 1 through 5 and 7 are written under Seller's corporate insurance program, with the Company as a named insured"); SPA (no insurance transition covenant).

**Issue.** The Company's property, general liability, umbrella, workers' comp, auto, and EPL policies are all written under Seller's corporate insurance program. After closing, the Company will no longer be part of Seller's corporate family. There is no covenant in the SPA requiring Seller to maintain these policies through a transition period or to cooperate in placing new coverage. Most policies expire January 1, 2027.

**Why it matters.** If closing occurs in Q4 2026, the Company will need to replace its entire insurance program within weeks of closing. Any gap in coverage (property, liability, workers' comp) would leave the hotels exposed. The cyber policy (Policy 6) runs from March 1, 2026 to March 1, 2027, providing a slightly longer runway, but the core policies expire January 1, 2027.

**Recommendation.**
- Require Seller to maintain the existing insurance program through at least the first policy renewal date post-closing, or to cooperate in good faith with the transition to new coverage.
- Direct Draibeir's risk management team to begin the insurance placement process now, targeting binding of new policies effective on or before January 1, 2027.
- **Flag for client call:** Confirm Draibeir has an insurance broker engaged and that the new program will be in place before the current policies lapse.

## 9. Press Release — Public Commitments Requiring Internal Alignment (Low)

**Documents:** Press Release § 6.2 (12-month wage commitment); § 12.4 (Pellam renovation "proceeding on schedule"); § 1.2 (no financing condition); § 4.4 (intent to "evaluate further capital projects").

**Issue.** The press release makes several public statements that create expectations:
- The 12-month wage commitment matches SPA § 5.11, but the press release states it as a "commitment" rather than a contractual obligation limited to "base salary or hourly wage rates." Employees may read it more broadly.
- The statement that the Pellam renovation is "proceeding on schedule" and Draibeir "intends to see it through to completion" is a public representation about a $4.75M GMP construction project with ~$3.54M remaining. If the project encounters delays or cost overruns, the press release creates a narrative problem.
- "The transaction is not subject to a financing condition" is accurate but means Draibeir must wire $148.5M (less escrow, debt payoff, and transaction expenses) at closing with no contingency.

**Recommendation.**
- Brief the client's communications team on the scope of the wage commitment (base pay only; does not extend to bonuses, benefits, or equity).
- Confirm with the construction manager that the Pellam renovation is truly on schedule before the press release is finalized. If there is any slippage, the language should be softened.
- **Flag for client call:** Confirm Draibeir's treasury has the full closing funds arranged and that the client is comfortable with the public "no financing condition" statement.

## 10. Non-Compete Carve-Out for 200+ Room Properties (Low)

**Documents:** SPA § 5.10(a) (restricts properties "having fewer than two hundred (200) guest rooms").

**Issue.** The non-compete does not restrict Seller from opening or acquiring a property with 200 or more rooms within 10 miles of any Hotel. A 200-room hotel in the same market could compete directly with a 142-room property like Hotel Pellam.

**Why it matters.** The practical risk is low (Bruneis is selling, not expanding), but if Seller redeploys capital into the same markets, the 200-room threshold provides a meaningful escape hatch.

**Recommendation.**
- If the client is concerned, negotiate to lower the threshold to 150 rooms or delete the room-count limitation entirely.
- If Seller resists, accept as-is given the 3-year duration and 10-mile radius provide meaningful protection for boutique-scale competition.
