## Issue 1 – Fresno Warehouse Lease Renewal Option Has Likely Already Lapsed

**Location:** SPA §6.4; Disclosure Schedule §8.1(d)–(g); SPA §8.1(b)

**Facts:**

- The Fresno Warehouse Lease (with Pellucid Logistics Park LLC) expires **March 31, 2027** (Disclosure Schedule §8.1(d)).
- The tenant's sole five-year renewal option requires written notice delivered **no later than 180 days before the expiration date**, i.e., on or before approximately **October 2, 2026** (Disclosure Schedule §8.1(f)).
- The Disclosure Schedule expressly states: "As of the date of this Disclosure Schedule, no renewal notice has been delivered" (§8.1(g)).
- The SPA was signed **October 20, 2026** — 18 days after the renewal deadline.
- SPA §6.4 covenants that "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to exercise the renewal option under the Fresno Warehouse Lease in accordance with its terms."

**Why it matters:** The Fresno Warehouse is described as "the Company's principal inventory storage, fleet staging and dispatch facility" (Disclosure Schedule §8.1(b)). It houses all inverter/racking inventory ($1,184,300 book value) and all warehouse equipment (Disclosure Schedule §§2.2–2.3). If the option has lapsed, the Company faces a lease expiration in approximately five months with no contractual right to remain. SPA §6.4 is now impossible to perform as drafted, which could (a) constitute a continuing covenant breach giving Buyer a termination right under §8.1(c), or (b) more practically, force a last-minute negotiation with Pellucid on uncertain terms. The outside date (January 31, 2027, per §8.1(b)) gives only about two months of lease term post-Closing in the worst case.

**Recommendation:**

- **Immediately** confirm with the client whether a renewal notice was in fact delivered before October 2, 2026 (the Disclosure Schedule says no, but we should verify with the client's deal team whether anything was sent informally).
- If the option has lapsed, request that Seller (a) confirm in writing that it will negotiate a new lease or extension with Pellucid on terms acceptable to Buyer as a condition to Closing, or (b) agree to a specific amendment to §6.4 replacing the "exercise the renewal option" language with a covenant to obtain a lease extension or new lease of at least [X] years.
- Consider whether to make delivery of an executed lease extension (or at minimum a letter of intent from Pellucid) a closing deliverable under §3.2(a).
- Assess whether the lapse constitutes a Material Adverse Effect (it likely does not on the current MAE definition, which looks at the business "taken as a whole," but the loss of the principal facility on a five-month clock is operationally severe).
- **Judgment call / facts needed:** We need to know (a) whether Pellucid has informally indicated willingness to extend, (b) whether the client is prepared to walk if no extension is obtained, and (c) the client's fallback plan if the Company must relocate before March 31, 2027.

## Issue 2 – Buyer's Indemnification for Assumed Warranty Obligations Is Uncapped (New in Executed Version)

**Location:** SPA §9.3(c) and §9.4(d) (executed); compare draft §9.3 and §9.4(d)

**Facts:**

- The **draft** SPA §9.3 contained only two Buyer indemnity obligations: (a) rep breaches and (b) covenant breaches. There was no §9.3(c).
- The **executed** SPA adds §9.3(c): Buyer indemnifies Seller for "the Assumed Warranty Obligations, as defined in Schedule 4.12 of the Disclosure Schedule."
- Disclosure Schedule §9.2 defines "Assumed Warranty Obligations" as **all** warranty obligations "for installations whether completed **before or after** the Closing Date" — a sweeping definition covering the entire installed base.
- Executed §9.4(d) states: "Buyer's obligations under Section 9.3(c) shall **not** be subject to the Cap or the Deductible."
- The installed base is approximately 6,120 residential and 214 commercial installations within warranty (Disclosure Schedule §9.3), with annual warranty costs trending upward from $611,400 (2023) to $902,300 (2025) (Disclosure Schedule §9.4).

**Why it matters:** This is a material adverse change from the draft. Buyer is now on the hook, without limit, for the full cost of all warranty obligations of the Company — both legacy and future. In a stock purchase, the Company retains its contractual warranties to customers regardless; the §9.3(c) indemnity effectively means that if Seller is ever called on a warranty (e.g., a customer sues Seller directly, or a manufacturer pass-through creates joint liability), Buyer must reimburse Seller dollar-for-dollar with no cap. Given the known flashing issue affecting 140 installations (80 remaining, per Disclosure Schedule §9.6) and the rising claims trend, this is an unquantified contingent liability.

**Recommendation:**

- Flag to the client that this provision was not in their own draft and appears to have been added by Seller's counsel. Confirm whether the client's deal team was aware of and agreed to this change.
- If the client did not intend to accept uncapped warranty indemnification, this is too late to renegotiate the executed SPA, but it should inform: (a) post-Closing reserves and budgeting, (b) the client's insurance program (confirm that the Company's general liability and professional liability policies adequately cover warranty claims), and (c) any future disputes with Seller (the existence of this provision may limit Buyer's practical ability to push Seller on pre-Closing warranty claims under §9.2(d) without triggering a cross-claim under §9.3(c)).
- **Facts needed:** Confirm the scope and limits of the Company's current insurance coverage for warranty claims, and whether the policies follow the business post-Closing without change-in-control restrictions.

## Issue 3 – Conflict Between Bill of Sale Title Warranty Cap and SPA Fundamental Representation Exclusion

**Location:** Bill of Sale §4.3; SPA §9.4(a)–(b); SPA §11.12

**Facts:**

- SPA §9.4(b) designates §4.6 (Title to Transferred Assets) as a **Fundamental Representation**, excluded from the $4,850,000 Cap. Seller's liability for Fundamental Rep breaches is capped only at the full Purchase Price ($48.5M).
- Bill of Sale §4.1–4.2 contains a separate set of title warranties. Bill of Sale §4.3 provides: "Seller's liability for any breach of the warranties in this Section 4 is subject to the Cap set forth in Section 9.4(a) of the Purchase Agreement" — i.e., $4,850,000.
- SPA §11.12 (new in the executed version; not in the draft) provides: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Document, **the Bill of Sale controls as to the Transferred Assets**."

**Why it matters:** If a title defect is discovered in a Transferred Asset (e.g., a undisclosed lien on a vehicle, or a third-party ownership claim on inventory), Seller will likely argue that the claim falls under Bill of Sale §4 (capped at $4.85M) rather than SPA §4.6 (capped at $48.5M), relying on §11.12's order-of-precedence clause. This could reduce Buyer's recovery by more than $43M in a worst-case scenario. The practical risk is lower (title defects in vehicles and inventory are uncommon), but the structural ambiguity is significant.

**Recommendation:**

- This cannot be fixed unilaterally post-signing. However, for any future transaction or amendment, the client should insist that the order-of-precedence clause either (a) carve Fundamental Representations out of the "Bill of Sale controls" rule, or (b) provide that the Bill of Sale's cap applies only to the Bill of Sale's own warranties and does not limit remedies under the SPA.
- For this deal, document the ambiguity in the file. If a title issue arises, argue that SPA §4.6 and Bill of Sale §4 are cumulative (not in conflict) — the Bill of Sale adds a warranty; it does not replace the SPA representation. The §11.12 "conflict" language should not be read to apply where the provisions are complementary.
- **Judgment call:** This turns on interpretation. If a dispute arises, Delaware courts (governing the SPA) would likely look at the parties' intent. The fact that §11.12 was added in the executed version (not the draft) and that the Bill of Sale's cap language cross-references the SPA's Cap suggests the drafter may have intended the Bill of Sale cap to be the operative limit for asset-title claims. We should be prepared for Seller to take that position.

## Issue 4 – Missing Definitions of "Closing Working Capital" and "Closing Indebtedness"

**Location:** SPA §2.3(a)–(b); §2.2(a); §3.2(a)(iv); §9.2(c); §1.1 (Definitions)

**Facts:**

- "Closing Working Capital" is used in §2.3(a) and (b) as the basis for the purchase price adjustment against a $3,200,000 target, but is **not defined** in §1.1 or anywhere else in the SPA.
- "Closing Indebtedness" is used in §2.2(a) (payoff at Closing), §2.3(a) (reduces the price), §3.2(a)(iv) (payoff letters), and §9.2(c) (indemnity), but is also **not defined** in §1.1.
- The same omissions exist in the draft SPA, so this was not introduced in negotiation — it was a drafting gap from the start.

**Why it matters:** The working capital adjustment is a dollar-for-dollar price mechanism. Without agreed definitions (e.g., which items are current assets, whether restricted cash is included, whether the warranty reserve is netted against current liabilities, what constitutes "indebtedness" — only the Sierra Valley Credit Agreement or also accrued expenses, taxes payable, etc.), the parties are likely to dispute the post-Closing true-up under §2.3(b). The 90-day statement and 30-day objection window make this a near-term practical issue.

**Recommendation:**

- **Before Closing**, negotiate a side letter or amendment that defines "Closing Working Capital" and "Closing Indebtedness" with specificity. At minimum, agree on: (a) the components of current assets and current liabilities, (b) treatment of the warranty reserve, (c) whether the Transferred Assets (which are Seller's, not the Company's) are excluded, and (d) the precise scope of "Closing Indebtedness" (confirm it means only the Sierra Valley Credit Agreement per Schedule 4.3).
- If the parties refuse to amend, document the client's position in writing before Closing to preserve arguments for the §2.3(b) dispute-resolution process.
- **Facts needed:** Confirm with the client's financial advisors what they expect "Closing Working Capital" to include and whether the $3.2M target was negotiated with a particular definition in mind.

## Issue 5 – Shared Cap Between Representation Breaches and Pre-Closing Warranty Claims

**Location:** SPA §9.4(a); §9.2(a) and §9.2(d)

**Facts:**

- The $4,850,000 Cap (10% of Purchase Price) applies to the **combined** losses under §9.2(a) (breach of any Seller representation or warranty) and §9.2(d) (pre-Closing warranty claims).
- The Deductible ($485,000) applies only to §9.2(a), not §9.2(d) — so warranty claims have no deductible, which is favorable to Buyer.
- However, any significant representation breach (e.g., if the Financial Statements turn out to be materially misleading, or if undisclosed litigation emerges) will consume cap headroom that would otherwise be available for warranty claims, and vice versa.

**Why it matters:** Given the known warranty exposure (rising claims, 140 defective installations, $2.14M reserve that may be inadequate), Buyer should expect to use a meaningful portion of the $4.85M cap for pre-Closing warranty claims within 18 months. If a material representation also proves inaccurate, the remaining cap may be insufficient. The 18-month survival period (§9.1) is relatively short for a solar installation business with 10-year warranties — many pre-Closing warranty claims will surface after the survival period expires and will be unrecoverable from Seller.

**Recommendation:**

- This is a structural feature of the deal that cannot be changed post-signing. The client should:
  - (a) Build a robust post-Closing warranty management process to identify and document pre-Closing claims quickly (within the 18-month window);
  - (b) Ensure the §9.1 "claim asserted in writing with reasonable specificity" language is used proactively — if a pattern of pre-Closing warranty defects is identified, put Seller on notice in writing before the 18-month deadline even if individual claims are still developing;
  - (c) Factor the shared-cap risk into the client's insurance and reserve planning.
- **Judgment call:** Whether 18 months is adequate survival for pre-Closing warranty claims is a business decision for the client. The 10-year workmanship warranty means most legacy claims will arise after survival expires. The client accepted this risk by signing; the question is whether it was a knowing acceptance.

## Issue 6 – Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction (SPA vs. Bill of Sale)

**Location:** SPA §§11.8–11.9; Bill of Sale §§7.1–7.2; SPA §11.12

**Facts:**

- The SPA is governed by **Delaware** law with exclusive jurisdiction in the **Delaware Court of Chancery** (SPA §§11.8–11.9).
- The Bill of Sale is governed by **California** law with exclusive jurisdiction in **Fresno County, California** state courts (Bill of Sale §§7.1–7.2).
- SPA §11.12 provides that the Bill of Sale controls over the SPA "as to the Transferred Assets."

**Why it matters:** A single dispute involving the Transferred Assets (e.g., a title defect that also implicates the SPA representation in §4.6, or a pre-Closing liability related to a vehicle) could require litigation in two forums under two bodies of law. This increases cost, creates procedural complexity, and raises the risk of inconsistent outcomes. The jury-trial waiver in the SPA (§11.10) does not appear in the Bill of Sale, so a Fresno County action might proceed to jury.

**Recommendation:**

- This is baked into the executed documents. For practical purposes, the client should assume that Transferred Asset disputes will be litigated in Fresno under California law. Ensure the client's post-Closing legal team has California counsel lined up.
- In any future transaction, insist on a uniform governing law and forum across all deal documents, or at minimum a provision that related claims must be consolidated in a single forum.

## Issue 7 – City of Fresno Business License Expires December 31, 2026

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §6.5; SPA §4.8; §7.2(a)

**Facts:**

- The Company's City of Fresno business license expires **December 31, 2026** (Disclosure Schedule §6.5).
- The SPA outside date is **January 31, 2027** (§8.1(b)), meaning Closing could occur after the license expiration.
- SPA §4.8 represents that the Company "holds all permits and licenses required to conduct the Business as currently conducted."
- SPA §7.2(a) makes it a closing condition that all non-Fundamental representations be true and correct in all material respects as of the Closing Date.

**Why it matters:** If the license is not renewed before Closing, the §4.8 representation may be inaccurate at the Closing Date, potentially giving Buyer the right to refuse to close under §7.2(a). Conversely, if the client wants to close, it may need to waive the condition. The practical risk is low (business license renewals are routine), but the technical issue should be addressed.

**Recommendation:**

- Confirm with the client whether the Fresno business license renewal has been filed. If not, request that Seller file the renewal before Closing and deliver evidence of the new license as a closing deliverable (or as a post-Closing covenant with a short deadline).
- This is a minor item but easy to fix and avoids a technical closing-condition issue.

## Issue 8 – Warranty Reserve May Be Inadequate

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §§9.3–9.6; SPA §4.4, §4.12

**Facts:**

- The Company's warranty reserve as of June 30, 2026 is **$2,140,000** (Disclosure Schedule §9.5).
- Annual warranty costs have increased every year: $611,400 (2023) → $734,800 (2024) → $902,300 (2025) → $688,100 for 9 months of 2026 (annualized ≈ $850,000) (Disclosure Schedule §9.4).
- There are 6,120 residential and 214 commercial installations within the workmanship warranty period, plus 1,480 residential installations in the Performance Plus production warranty program (Disclosure Schedule §9.3).
- 80 of the 140 known flashing-defect installations remain unremediated (Disclosure Schedule §9.6).
- The production warranty guarantees 90% of estimated first-year production declining 0.5%/year for 10 years — a potentially significant liability if underperformance is systemic.

**Why it matters:** If the reserve is inadequate, the Company (now owned by Buyer) will absorb the shortfall as an operating expense post-Closing, reducing cash flow and enterprise value. The reserve also affects the working capital calculation (Issue 4) — if the reserve is a current liability, an under-reserved balance sheet inflates working capital and could trigger a purchase price reduction in Buyer's favor (or, if the client is on the receiving end of the true-up, a payment obligation). More importantly, the adequacy of the reserve bears on whether the §4.4 Financial Statements representation (GAAP, fair presentation) is accurate.

**Recommendation:**

- Request the client's financial advisors perform a quick look at the reserve adequacy using the claims trend data. If the reserve appears materially under-stated, this supports either (a) a working capital adjustment in the client's favor at the §2.3 true-up, or (b) a post-Closing indemnification claim under §9.2(a) for inaccuracy of the §4.4 representation (subject to the Cap and Deductible).
- **Facts needed:** The client's advisors' view on reserve adequacy, and whether the Performance Plus production warranty has any historical underpayment trend.

## Issue 9 – Delgado Litigation: Insurance Reservation of Rights

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §7.1; SPA §9.2(d)

**Facts:**

- *Delgado v. Tenein Rooftop Solar, Inc.* (Fresno County Superior Court, Case No. 26CECG01183) alleges water intrusion and roof damage from a 2024 installation. Claimed damages: $86,500.
- The claim was tendered to the Company's general liability insurer, which accepted defense **under a reservation of rights** (Disclosure Schedule §7.1).

**Why it matters:** A reservation of rights means the insurer may later deny coverage (in whole or in part), leaving the Company responsible for the defense cost and any judgment or settlement. The claim arises from a pre-Closing installation, so it falls within Seller's indemnity under §9.2(d) — but that indemnity is subject to the shared $4.85M Cap (Issue 5). The dollar amount is modest, but the reservation-of-rights posture creates uncertainty.

**Recommendation:**

- Monitor the case. If the insurer issues a formal denial of coverage post-Closing, make a prompt written indemnification claim under §9.2(d) (well within the 18-month survival period).
- Confirm that the Company's insurance policy does not have a change-in-control provision that could affect coverage post-Closing.
- Low priority given the dollar amount, but should be tracked.

## Issue 10 – Draft-to-Executed Changes: Loss of General Lease Performance Covenant

**Location:** Draft SPA §6.4; Executed SPA §6.4

**Facts:**

- **Draft §6.4:** "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to perform its obligations under **each Lease** and to timely exercise any renewal option under the Fresno Warehouse Lease in accordance with its terms. Seller shall deliver to Buyer a copy of any notice given or received by the Company under **any Lease** promptly after it is given or received."
- **Executed §6.4:** "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to exercise the renewal option under the **Fresno Warehouse Lease** in accordance with its terms. Seller shall deliver to Buyer a copy of the renewal notice and any written acknowledgment from **Pellucid Logistics Park LLC**."

The executed version (a) eliminates the general obligation to perform under all leases, and (b) eliminates the notice-delivery obligation for lease communications other than the Fresno renewal.

**Why it matters:** The Company also leases a Sacramento sales office (Disclosure Schedule §8.2). The draft required Seller to ensure the Company performed under that lease and to share any lease notices. The executed version removes that protection. The practical risk is low (the Sacramento lease runs to June 30, 2029 with no renewal option), but it reflects a pattern of Seller-favorable changes in the executed version (see also Issue 2).

**Recommendation:**

- Note for the file. No actionable fix at this point, but if any issue arises under the Sacramento lease pre-Closing, the client has no contractual hook to require Seller to address it (other than the general ordinary-course covenant in §6.1 and the MAE condition in §7.2(d)).
- Use as context when discussing with the client the overall pattern of changes from draft to execution.
