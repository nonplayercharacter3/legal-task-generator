## Issue 1 – Direct Conflict: Buyer Indemnifies Seller for Pre-Closing Warranty Claims That Seller Also Indemnifies Buyer For

**Location:** Executed SPA § 9.2(d) (Seller indemnifies Buyer for pre-Closing warranty claims) vs. § 9.3(c) (Buyer indemnifies Seller for "Assumed Warranty Obligations"); Disclosure Schedule § 9.2 (defining "Assumed Warranty Obligations" to include pre-Closing installations); superseded draft SPA § 9.3 (contains no subsection (c)).

**Why it matters:** Section 9.2(d) provides that Seller indemnifies Buyer for "any workmanship, roof-penetration or production warranty claim, or any related property damage claim, arising out of an installation completed by the Company before the Closing Date." Section 9.3(c) provides that Buyer indemnifies Seller for "the Assumed Warranty Obligations, as defined in Schedule 4.12 of the Disclosure Schedule." That definition (Disclosure Schedule § 9.2) sweeps in "all obligations of the Company under the workmanship, roof-penetration and production warranties … for installations **whether completed before or after the Closing Date**."

These two provisions cover the same ground from opposite directions. For any pre-Closing warranty claim, both Seller (under 9.2(d)) and Buyer (under 9.3(c)) appear to owe an indemnity. The net effect is unclear, but the practical risk to Gleidesk is that Seller will argue 9.3(c) is the operative allocation (Buyer bears the warranty risk), neutralizing 9.2(d).

The superseded draft contains no § 9.3(c). This provision was added between the draft and execution, apparently without conforming changes to § 9.2(d).

**Exposure magnitude:** The installed base within warranty is approximately 6,120 residential and 214 commercial installations (Disclosure Schedule § 9.3). Annual warranty costs have trended upward: $611,400 (2023) → $734,800 (2024) → $902,300 (2025) → $688,100 for nine months of 2026 (Disclosure Schedule § 9.4). The known 140-installation attachment-flashing sealant issue has 80 units still unremediated (Disclosure Schedule § 9.6). The recorded warranty reserve is only $2,140,000 (Disclosure Schedule § 9.5), which may not cover the full run-off of a 10-year warranty on 6,334 installations.

**Recommendation:** This needs to be resolved before Closing if at all possible, or addressed in a side letter or amendment. The fix should either (a) delete § 9.3(c) entirely (restoring the draft's allocation, under which Seller bears pre-Closing warranty risk via 9.2(d) and Buyer bears post-Closing warranty risk as owner of the Company), or (b) if the business deal is that Buyer takes the warranty risk, then § 9.2(d) should be deleted or narrowed to avoid the circularity, and the purchase price should reflect the assumed liability. **This is a judgment call for the client** – we need to understand what was commercially agreed. I would flag to the client that as drafted, the provision is incoherent and creates litigation risk that could swallow the indemnity protection.

## Issue 2 – Fresno Warehouse Lease Renewal Option Has Likely Lapsed

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule § 8.1(d)–(g) (lease expires March 31, 2027; renewal notice required 180 days before expiration; no notice delivered as of schedule date); Executed SPA § 6.4 (requires Seller to exercise renewal "before the Closing"); SPA dated October 20, 2026.

**Why it matters:** The lease requires written notice no later than 180 days before the March 31, 2027 expiration. That deadline falls on or about **October 1, 2026**. The SPA was signed on **October 20, 2026**, and the Disclosure Schedule (delivered the same day) expressly states that "no renewal notice has been delivered" (§ 8.1(g)). Under the lease terms, the option "lapses" if timely notice is not given (§ 8.1(f)).

The Fresno Warehouse is described as "the Company's principal inventory storage, fleet staging and dispatch facility" (Disclosure Schedule § 8.1(b)) – approximately 38,000 rentable square feet. Loss of this facility would be operationally devastating. Section 6.4 of the SPA obligates Seller to exercise the renewal "before the Closing," but if the contractual deadline has already passed, Seller cannot perform. This is not a cure-period issue; the option is gone.

**Recommendation:** We need to confirm the exact deadline calculation with the client and, if possible, with the landlord. Options include: (a) seeking a waiver or extension from Pellucid Logistics Park LLC (the landlord is referenced in the matter but is not a party to the SPA, so we have no contractual leverage over it); (b) negotiating a new lease or holdover terms with Pellucid as a condition to Closing; (c) if the option has truly lapsed and Pellucid will not negotiate, assessing whether this constitutes a failure of the § 7.2(b) condition (material performance of covenants) or a § 7.2(d) MAE, which would give Gleidesk the right to walk. **We need facts from the client:** Has anyone spoken to Pellucid since signing? Is there any informal agreement or extension? What is the financial impact of losing the facility (relocation costs, disruption to dispatch)?

## Issue 3 – Buyer's Indemnity for Assumed Warranty Obligations Is Expressly Uncapped

**Location:** Executed SPA § 9.4(d) (second sentence: "Buyer's obligations under Section 9.3(c) shall not be subject to the Cap or the Deductible"); Disclosure Schedule § 9.2 (broad definition of Assumed Warranty Obligations).

**Why it matters:** Even if the conflict in Issue 1 is resolved in Seller's favor (i.e., 9.3(c) is the operative provision), Gleidesk would bear unlimited financial exposure for all warranty obligations on pre- and post-Closing installations. The Cap is $4,850,000 (10% of Purchase Price). The uncapped nature of this obligation means that if warranty claims exceed the reserve ($2,140,000) plus the Cap, Gleidesk absorbs the difference with no ceiling.

Given the upward trend in annual warranty costs (Issue 1) and the 10-year tail on residential warranties, a systemic issue (like the sealant problem, but worse) could generate claims well into the tens of millions over the warranty period.

**Recommendation:** If the client accepts that it is taking the warranty risk (as the structure implies), we should negotiate (a) a cap or at least a basket on § 9.3(c), (b) a specific indemnity from Seller for the known 140-installation sealant issue (carved out of 9.3(c)), and (c) a purchase price credit or escrow for the unremediated portion. **Judgment call:** The client needs to decide whether it is comfortable with uncapped warranty exposure on a 6,334-installation base with a 10-year tail.

## Issue 4 – "Closing Working Capital" and "Closing Indebtedness" Are Not Defined

**Location:** Executed SPA §§ 2.3(a)–(b), 2.2(a), 3.2(a)(iv), 9.2(c) (all use these terms); § 1.1 (Definitions – neither term appears).

**Why it matters:** The purchase price adjustment mechanism (Section 2.3) turns entirely on these two undefined terms. "Closing Working Capital" is measured against a $3,200,000 target, and "Closing Indebtedness" reduces the price dollar-for-dollar. Without definitions specifying which balance sheet items are included or excluded (e.g., whether deferred revenue, warranty reserves, or intercompany balances are in or out), the post-Closing true-up process is ripe for dispute. The 90-day statement / 30-day objection / independent accountant mechanism in § 2.3(b) will be triggered by exactly the kind of definitional ambiguity that is easiest to avoid upfront.

This gap exists in both the executed SPA and the superseded draft, so it was not introduced in negotiation – it was simply never drafted.

**Recommendation:** If Closing has not yet occurred, we should push for a definition (or a side letter) before the Closing Date. A typical definition would specify: current assets (cash, A/R, inventory) minus current liabilities (A/P, accrued expenses, current portion of debt), with enumerated exclusions (e.g., exclude the warranty reserve, exclude deferred revenue, exclude intercompany balances). For "Closing Indebtedness," we need to confirm whether it means only the Sierra Valley Credit Agreement (the only disclosed debt, per Schedule 4.3 § 4.2 and Schedule 4.7 § 5.4) or any other obligation. **We should confirm with the client whether Closing has occurred or is still pending.**

## Issue 5 – Bill of Sale Cap Conflicts with SPA Fundamental Representation Treatment of Transferred Assets Title

**Location:** Bill of Sale § 4.3 ("Seller's liability for any breach of the warranties in this Section 4 is subject to the Cap set forth in Section 9.4(a)"); Executed SPA § 9.4(b) (Cap does not apply to Fundamental Representations, which include § 4.6 – Title to Transferred Assets); Executed SPA § 11.12 (Bill of Sale controls as to Transferred Assets).

**Why it matters:** If Seller's title to the Transferred Assets is defective (e.g., a vehicle has an undisclosed lien), Gleidesk has two potential claims: (a) breach of SPA § 4.6, a Fundamental Representation, which is uncapped up to the full Purchase Price under § 9.4(b); and (b) breach of Bill of Sale § 4, which is expressly subject to the $4,850,000 Cap under Bill of Sale § 4.3. Section 11.12 provides that "the Bill of Sale controls as to the Transferred Assets." A reasonable reading is that the Bill of Sale's Cap limitation governs title disputes over the Transferred Assets, effectively reducing the remedy from uncapped (up to $48.5M) to capped ($4.85M) for the same underlying defect.

**Recommendation:** We should seek clarification or an amendment stating that the Fundamental Representation treatment in SPA § 9.4(b) applies to title defects in Transferred Assets notwithstanding Bill of Sale § 4.3. Alternatively, if the parties intended the Cap to apply, we should confirm that the $4.85M Cap is adequate given that the Transferred Assets are allocated $2,600,000 of the Purchase Price (Disclosure Schedule § 3: Vehicles $640K + Inventory $1,184.3K + Warehouse Equipment $775.7K). The Cap is roughly 1.85× the asset value, which provides some cushion, but a total title failure on the vehicle fleet (10 vehicles) would be severe.

## Issue 6 – Ambiguity on Survival of Section 9.2(d) Indemnity

**Location:** Executed SPA § 9.1 (representations and warranties survive 18 months post-Closing); § 9.2(d) (Seller indemnifies for pre-Closing warranty claims); Disclosure Schedule § 9.1 (warranties run 5–10 years from permission to operate).

**Why it matters:** Section 9.1 states that "representations and warranties" survive 18 months. Section 9.2(d) is an indemnification obligation, not a representation or warranty per se. However, it is triggered by warranty claims that may not surface for years. If a court reads § 9.1 as limiting all claims under Article IX to 18 months (except Fundamental Reps and Tax reps), then Gleidesk would lose its § 9.2(d) indemnity for pre-Closing warranty claims arising after month 18 – even though the underlying customer warranties run for 10 years.

This issue is somewhat muted if Issue 1 is resolved by deleting § 9.3(c) (restoring Seller's exclusive responsibility for pre-Closing warranty risk). But if § 9.3(c) remains and § 9.2(d) is read as surviving only 18 months, the net effect is that Seller's indemnity for pre-Closing warranties expires at month 18 while Buyer's indemnity to Seller (for the same warranties) is perpetual and uncapped.

**Recommendation:** Seek a clarifying amendment stating that the indemnification obligations in § 9.2(d) survive for the full term of the underlying customer warranties (i.e., up to 10 years post-Closing). If that is not achievable, at minimum confirm that § 9.1's survival period does not truncate § 9.2(d).

## Issue 7 – Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction for Transferred Assets Disputes

**Location:** Bill of Sale § 7.1 (California law) and § 7.2 (exclusive jurisdiction, Fresno County, California); Executed SPA § 11.8 (Delaware law) and § 11.9 (exclusive jurisdiction, Delaware Court of Chancery); Executed SPA § 11.12 (Bill of Sale controls as to Transferred Assets).

**Why it matters:** Any dispute involving the Transferred Assets (title, condition, the Bill of Sale warranties, the power of attorney in Bill of Sale § 5.2, the trust provision in § 5.3) will be governed by California law and litigated in Fresno County. Disputes about the Shares, the purchase price adjustment, covenants, or the SPA representations will be governed by Delaware law and litigated in the Court of Chancery. Gleidesk could find itself defending related claims in two forums under two bodies of law. California and Delaware differ on issues like the economic loss doctrine, implied warranties, and the scope of "good and valid title."

This is not necessarily a defect – it may reflect the reality that the assets are in California and the vehicle titles are California DMV instruments. But it increases cost and complexity.

**Recommendation:** Low priority to fix (the SPA is executed), but the client should be aware that any Transferred Assets dispute will be in Fresno, California, under California law. If the vehicle fleet or warehouse equipment has a title or condition issue, we will need California counsel. I would confirm whether the client has a California litigation relationship.

## Issue 8 – Delgado Litigation: Reservation of Rights Creates Coverage Uncertainty

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule § 7.1 (Delgado v. Tenein Rooftop Solar, Inc., $86,500 claimed, insurer defending under reservation of rights); Executed SPA § 9.2(d) (Seller indemnifies for pre-Closing warranty/property damage claims); § 4.12 (representation on workmanlike installation, qualified by Disclosure Schedule).

**Why it matters:** The Delgado claim (water intrusion and roof damage from a 2024 installation) is a pre-Closing warranty claim that falls within § 9.2(d). However, the insurer's reservation of rights means it could later deny coverage, arguing the damage was not caused by the Company's workmanship or that an exclusion applies. If coverage is denied, the Company (now Gleidesk's subsidiary) would owe the judgment, and Gleidesk would look to Seller's § 9.2(d) indemnity – but that indemnity is subject to the $4,850,000 Cap and the $485,000 Deductible (since § 9.2(d) is within the Cap per § 9.4(a)). If other claims exhaust the Deductible first, the Delgado recovery could be reduced.

The amount ($86,500) is modest, so this is not a deal-critical issue. But the reservation of rights is a watch item.

**Recommendation:** Monitor the case. Confirm with the client whether the reservation of rights letter has been reviewed and what the insurer's stated basis is. If Closing has not yet occurred, consider requiring Seller to deliver the reservation of rights letter and the insurer's coverage position as a Closing deliverable (or a post-Closing covenant). If coverage is denied, ensure the claim is noticed under § 9.5 promptly to preserve the indemnity.

## Issue 9 – Narrowing of Section 6.4 from Draft to Executed: Loss of General Lease Performance Covenant

**Location:** Superseded draft SPA § 6.4 (titled "Leases": requires Seller to cause the Company to "perform its obligations under each Lease" and to deliver copies of all lease notices); Executed SPA § 6.4 (titled "Fresno Warehouse Lease Renewal": requires only exercise of the Fresno renewal and delivery of the renewal notice and Pellucid acknowledgment).

**Why it matters:** The executed version drops the general obligation to perform under all leases and the requirement to deliver copies of any lease notices. The only other lease is the Sacramento Sales Office (Disclosure Schedule § 8.2), which runs to June 30, 2029 with no renewal rights. The risk is low because the Sacramento lease is small (2,900 sq. ft., $7,250/month) and has a long remaining term. But if the Company were in default under the Sacramento lease (e.g., unpaid CAM charges), there would be no specific covenant requiring Seller to cure it pre-Closing – only the general § 6.1 ordinary-course obligation and the § 7.2(b) condition (material performance of covenants).

**Recommendation:** Low priority. If Closing has not yet occurred, we could request confirmation that the Company is not in default under the Sacramento lease (which is already covered by the § 4.10(b) representation). No amendment needed unless the client wants belt-and-suspenders.
