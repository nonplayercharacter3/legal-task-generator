## Issue 1 – Fresno Warehouse Lease Renewal Option Has Already Lapsed

**Location:** Executed SPA §6.4; Disclosure Schedule §8.1(d)–(g); Lease expiration March 31, 2027.

**Facts.** The Fresno Warehouse Lease (38,000 rsf; the Company's principal inventory storage, fleet staging, and dispatch facility) expires March 31, 2027. The tenant's sole five-year renewal option required written notice "no later than one hundred eighty (180) days before the lease expiration date" (Disclosure Schedule §8.1(f)). That deadline was **October 2, 2026**. The SPA was executed **October 20, 2026**—eighteen days after the deadline. The Disclosure Schedule confirms: "As of the date of this Disclosure Schedule, no renewal notice has been delivered" (§8.1(g)). The lease provides that "[i]f the tenant does not deliver timely notice, the option lapses."

SPA §6.4 covenants that "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to exercise the renewal option under the Fresno Warehouse Lease in accordance with its terms." On its face, this covenant is now **impossible to perform** because the option has lapsed by its own terms.

**Why it matters.** Without the renewal, the Company loses its principal operating facility on March 31, 2027—potentially before or shortly after the outside date (January 31, 2027, §8.1(b)). The Transferred Assets (vehicles, inventory, warehouse equipment totaling ~$2.6M per Schedule 2.4) are all located at or staged from the Fresno Warehouse (Disclosure Schedule §§2.2, 2.3). Loss of the facility would disrupt operations, force a costly and time-sensitive relocation, and undermine the value premise of the deal.

**Complicating factor.** Pellucid Logistics Park LLC (the landlord) is not a party to the SPA. We have no visibility into whether Pellucid would voluntarily extend the option deadline, negotiate a new lease, or assert the lapse. The SPA does not require Pellucid consent or address change-of-control provisions in the lease (the Disclosure Schedule is silent on this point).

**Recommendation.**

- Confirm with the client immediately whether any renewal notice was in fact delivered before October 2, 2026 (the Disclosure Schedule says no, but we should verify directly with the Company's management).
- If the option has lapsed, instruct the client to open a dialogue with Pellucid (through Seller, per §6.4) to negotiate either (a) a waiver of the lapse and exercise of the option, (b) a new lease on comparable terms, or (c) a short-term extension to preserve optionality.
- Assess whether Seller's inability to perform §6.4 constitutes a breach that would fail the closing condition in §7.2(b) (covenants performed in all material respects). If the lease is critical to the Business (and the Disclosure Schedule describes it as the "principal" facility), a reasonable argument exists that this is material. This gives the client leverage in any renegotiation.
- Consider whether to require, as a condition to closing, a signed lease extension or renewal from Pellucid. This would require an amendment to the SPA.

**Judgment call / facts needed.** We need to know: (a) whether the client is willing to proceed if Pellucid will not agree to a renewal or extension; (b) whether the client has any independent relationship with Pellucid; (c) the cost and timeline of an alternative facility. These are business decisions that will drive the legal strategy.

## Issue 2 – Warranty Exposure Far Exceeds the Indemnification Backstop

**Location:** Executed SPA §§9.1, 9.2(d), 9.3(c), 9.4(a)–(d); Disclosure Schedule §9 (Schedule 4.12) in its entirety.

**Facts.** The Company has given customers:

- 10-year workmanship warranties (residential) and 5-year (commercial);
- 10-year roof-penetration warranties;
- 10-year production warranties (Performance Plus program only).

As of September 30, 2026, approximately **6,120 residential** and **214 commercial** installations remain within the workmanship warranty period (Disclosure Schedule §9.3). Of the residential installations, 1,480 are in the Performance Plus program.

The "Assumed Warranty Obligations" (defined in Disclosure Schedule §9.2) cover **all** of these obligations "for installations whether completed before or after the Closing Date." Under SPA §9.3(c), **Buyer indemnifies Seller** for the Assumed Warranty Obligations, and under §9.4(d) this indemnity is **not subject to the Cap or the Deductible**—it is uncapped.

On the other side, Seller's indemnity to Buyer for pre-closing warranty claims (§9.2(d)) is:

- Subject to the **$4,850,000 Cap** (§9.4(a)), which is **shared** with all general representation and warranty breaches under §9.2(a); and
- Subject to the **18-month survival period** (§9.1).

The Company's recorded warranty reserve is **$2,140,000** as of June 30, 2026 (Disclosure Schedule §9.5). Actual warranty costs have been trending upward: $611,400 (2023) → $734,800 (2024) → $902,300 (2025) → $688,100 (first 9 months of 2026, annualizing to ~$917,000) (Disclosure Schedule §9.4).

**Why it matters.** Buyer is stepping into a 10-year warranty obligation for over 6,300 installations with an annual run-rate cost of roughly $900,000 and rising. Over the full 10-year warranty tail, cumulative costs could reach **$9 million or more**. Against that:

- The warranty reserve covers less than two and a half years at current run-rate.
- Seller's indemnity expires 18 months post-closing and is capped at $4.85 million **in the aggregate with all other rep/warranty claims**. If, for example, $2 million of the cap is consumed by a financial-statement inaccuracy or a contract breach, only $2.85 million remains for warranty claims.
- After 18 months, Buyer/Company bears 100% of all warranty costs with no recourse to Seller.
- Buyer's own indemnity to Seller for Assumed Warranty Obligations is uncapped (§9.4(d)), meaning if a customer ever pursues Seller directly (e.g., as a prior owner or alter ego), Buyer must backstop that.

This is the single largest economic risk in the transaction.

**Recommendation.**

- Present the client with a quantified exposure model: project warranty costs over 10 years using the disclosed claims trajectory, compare against the reserve and the cap, and show the net uninsured gap.
- Before closing (if still possible) or as a condition to waiving any closing condition, negotiate one or more of the following:
  - **Specific indemnity or escrow** for the known 140-unit sealant issue (see Issue 7 below) and for warranty claims arising from installations completed before a specified date (e.g., before January 1, 2025), with a survival period matching the warranty term.
  - **Extension of the §9.2(d) survival period** to match the warranty term (10 years for residential, 5 years for commercial), or at minimum to 5 years.
  - **Separate cap** for §9.2(d) warranty claims, so they do not erode the cap available for other representation breaches.
  - **Increase in the warranty reserve** as a condition to closing, with the delta reflected in the Purchase Price or working capital target.
- If the deal is already closed or closing is imminent and these points cannot be renegotiated, ensure the client's post-closing integration team has a robust warranty claims tracking and reserving process, and that the Company's insurance program (general liability, and any separate warranty or performance bond coverage) is reviewed for adequacy.

**Judgment call / facts needed.** We need from the client: (a) its risk appetite for the warranty tail; (b) whether it has (or can obtain) insurance that covers the workmanship and roof-penetration warranties; (c) whether the Purchase Price was negotiated with reference to the warranty reserve and claims history (i.e., whether the price already reflects this risk). If the price was set assuming the reserve is adequate, the client may have a commercial argument that the reserve was misstated, which would be a §9.2(a) claim (subject to the cap and deductible).

## Issue 3 – No Escrow or Holdback Mechanism

**Location:** Executed SPA §§2.2, 9.1–9.6 (no escrow provision exists).

**Facts.** The full Purchase Price (less Closing Indebtedness payoff) is wired at closing (§2.2). There is no escrow, holdback, or letter of credit securing Seller's post-closing indemnification obligations. Buyer's sole remedy for a post-closing indemnification claim is to sue Seller for a money judgment.

**Why it matters.** This compounds Issue 2. If warranty claims materialize at the projected rate, Buyer will be pursuing an unsecured claim against Seller (a Delaware LLC whose post-closing assets may be limited, particularly if the Purchase Price has been distributed to its members). The 18-month survival window means Buyer must identify, quantify, and assert claims relatively quickly after closing, with no security backing the obligation.

**Recommendation.**

- If the deal has not yet closed, negotiate a **warranty escrow** of a meaningful amount (e.g., $2–3 million, or an amount equal to the warranty reserve) held for 18 months (or longer if the survival period is extended per Issue 2).
- If the deal is already closed, assess Seller's creditworthiness and post-closing asset position. If Seller is a single-purpose entity that will distribute the proceeds, the practical recoverability of an indemnification judgment may be low. In that case, the client's protection is limited to the insurance program and the Company's own reserves.

**Facts needed.** Whether closing has occurred, and if so, the timing. Also: the structure of Seller (is it a holding company with other assets, or a single-purpose entity?).

## Issue 4 – Westline Distribution Agreement: Consent Required but No Hard Closing Condition

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §4.1 (Schedule 4.3); Executed SPA §§6.3, 7.2(b); Disclosure Schedule §5.1 (Schedule 4.7).

**Facts.** The Distribution Agreement with Westline Solar Distribution, Inc. (dated February 1, 2024) is the Company's **primary module and inverter supply agreement** (Disclosure Schedule §5.1). A change-of-control consent is required (Disclosure Schedule §4.1). SPA §6.3 obligates Seller to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain the consent before closing. However, §7.2 does not list receipt of the Westline consent as a specific closing condition. The only hook is §7.2(b) (covenants performed in all material respects).

**Why it matters.** If the consent is not obtained, two risks arise:

1. **Breach of the Distribution Agreement.** Depending on its terms, a change of control without consent could constitute a default, giving Westline the right to terminate or impose penalties. Loss of the primary supply chain would be operationally devastating.
2. **Adequacy of the "commercially reasonable efforts" standard.** Seller could argue it made commercially reasonable efforts even if Westline refuses or delays. The client would then need to determine whether the failure is "material" enough to trigger §7.2(b). This is a fact-intensive and potentially litigious determination.

**Recommendation.**

- Confirm the current status of the Westline consent. Has it been requested? Is there a response?
- Review the Distribution Agreement (available in the data room per Disclosure Schedule §1.4) to identify the specific change-of-control provision, any termination rights, and the consequences of a technical default.
- If the consent has not been obtained, consider whether to (a) make receipt of the consent an express closing condition via amendment, or (b) at minimum, require Seller to deliver evidence of the consent request and Westline's response as part of the §7.2(c) officer's certificate.
- Assess whether the Company can operate for a meaningful period without Westline (e.g., using inventory on hand or alternative suppliers) to understand the client's walk-away point.

**Facts needed.** Status of the consent request; terms of the change-of-control provision in the Distribution Agreement; whether the client has identified alternative supply sources.

## Issue 5 – "Closing Working Capital" and "Closing Indebtedness" Are Undefined

**Location:** Executed SPA §2.3(a)–(b); §1.1 (Definitions—neither term appears).

**Facts.** The working capital adjustment mechanism is central to the Purchase Price: the price is adjusted dollar-for-dollar based on whether Closing Working Capital exceeds or falls short of the $3,200,000 target, and is reduced by Closing Indebtedness (§2.3(a)). However, neither "Closing Working Capital" nor "Closing Indebtedness" is defined in §1.1 or anywhere else in the SPA. There is no schedule specifying which balance sheet items are included or excluded (e.g., are the Transferred Assets' related receivables included? Is the warranty reserve a working capital item? Are restricted cash or the security deposit under the Fresno lease included?).

**Why it matters.** The post-closing true-up (due within 90 days after closing, §2.3(b)) will require the parties to compute actual Closing Working Capital. Without a definition, the parties are likely to disagree about inclusions and exclusions, potentially leading to the expert determination process (§2.3(b)) or worse. Given that the Transferred Assets include $1.18M of inventory that may be commingled with the Company's operations, the boundary between "Company working capital" and "Seller's asset-related items" is unclear.

**Recommendation.**

- If the deal has not closed, negotiate a definition of "Closing Working Capital" (typically current assets minus current liabilities, with specified exclusions) and "Closing Indebtedness" (typically all interest-bearing debt, with identification of which specific facilities are included). This should be added as a schedule or in §1.1.
- If the deal has closed, prepare now for the true-up. Identify the items most likely to be disputed (inventory valuation, the warranty reserve treatment, the Fresno security deposit, any intercompany balances between Seller and the Company relating to the Transferred Assets) and develop the client's position.
- Note that the allocation provision (§2.4, Schedule 2.4) states that any adjustment "shall be allocated to the Shares." This is clear, but the underlying computation is not.

**Facts needed.** Whether closing has occurred. If so, the preliminary working capital estimate Seller delivered under §2.3(a) and the client's initial reaction to it.

## Issue 6 – Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction for Transferred Assets

**Location:** Executed SPA §§11.8–11.9 (Delaware law; Delaware Court of Chancery); Bill of Sale §§7.1–7.2 (California law; Fresno County state courts); Executed SPA §11.12 (Bill of Sale controls as to Transferred Assets).

**Facts.** SPA §11.12 provides that in the event of a conflict between the SPA and any Ancillary Document, "the Bill of Sale controls as to the Transferred Assets." The Bill of Sale specifies California governing law (§7.1) and exclusive jurisdiction in Fresno County, California (§7.2). The SPA specifies Delaware law (§11.8) and the Delaware Court of Chancery (§11.9).

**Why it matters.** Any dispute involving the Transferred Assets (title, condition, the Bill of Sale's own warranties in §4) will be governed by California law and litigated in Fresno County, while all other disputes (reps and warranties about the Company, covenants, the working capital adjustment, indemnification procedure) will be governed by Delaware law and litigated in Delaware. This creates:

- **Forum complexity.** A single factual dispute (e.g., a vehicle title defect that also implicates the §4.6 Fundamental Representation) could potentially be framed as either a Bill of Sale claim (California) or an SPA indemnification claim (Delaware).
- **Inconsistent outcomes.** California and Delaware law may differ on issues like warranty implied terms, the scope of "good and valid title," or the effect of the no-assumption-of-liabilities clause.
- **Practical burden.** The client, a Delaware corporation, would need California counsel for Transferred Assets disputes.

The Bill of Sale §4.3 attempts to bridge this by subjecting the Bill of Sale's title warranty to the SPA's Cap and procedures, but the underlying substantive law is still California.

**Recommendation.**

- This is baked into the executed documents and cannot be unilaterally changed. However, the client should be aware of the split when evaluating any Transferred Assets issue.
- If a Transferred Assets dispute arises, analyze whether it can be framed as an SPA indemnification claim (Delaware) rather than a Bill of Sale warranty claim (California), as the SPA's indemnification article is more developed (materiality scrape, expert determination, etc.).
- For future deals, recommend a single governing law and forum for all deal documents.

## Issue 7 – 140-Unit Attachment-Flashing Sealant Issue: 80 Units Unremediated

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §9.6 (Schedule 4.12); Executed SPA §§9.2(d), 9.4(a).

**Facts.** The Company has identified an attachment-flashing sealant issue affecting approximately 140 residential installations completed between March and August 2024. As of September 30, 2026, approximately 60 have been remediated. **80 remain.** The Company is remediating under its roof-penetration warranty.

**Why it matters.** This is a known, quantifiable, ongoing cost that will hit the Company post-closing. It is covered by §9.2(d) (pre-closing warranty claims) and thus subject to the shared $4.85M Cap and 18-month survival. If the remediation cost per unit is even $5,000–$10,000 (reasonable for roof work), the remaining 80 units represent $400,000–$800,000 in near-term cost. This will erode the cap available for all other claims.

**Recommendation.**

- Obtain from the client (or the Company) an estimate of the per-unit remediation cost and the total projected cost for the remaining 80 units.
- If the deal has not closed, negotiate a **specific indemnity** for the sealant issue, carved out from the Cap (similar to the Fundamental Representations treatment in §9.4(b)), or a dedicated escrow/holdback equal to the estimated remediation cost.
- If the deal has closed, track the remediation as a §9.2(d) claim and include it in the first indemnification demand. Ensure the claims are documented with reasonable specificity to preserve them under §9.1 (last sentence).

**Facts needed.** Per-unit remediation cost; timeline for completing the remaining 80 units; whether the cost is being borne by the Company (reducing working capital) or by Seller pre-closing.

## Issue 9 – Delgado Litigation: Insurance Reservation of Rights

**Location:** Disclosure Schedule §7.1 (Schedule 4.9); Executed SPA §9.2(d).

**Facts.** *Delgado v. Tenein Rooftop Solar, Inc.* (Fresno County Superior Court, Case No. 26CECG01183) alleges $86,500 in water intrusion and roof damage from a 2024 installation. The claim has been tendered to the Company's general liability insurer, which "accepted defense under a reservation of rights."

**Why it matters.** A reservation of rights means the insurer reserves the right to later deny coverage (in whole or in part) if it determines the claim is excluded (e.g., if it characterizes the damage as a warranty issue rather than third-party liability). If coverage is denied, the Company (and thus Buyer) bears the $86,500 in damages plus defense costs. The claim is covered by §9.2(d) as a pre-closing property damage claim, but it counts against the shared Cap.

The dollar amount is modest, but the reservation of rights is a flag that the insurance program may not be as robust as assumed.

**Recommendation.**

- Obtain a copy of the reservation of rights letter and the insurer's coverage analysis.
- Confirm whether the general liability policy excludes warranty claims (many do). If it does, the Delgado claim may not be covered, and the cost falls to the Company.
- Include this claim in the client's post-closing indemnification tracking. It is a known, quantified item.
- Broader point: review the Company's insurance program (general liability, property, auto, and any warranty-specific coverage) to understand what is actually covered versus what the client is self-insuring.

**Facts needed.** The reservation of rights letter; the Company's insurance policy limits and exclusions; whether the client has reviewed the insurance program in diligence.

## Issue 10 – No Environmental Representation or Warranty

**Location:** Executed SPA Article IV (no environmental section); Disclosure Schedule (no environmental schedule).

**Facts.** The SPA contains no representation regarding environmental compliance, contamination, hazardous materials, or environmental liabilities. The Company operates a warehouse with propane forklifts and a battery charging station (Disclosure Schedule §2.3(a), (d)), and the Transferred Assets include vehicles that may have fuel or fluid leaks.

**Why it matters.** In a stock purchase, Buyer inherits all of the Company's liabilities, including any environmental liabilities, with no specific indemnity backstop. While the risk profile for a solar installation company is lower than for, e.g., a manufacturing or chemical business, the absence of any environmental rep means the client has no contractual recourse if an environmental issue surfaces (e.g., a fuel leak at the warehouse, improper disposal of battery materials).

**Recommendation.**

- If the deal has not closed, consider adding a basic environmental representation (compliance with environmental laws; no known contamination; no pending environmental claims). Even a limited rep would provide an indemnification hook under §9.2(a).
- If the deal has closed, ensure the client's integration team conducts a basic environmental walk-through of the Fresno Warehouse (or its successor facility) and reviews any Phase I ESA that may be in the data room.
- This is a lower-priority item given the nature of the business, but it is a gap that should be noted.

**Facts.** The most recent financial statements are the unaudited balance sheet as of June 30, 2026. The SPA was signed on October 20, 2026—a **four-month gap**. There is no requirement in the SPA for Seller to deliver interim financial statements (e.g., for the third quarter 2026) before closing.

- If the lease is silent on change of control, document that conclusion for the file.

**Facts needed.** The actual lease document. This is in the data room and should be pulled immediately.

## Issue 13 – Purchase Price Allocation: Tax Optimization Question

**Location:** Executed SPA §2.4; Disclosure Schedule §3 (Schedule 2.4).

**Facts.** The $48.5M Purchase Price is allocated as follows: $45.9M to Shares; $640K to Vehicles; $1,184,300 to Inverter and Racking Inventory; $775,700 to Warehouse Equipment. The Transferred Assets represent only **5.4%** of the total price. §2.4 requires the parties to file all tax returns consistently with this allocation.

**Why it matters.** In a stock purchase, the allocation between stock and separately purchased assets affects:

- **Buyer's depreciation/amortization.** The $2.6M in Transferred Assets provides a depreciable basis (vehicles: 5-year MACRS; inventory: expensed or capitalized and written off as sold; warehouse equipment: 7-year MACRS). The $45.9M in stock provides an outside basis that is not depreciable.
- **Future sale.** If Buyer later sells the stock, the outside basis matters. If Buyer later liquidates the Company and sells its assets, the inside basis (which is unaffected by this stock purchase) matters.

The current allocation appears to reflect fair market value, but the client should confirm with its tax advisors whether a different allocation (e.g., shifting more value to depreciable assets) would be more favorable, subject to the requirement that the allocation be consistent with fair market value and the parties' agreement.

**Additional point.** §1.3 allows Buyer to direct the Transferred Assets to itself or to the Company. If assigned to the Company, the Company gets the asset basis (and can depreciate it). If assigned to Buyer directly, Buyer does. This decision has tax and operational implications and should be made deliberately.

**Recommendation.**

- Confirm with the client's tax advisors that the allocation is appropriate and that the parties are comfortable being locked into it for all tax filings (§2.4).
- Make the Buyer/Company designation decision for the Transferred Assets (§1.3) with tax input. Generally, assigning to the Company is operationally cleaner (the assets are used in the Business) and allows the Company to depreciate them.

**Facts needed.** Client's tax advisors' view; the client's intended post-closing structure (will it hold the Company as a subsidiary, or merge it?).

## Issue 14 – Superseded Draft: Removal of General Lease Performance Covenant

**Location:** Superseded Draft §6.4 vs. Executed SPA §6.4.

**Facts.** The September 2026 draft provided: "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to perform its obligations under each Lease and to timely exercise any renewal option under the Fresno Warehouse Lease in accordance with its terms. Seller shall deliver to Buyer a copy of any notice given or received by the Company under any Lease promptly after it is given or received."

The executed SPA narrowed this to: "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to exercise the renewal option under the Fresno Warehouse Lease in accordance with its terms. Seller shall deliver to Buyer a copy of the renewal notice and any written acknowledgment from Pellucid Logistics Park LLC."

The executed version **removed** (a) the general obligation to perform under all leases and (b) the requirement to deliver copies of any lease notices.

**Why it matters.** The Sacramento Sales Office Lease (Disclosure Schedule §8.2) runs through June 30, 2029, so it is less urgent. But the removal of the general lease performance covenant means that if the Company falls into default under any lease between signing and closing (e.g., a late rent payment, a CAM charge dispute), Seller has no specific covenant obligation to cure it. The client's protection would be through the §4.10(b) representation (no material default) and the §7.2(b) closing condition (covenants performed), but these are narrower than the deleted covenant.

This is a relatively minor point given the Sacramento lease's long remaining term, but it reflects a pattern of the executed SPA being slightly less protective of Buyer than the draft.

**Recommendation.**

- Note for the file. If any lease issue arises pre-closing, the client should raise it under §6.6 (notification) and §7.2(b) (closing condition) rather than under a specific lease covenant.
- No action required unless a lease issue actually emerges.
