## Issue 1: Non-Renewal Notice Window Has Likely Passed — Harrowgate May Be Locked Into a Five-Year Renewal

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 2.2

**Explanation:**

The Initial Term is three years from March 1, 2024, expiring February 28, 2027. Section 2.2 provides for automatic renewal for successive five-year terms unless either party gives written notice of non-renewal "not more than 270 days and not less than 240 days before the expiration of the Initial Term." That window was approximately **June 3 – June 23, 2026**.

Amendment No. 1 is dated September 21, 2026 — well after the window closed. Nothing in the matter folder indicates that Harrowgate delivered a non-renewal notice. If no notice was given, the Agreement will **automatically renew for five years** (through February 28, 2032) on March 1, 2027, with all terms continuing to apply, including the expanded exclusivity (Issue 2) and the amended liability cap (Issues 4–5).

**Why it matters:** This is the single most time-sensitive issue. If Harrowgate wanted out at the end of the Initial Term, the opportunity has almost certainly passed. The client is now likely committed to at least five additional years under terms that are materially less favorable than what was originally negotiated (see Issues 2–5 below).

**Recommended fix:**

- **Immediately confirm with the client** whether a non-renewal notice was delivered by June 23, 2026. If not, the renewal is likely binding.
- If the renewal is binding, the client should treat the next renewal cycle (five years from March 1, 2032, with a notice window approximately June 2031) as a hard deadline and calendar it now.
- Consider whether to negotiate a new amendment that (a) shortens the renewal term, (b) adds a termination-for-convenience right, or (c) ties renewal to a price reset.

**Proposed language (if negotiating a new amendment):**

> "2.2 **Renewal.** At the end of the Initial Term, this Agreement shall renew for successive one (1) year terms unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal at least one hundred eighty (180) days before the expiration of the then-current term. On each renewal, the prices in Exhibit B shall be subject to re-negotiation in good faith."

## Issue 2: Exclusivity Scope Is Dramatically Broader Than What Was Negotiated

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 3.1, compared with Term Sheet §§ 2.1–2.2

**Explanation:**

| Dimension | Term Sheet (negotiated) | Supply Agreement (signed) |
|---|---|---|
| Container type | Amber glass bottles only | **All glass and PET containers** |
| Product scope | Beer brands only | **Any beverage product** |
| Geographic scope | U.S. and Canadian facilities only | **Worldwide** |
| Affiliates | Not mentioned | **Expressly bound** |

Term Sheet § 2.2 expressly preserved Customer's freedom to source "all other containers, including containers for non-beer products, containers made from materials other than amber glass, and containers for facilities outside the United States and Canada." The final Supply Agreement § 3.1 eliminates all of those carve-outs.

**Why it matters:** Harrowgate is contractually prohibited from buying any glass or PET container from any third party, for any beverage, anywhere in the world, including through its Affiliates. If the client has (or acquires) a juice, water, or spirits division, or a bottling facility in Europe or Asia, it cannot source containers independently without breaching the Agreement. This is a significant commercial constraint that goes well beyond what the term sheet contemplated.

Additionally, "PET containers" appears in the exclusivity obligation (§ 3.1) but no PET products are listed in Exhibit A. This creates ambiguity: if Harrowgate wants to introduce a PET bottle product, is it obligated to source it from Veltmar (which manufactures glass)? The definition of "Products" in § 1.3 is tied to Exhibit A, but § 3.1's exclusivity is not so limited.

**Recommended fix:**

Negotiate a restatement of § 3.1 that restores the negotiated scope. At minimum, Harrowgate should seek:

> "3.1 **Exclusive Purchase.** During the Term, Customer shall purchase from Supplier, and Supplier shall be Customer's exclusive source of, amber glass containers for beer products manufactured, filled, bottled or packaged by or for Customer at its facilities in the United States and Canada. Customer remains free to source all other containers (including non-amber glass, PET, aluminum, and containers for non-beer products, and containers for facilities outside the United States and Canada) from any supplier."

If the broader scope was a deliberate business decision (e.g., Veltmar agreed to supply PET as well), the client should confirm that Veltmar has the capability and capacity to do so, and that pricing for PET products is addressed in Exhibit B.

**Information needed from client:**
- Does Harrowgate currently source, or plan to source, any PET or non-amber glass containers?
- Does it have or plan to acquire Affiliates that bottle beverages?
- Does it have or plan to open facilities outside the U.S. and Canada?
- Was the expanded exclusivity a deliberate trade-off for something else (e.g., pricing, volume commitments)?

## Issue 3: Amendment No. 1 Removes Recall Costs from the Liability Cap Exclusions

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 2.1 (replacing Supply Agreement § 11.2), read with Supply Agreement §§ 11.1 and 10.2

**Explanation:**

The original § 11.2 excluded from the liability cap:
- (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations (§ 9.1)
- **(b) Recall costs under Section 10**
- (c) Breach of confidentiality (§ 12)
- (d) Gross negligence, fraud, willful misconduct

The amended § 11.2 (effective for claims arising on or after September 21, 2026) excludes only:
- (a) Supplier's indemnity for **third-party IP claims** (§ 9.1(c)) — see Issue 4
- (b) Breach of confidentiality
- (c) Gross negligence, fraud, willful misconduct

**Recall costs are no longer excluded from the cap.** The cap is "fees paid by Customer in the six months before the claim arose" (§ 11.1).

**Why it matters:** A beverage recall can be extremely expensive — the Supply Agreement itself (§ 10.2) contemplates costs including "notification, retrieval, transport, storage and destruction costs, the cost of replacement Products and lost beverage contents, and customer credits and refunds." For a major recall affecting multiple SKUs across multiple markets, costs could easily reach tens or hundreds of millions of dollars. The six-month fee cap (based on current pricing in Exhibit B, roughly in the range of several million dollars depending on volume) would be a fraction of actual recall costs. Harrowgate would bear the excess.

This is a material adverse change from the original agreement and from the term sheet (§ 7.2, which provided that the cap "will not apply to … recall costs").

**Recommended fix:**

Restore the recall cost exclusion. Propose the following amendment to § 11.2:

> "11.2 **Exclusions.** The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1; (b) Supplier's recall cost obligations under Section 10; (c) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (d) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

If Supplier resists full restoration, a fallback position would be to raise the cap specifically for recall costs (e.g., "the cap in Section 11.1 shall not apply to recall costs under Section 10 in excess of [X] dollars") or to require Supplier to maintain recall insurance with a limit sufficient to cover the gap (the current $10M requirement in § 14.1 may be inadequate for a large recall).

## Issue 4: Amendment No. 1 Narrows the Indemnity Cap Exclusion to IP Claims Only

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 2.1 (replacing Supply Agreement § 11.2(a)), read with Supply Agreement § 9.1

**Explanation:**

The original § 11.2(a) excluded **all** of Supplier's indemnity obligations under § 9.1 from the cap. The amended § 11.2(a) excludes only "third-party intellectual property claims under Section 9.1(c)."

This means the following indemnity claims are now **subject to the six-month fee cap**:
- § 9.1(a): Product defects or nonconformity
- § 9.1(b): Personal injury, death, or property damage caused by the Products
- § 9.1(d): Supplier's breach, negligence, or willful misconduct

**Why it matters:** The most likely and most costly indemnity claims in a beverage container supply relationship are product defect and personal injury claims (e.g., a bottle shatters on a filling line, injuring a worker; or a consumer is cut by a defective bottle). These are now capped at six months of fees. For a serious product liability event, that cap may be grossly inadequate.

The term sheet (§ 7.2) provided that the cap "will not apply to Supplier's indemnity obligations" without limitation. The Amendment is a significant departure.

**Recommended fix:**

Restore the full indemnity exclusion, or at minimum exclude personal injury and product defect claims:

> "11.2 **Exclusions.** The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1(a) (product defects and nonconformity) and Section 9.1(b) (personal injury, death, or property damage); (b) Supplier's recall cost obligations under Section 10; (c) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (d) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

## Issue 5: Amendment No. 1 Permits Supplier to Assign Without Customer Consent

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 4.1 (adding new Supply Agreement § 14.4), read with Supply Agreement § 14.3

**Explanation:**

The original § 14.3 required mutual consent for any assignment. The Amendment adds § 14.4, permitting Supplier to assign without Customer's consent to:
- (a) any Affiliate of Supplier; or
- (b) any successor by merger, consolidation, change of control, or sale of all/substantially all assets.

Supplier need only give notice within 30 days **after** the assignment takes effect.

**Why it matters:** Harrowgate negotiated a mutual consent requirement, presumably because it wanted to ensure it was dealing with a known, creditworthy counterparty. Now Veltmar can transfer the entire Agreement — including the pricing, quality, and exclusivity obligations — to an affiliate or acquirer that Harrowgate has not vetted. The notice is after-the-fact, giving Harrowgate no opportunity to object or walk away.

This is particularly concerning in light of the expanded exclusivity (Issue 2): Harrowgate is locked into buying exclusively from an entity it may not have chosen.

**Recommended fix:**

Either restore the mutual consent requirement or add meaningful protections:

> "14.4 **Assignment by Supplier.** Notwithstanding Section 14.3, Supplier may assign this Agreement to (a) an Affiliate of Supplier that has a demonstrated capability to manufacture Products conforming to the Specifications, or (b) a successor to Supplier by merger or consolidation, provided that in each case: (i) Supplier gives Customer at least sixty (60) days' prior written notice identifying the proposed assignee; (ii) the assignee assumes all of Supplier's obligations under this Agreement in writing; and (iii) Customer may terminate this Agreement without penalty by written notice given within thirty (30) days after receipt of the assignment notice if Customer reasonably determines that the assignee does not have the financial or operational capacity to perform Supplier's obligations."

## Issue 6: 14% Price Increase Already Deemed Accepted

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 5.4; Amendment No. 1, Recital C

**Explanation:**

Recital C of the Amendment states that Supplier issued a Price Adjustment Notice on July 30, 2026, for a 14% increase effective October 2, 2026. Customer received it on August 3, 2026. The 30-day objection period under § 5.4 expired on **September 2, 2026**. The Amendment is dated September 21, 2026, and confirms that "No objection to the Price Adjustment Notice has been delivered by Customer."

Under § 5.4, the increase is now "deemed accepted by Customer and takes effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice" (October 2, 2026).

**Why it matters:** The 14% increase is binding. There is no mechanism in the Agreement to challenge it after the objection period has passed. The client should be aware that this is the new baseline pricing going forward.

Additionally, § 5.3 contains **no cap on the percentage** of any price adjustment. Supplier could theoretically issue another 14% (or larger) increase in the next 12-month period (on or after October 2, 2027), and the only protection is the 30-day objection window.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Going forward:** Calendar the objection deadline for any future Price Adjustment Notice the day it is received. The window is only 30 days.
- **Negotiate a cap:** Add a limitation on the magnitude of adjustments, for example:

> "5.3 **Price Adjustments.** … No single price adjustment shall exceed five percent (5%) of the then-current price for any Product, and the cumulative adjustment over any consecutive twenty-four (24) month period shall not exceed eight percent (8%)."

- **Consider a most-favored-customer or indexation clause** tying adjustments to a published index (e.g., PPI for glass or a composite of energy and raw material costs) rather than leaving them to Supplier's unilateral determination with "reasonable supporting detail."

## Issue 7: Limited Remedies for Late or Non-Delivery

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 4.5, 11.1, 11.3

**Explanation:**

If Supplier delivers more than five business days late, Customer's express remedy is to "cancel the affected portion of the Purchase Order without liability" (§ 4.5). Beyond that:

- The consequential damages waiver (§ 11.3) bars recovery of lost profits, lost production, or downstream customer claims.
- The liability cap (§ 11.1) limits total recovery to six months of fees.
- There is no express right to cover (buy substitute goods at Supplier's expense).
- The force majeure exception (§ 15.7) permits third-party sourcing only after 30 days of a force majeure event, not for ordinary supply failures.

**Why it matters:** For a bottler, a container shortage can idle filling lines, breach downstream distribution commitments, and create brand damage. The current remedy structure leaves Harrowgate with very little recourse short of termination for material breach (§ 13.1), which requires a 30-day cure period and is a nuclear option.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a cover right and a liquidated damages or service-level mechanism:

> "4.5 **Late Delivery.** If Supplier fails to deliver any Products within five (5) business days after the agreed delivery date, Customer may, in addition to its other remedies: (a) cancel the affected portion of the Purchase Order without liability; (b) purchase substitute Products from a third party and recover from Supplier the reasonable excess cost of such substitute purchase over the price in Exhibit B; or (c) if the delay continues for more than fifteen (15) business days, terminate the affected Purchase Order and any Purchase Orders for the same Product for the following sixty (60) days without liability."

> "11.3 **Consequential Damages.** … Notwithstanding the foregoing, Customer's right to recover the excess cost of substitute purchase under Section 4.5(b) shall not be deemed consequential damages."

## Issue 8: Firm Forecast Creates a Take-or-Pay Obligation Without a Corresponding Supplier Commitment

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 3.2, 3.3, 4.1

**Explanation:**

Section 3.2 makes the first three months of each rolling 12-month forecast "firm and binding on Customer." Section 4.1 prohibits Supplier from rejecting a Purchase Order "consistent with the firm portion of Customer's forecast." However:

- There is no stated remedy if Customer fails to purchase the firm forecasted quantity (e.g., if demand drops).
- Section 3.3 requires Supplier to maintain 115% capacity, but there is no stated remedy if it does not (short of the general material breach termination in § 13.1).
- The obligations are asymmetric in practice: Customer is "bound" to the forecast, but Supplier's capacity obligation lacks an enforcement mechanism.

**Why it matters:** If Harrowgate's beer sales decline (e.g., due to market conditions, a competitor's promotion, or a shift to canned), it may still be contractually committed to the firm forecast quantities. Conversely, if Veltmar under-invests in capacity, Harrowgate's only remedy is to argue material breach and wait 30 days for cure.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a cap on Customer's firm forecast liability (e.g., "Customer's obligation under the firm forecast shall not exceed [X]% of the forecasted quantity for any Product in any month").
- Add a specific remedy for Supplier's failure to maintain capacity:

> "3.3 **Capacity.** … If Supplier fails to maintain the capacity required by this Section 3.3 and such failure results in Supplier's inability to deliver Products within the time required by an accepted Purchase Order, Supplier shall reimburse Customer for the reasonable excess cost of Customer's purchase of substitute Products from a third party for the duration of the shortfall."

## Issue 9: No PET Products in Exhibit A Despite PET Exclusivity

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 3.1 (exclusivity covers "all glass and PET containers") vs. Exhibit A (lists only glass bottles)

**Explanation:**

The exclusivity obligation in § 3.1 covers "all glass and PET containers," but Exhibit A lists only three amber glass beer bottles. There are no PET products, no pricing for PET, and no specifications for PET in either the Supply Agreement or the Quality Agreement.

**Why it matters:** This creates a gap. If Harrowgate introduces a PET product (e.g., a water or sports drink line), § 3.1 would appear to require it to source PET from Veltmar — a glass manufacturer. Veltmar has no contractual obligation to supply PET (no product, no price, no spec), but Harrowgate may have a contractual prohibition on buying PET from anyone else. This is a potential trap.

**Recommended fix:**

Either (a) remove "PET" from § 3.1, or (b) if PET supply is intended, add PET products to Exhibit A with pricing and specifications, and confirm Veltmar's capability. At minimum, add a clarifying sentence:

> "For the avoidance of doubt, Supplier's supply obligation under this Agreement extends only to Products listed in Exhibit A. The exclusivity in this Section 3.1 applies only to the container types and products for which Supplier has a supply obligation under this Agreement."

## Issue 10: Order of Precedence May Create Ambiguity with the Amendment

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 15.2; Amendment No. 1 § 5.2

**Explanation:**

Section 15.2 provides: "written amendments, then Supply Agreement, then Quality Agreement." Amendment § 5.2 confirms it is a "written amendment for the purposes of Sections 15.2 and 15.3." This is internally consistent.

However, the Amendment's amended § 11.2 cross-references "Section 11.3" (the consequential damages waiver) and "Section 9.1(c)" (IP indemnity). If a future amendment modifies § 9.1 or § 11.3, the interaction with the current amended § 11.2 could become unclear.

**Why it matters:** This is a lower-priority drafting hygiene issue, but it could matter in a dispute over whether a particular claim falls inside or outside the cap.

**Recommended fix:** No immediate action required, but in any future amendment, ensure cross-references are updated.

## Issue 11: Quality Agreement AQL for Critical Defects — Confirm Operational Feasibility

**Where:** Quality Agreement § 4.3

**Explanation:**

The AQL for Critical Defects is "0.0 (zero tolerance)" under ANSI/ASQ Z1.4, General Inspection Level II. Under that standard, a zero AQL means the acceptance number is zero: any single Critical Defect found in the sample results in lot rejection.

**Why it matters:** This is a very strict standard. In practice, with large lot sizes, even a well-controlled process may occasionally produce a single critical defect (e.g., a glass inclusion). A zero-tolerance standard means any such event triggers lot rejection, replacement, and potential line stoppage at Harrowgate's facility. The client should confirm that its incoming inspection team understands and can operate under this standard, and that Supplier is aware of the operational implications.

**Recommended fix:** No contractual change needed if the zero-tolerance standard was deliberately negotiated. However, the client should ensure its QA team is trained on the sampling plan and that the nonconformance process (§ 6.2–6.4 of the Quality Agreement) is operationally ready. Consider whether a "one-in-lot" exception with mandatory Supplier corrective action (rather than automatic rejection) would be more practical, though this would weaken the protection.

## Issue 12: Insurance Requirements May Be Inadequate in Light of Amended Cap

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 14.1; Amendment No. 1 § 2.1

**Explanation:**

Section 14.1 requires Supplier to maintain $10M per occurrence in products liability and product recall coverage. Before the Amendment, this insurance was a backstop for uncapped recall and indemnity obligations. After the Amendment, recall costs and most indemnity claims are subject to the six-month fee cap, which may be well below $10M.

**Why it matters:** The insurance is now more important as a practical source of recovery, but the contractual right to recover from Supplier is capped. If a recall costs $50M and the cap is $5M, Harrowgate can recover at most $5M from Supplier under the Agreement. The insurance policy (naming Harrowgate as additional insured) may provide a direct recovery path, but the client should confirm:
- That the policy is actually in force and compliant with § 14.1.
- That the additional insured status provides direct coverage (not just a right to seek contribution).
- That the policy limits are sufficient for the client's risk profile.

**Recommended fix:**

- Request a certificate of insurance from Veltmar confirming current coverage.
- In any renegotiation, either restore the uncapped recall/indemnity obligations (Issues 3–4) or increase the insurance requirement to a level that reflects the client's actual recall risk (e.g., $25M or $50M per occurrence).
- Add a requirement that Supplier provide notice of any material change or cancellation of its insurance policy.
