## Issue 1 – Exclusivity Scope Dramatically Expanded Beyond Term Sheet

**Where:** Supply Agreement §3.1, compared with Term Sheet §§2.1–2.2.

**What the Term Sheet provided:**

- Exclusive supply limited to **amber glass bottles** (Term Sheet §2.1).
- Limited to **beer brands** only (Term Sheet §2.1).
- Limited to **U.S. and Canadian facilities** only (Term Sheet §2.1).
- Customer expressly retained the right to source all other containers from any supplier, including non-beer products, non-amber-glass materials, and facilities outside the U.S. and Canada (Term Sheet §2.2).

**What the Supply Agreement provides:**

> "During the Term, Customer shall purchase from Supplier, and Supplier shall be Customer's exclusive source of, **all glass and PET containers** for **any beverage product** manufactured, filled, bottled or packaged by or for **Customer or its Affiliates**, **worldwide**." (Supply Agreement §3.1)

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

The exclusivity obligation in the executed agreement is materially broader than what was discussed in the Term Sheet in at least four dimensions:

| Dimension | Term Sheet | Supply Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Product type | Amber glass bottles | All glass **and PET** containers |
| Beverage scope | Beer brands | **Any** beverage product |
| Geographic scope | U.S. and Canada | **Worldwide** |
| Entity scope | Customer | Customer **and its Affiliates** |

If Harrowgate bottles soft drinks, water, or any non-beer product—or operates filling facilities in Europe, Latin America, or Asia—it is contractually prohibited from sourcing containers from any other supplier. This could lock Harrowgate into a single-source dependency for its entire global container needs, with no ability to diversify for cost, quality, or supply-chain resilience reasons.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend §3.1 to restore the scope discussed in the Term Sheet, or at minimum to carve out the categories Harrowgate needs. Proposed replacement language:

> "3.1 **Exclusive Purchase.** During the Term, Supplier shall be Customer's exclusive source of amber glass containers for beer products manufactured, filled, bottled or packaged by Customer at its facilities in the United States and Canada. Customer shall not purchase amber glass containers for such beer products from any third party for such facilities during the Term. For the avoidance of doubt, Customer retains the right to source (a) containers made from materials other than amber glass, (b) containers for non-beer beverage products, and (c) containers for facilities located outside the United States and Canada, from any supplier."

If the broader scope was a deliberate commercial decision, we need to confirm that with the client and assess whether the pricing in Exhibit B adequately compensates for the expanded obligation.

**Information needed from client:** Was the expansion from the Term Sheet to the Supply Agreement a negotiated trade-off (e.g., for the pricing in Exhibit B), or was it a drafting error? Does Harrowgate currently source any containers from third parties for non-beer products, non-amber-glass products, or international facilities?

## Issue 2 – Amendment No. 1 Eliminates Recall Costs and Most Indemnity Claims from the Liability Cap Exclusions

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 §2.1 (replacing Supply Agreement §11.2), read with Supply Agreement §§11.1, 9.1, and 10.

**Original Supply Agreement §11.2 (pre-Amendment):**

> "The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1; (b) **Recall costs under Section 10**; (c) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (d) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

**Amended §11.2 (per Amendment No. 1 §2.1):**

> "The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1, **which are excluded solely to third-party intellectual property claims under Section 9.1(c)**; (b) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (c) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

**What changed:**

| Exclusion | Pre-Amendment | Post-Amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier's indemnity (§9.1) – all sub-sections | Uncapped | Capped, **except** only §9.1(c) (IP) remains uncapped |
| Recall costs (§10) | Uncapped | **Now capped** |
| Breach of confidentiality (§12) | Uncapped | Uncapped (unchanged) |
| Gross negligence / fraud / willful misconduct | Uncapped | Uncapped (unchanged) |

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

The cap in §11.1 is "the fees paid by Customer in the six months before the claim arose." For a beverage container supply relationship, this is a relatively modest number. The practical effect of the Amendment is:

1. **Recall costs are now capped.** If a defective batch causes a product recall, Supplier's reimbursement obligation under §10.2 (which includes notification, retrieval, transport, storage, destruction, replacement products, lost beverage contents, and customer credits) is now subject to the six-month fees cap. A single recall of a national beverage brand can easily cost many multiples of six months of container purchases.

2. **Product defect and personal injury indemnity is now capped.** Claims under §9.1(a) (defects/nonconformity) and §9.1(b) (personal injury, death, property damage) are no longer excluded from the cap. For a glass container used in beverages, the most serious real-world risk is a bottle failure causing consumer injury. Harrowgate's indemnity recovery for such a claim is now capped.

3. **Only IP infringement remains uncapped.** This is the least likely and least costly type of claim in a glass bottle supply relationship.

The net effect is that Harrowgate's most significant risk exposures—product recalls and consumer injury—are now protected only by a modest contractual cap and by Supplier's insurance (see Issue 8 below).

**Recommended fix:**

Restore the pre-Amendment exclusions. Proposed replacement for §11.2:

> "11.2 **Exclusions.** The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1; (b) Recall costs under Section 10; (c) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (d) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

If Supplier resists a full restoration, the minimum acceptable position for Harrowgate should be to carve out at least (b) Recall costs under Section 10 and (a) to the extent arising from §9.1(a) or §9.1(b).

**Information needed from client:** What was the commercial rationale for accepting this Amendment? Was there consideration (e.g., a price concession, a new product line, a volume commitment) that justified the trade-off? If the Amendment was signed under time pressure or in connection with the Price Adjustment Notice (see Issue 5), the client should be aware of the full cost of that trade.

## Issue 3 – Automatic Renewal with a Narrow 30-Day Notice Window

**Where:** Supply Agreement §2.2.

**What it provides:**

> "At the end of the Initial Term, this Agreement shall automatically renew for successive five-year Renewal Terms, five years each … unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal **not more than 270 days and not less than 240 days** before the expiration of the Initial Term or the then-current Renewal Term."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

- The Initial Term expires **February 28, 2027** (three years from March 1, 2024).
- The non-renewal notice window is approximately **June 3, 2026 to July 3, 2026** (270 to 240 days before February 28, 2027).
- If no notice was given in that 30-day window, the Agreement has **already auto-renewed** for a five-year term running through **February 28, 2032**.
- The Term Sheet (§3.2) stated that "any renewal mechanics will be negotiated in the Definitive Agreement." The final agreement imposed automatic five-year renewals without any indication that this was a negotiated term.
- The window is narrow (30 days), and the consequence of missing it (either by sending notice too early or too late) is a five-year lock-in.

**Recommended fix:**

If the client has not yet sent non-renewal notice and the window has passed, the immediate question is whether the Agreement has already renewed (see Information Needed below). Going forward, if the client wishes to retain flexibility, consider negotiating:

> "2.2 **Renewal.** At the end of the Initial Term, this Agreement shall renew for successive one-year Renewal Terms unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal not less than one hundred eighty (180) days before the expiration of the then-current term."

Or, if five-year renewals are acceptable, widen the notice window:

> "…unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal not more than **365** days and not less than **180** days before the expiration of the Initial Term or the then-current Renewal Term."

**Information needed from client (URGENT):** Did Harrowgate deliver a non-renewal notice to Veltmar between approximately June 3, 2026 and July 3, 2026? If not, the Agreement has likely auto-renewed through February 28, 2032, and the client should be aware of that commitment before relying on the contract going forward.

## Issue 4 – Amendment No. 1 Permits Supplier to Assign Without Customer Consent

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 §4.1 (new Supply Agreement §14.4), read with original Supply Agreement §14.3.

**What it provides:**

> "14.4 **Assignment by Supplier.** Notwithstanding Section 14.3, Supplier may assign or transfer this Agreement, in whole or in part, or any of its rights or obligations under it, without the consent of Customer, to (a) any Affiliate of Supplier; or (b) any successor to Supplier by merger, consolidation, change of control or sale of all or substantially all of the business or assets of Supplier to which this Agreement relates. Supplier shall give Customer written notice of any such assignment within thirty (30) days after it takes effect."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

- The original §14.3 required mutual consent for any assignment. The Amendment unilaterally grants Supplier a free assignment right.
- "Change of control" is not defined in the Agreement. The defined term "Affiliate" (§1.1) uses a 50% voting equity threshold, but "change of control" in §14.4 could be interpreted more broadly (e.g., a sale of a majority stake, a management buyout, or even a shift in board control).
- The notice is **after the fact** (within 30 days after the assignment takes effect). Harrowgate has no opportunity to consent, object, or terminate.
- There is no requirement that the assignee assume the Agreement's obligations, have equivalent financial capacity, or maintain the same quality certifications.
- Combined with the exclusivity obligation (Issue 1), Harrowgate could find itself locked into a supply relationship with an unknown entity that it never chose.

**Recommended fix:**

If the client cannot get the Amendment reverted, at minimum add protective language:

> "14.4 **Assignment by Supplier.** Notwithstanding Section 14.3, Supplier may assign this Agreement without Customer's consent to (a) an Affiliate of Supplier, or (b) a successor by merger or consolidation, provided that (i) the assignee is a corporation organized under the laws of the United States or its states with net worth of not less than [amount], (ii) the assignee assumes in writing all of Supplier's obligations under this Agreement, and (iii) Supplier gives Customer written notice at least thirty (30) days before the assignment takes effect. Supplier shall not assign this Agreement by operation of law or by sale of assets without Customer's prior written consent. If an assignee fails to perform any obligation under this Agreement, Supplier shall remain jointly and severally liable for such performance for a period of twelve (12) months after the assignment."

**Information needed from client:** Was this assignment right a negotiated term in the Amendment? Does the client have any concern about Veltmar's ownership or financial stability that would make this right a practical risk?

## Issue 5 – 14% Price Increase Already Deemed Accepted

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recital C; Supply Agreement §§5.3–5.4.

**Facts as stated in the documents:**

- Supplier issued a Price Adjustment Notice on **July 30, 2026**, providing for a **14% increase** in Exhibit B prices, effective **October 2, 2026**.
- Customer received the notice on **August 3, 2026**.
- The objection deadline under §5.4 was **30 days after receipt**, i.e., **September 2, 2026**.
- Amendment No. 1 (dated **September 21, 2026**) states in Recital C: "No objection to the Price Adjustment Notice has been delivered by Customer, and the Price Adjustment Notice remains subject to Section 5.4 of the Agreement."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

Under §5.4: "Any price adjustment to which Customer does not object within the period stated in this Section 5.4 **is deemed accepted** by Customer and takes effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice."

The 14% increase is therefore **in effect as of October 2, 2026**. The new prices are approximately:

| Item | Old Price/Gross | New Price/Gross (14% increase) |
|---|---|---|
| A-1 | $38.40 | ~$43.78 |
| A-2 | $61.20 | ~$69.77 |
| A-3 | $36.10 | ~$41.15 |

The client is now paying these increased prices. The deemed-acceptance mechanism means there is no longer a contractual basis to contest the increase under the Agreement.

**Recommended fix:**

There is no contractual fix available under the current documents—the increase is effective. However:

1. **Confirm the internal timeline.** Determine why no objection was filed by September 2, 2026. If there was a good-faith basis for objection that was simply missed due to an internal process failure, assess whether a course-of-dealing or equitable argument exists (though this would be weak under Delaware law given the express deemed-acceptance clause).

2. **For the future:** Implement a calendar-based compliance process to ensure any Price Adjustment Notice is reviewed and either accepted or objected to well within the 30-day window. Consider whether the objection period should be extended in any future amendment.

3. **Assess whether the 14% increase is supported.** Section 5.3 requires "reasonable supporting detail of the cost changes on which it is based." If the supporting detail was inadequate, there may be an argument that the notice was defective under §5.3, though this argument is complicated by the deemed-acceptance provision.

**Information needed from client:** What was the supporting detail provided in the July 30, 2026 Price Adjustment Notice? Was it internally reviewed? Why was no objection filed by September 2, 2026? Is the 14% increase within the range the client anticipated for input cost inflation?

## Issue 6 – No Convenience Termination Right for Customer

**Where:** Supply Agreement §13 (Termination).

**What it provides:**

Termination is available only for:
- Material breach with 30-day cure period (§13.1); or
- Insolvency/bankruptcy (§13.2).

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

There is no right to terminate for convenience. Combined with:
- The broad exclusivity (Issue 1),
- The automatic five-year renewals (Issue 3), and
- The firm forecast commitment (§3.2),

Harrowgate has no exit unless Veltmar materially breaches or becomes insolvent. If the market changes, if a better supplier emerges, or if Harrowgate's product portfolio shifts, it is locked in for the duration of the then-current term (potentially five years).

**Recommended fix:**

Add a convenience termination right with a reasonable notice period and transition assistance:

> "13.4 **Termination for Convenience by Customer.** Customer may terminate this Agreement for convenience upon not less than twelve (12) months' written notice to Supplier. Upon such termination, Customer shall (a) purchase all Products included in the firm portion of the then-current forecast for the notice period, and (b) pay a transition fee equal to [X]% of the forecasted purchase value for the notice period, to compensate Supplier for stranded capacity."

## Issue 7 – No Delivery Performance Metrics or Liquidated Damages

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§4.3, 4.5; Term Sheet §4.3.

**What it provides:**

- Delivery is FCA Supplier's facility on the date stated in the accepted PO (§4.3).
- If delivery is more than **five (5) business days** late, Customer may cancel the affected portion of the PO (§4.5).
- There is no liquidated damages provision, no on-time delivery percentage target, and no penalty for delivery that is 1–5 days late.

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

- A beverage bottling line has significant fixed costs. Even a one-day delay in container delivery can force a line shutdown, with costs that far exceed the value of the containers themselves.
- The only remedy for chronic lateness (but always under 5 business days) is nothing. Customer must accept late delivery without compensation.
- The right to cancel under §4.5 is of limited use given the exclusivity obligation: if Customer cancels a PO, it cannot replace the containers from a third party (except in the narrow force majeure scenario in §15.7).

**Recommended fix:**

Add a delivery performance provision:

> "4.6 **Delivery Performance.** Supplier shall achieve an on-time delivery rate of not less than ninety-five percent (95%) measured monthly. If Supplier fails to meet this threshold in any month, Customer shall be entitled to a credit of [X]% of the value of the late-delivered Products for that month, in addition to any other remedy available under this Agreement. If Supplier fails to meet the on-time delivery rate in three (3) or more months in any consecutive six-month period, Customer may terminate this Agreement under Section 13.1 without a cure period."

## Issue 8 – Insurance May Be Harrowgate's Primary Recourse for Recalls and Injury Claims Post-Amendment

**Where:** Supply Agreement §14.1; Amendment No. 1 §2.1; Supply Agreement §10.

**What it provides:**

> "During the Term and for three (3) years after its end, Supplier shall maintain commercial general liability insurance, including products liability and product recall coverage, with limits of not less than $10,000,000 per occurrence, and shall name Customer as an additional insured."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

Because Amendment No. 1 has capped recall costs and most indemnity claims (Issue 2), the $10M insurance policy is now effectively Harrowgate's **primary financial protection** against product recalls and consumer injury claims, rather than a backstop. Key considerations:

- $10M per occurrence may be insufficient for a large-scale recall of a national beverage brand (recalls can easily exceed $10M when lost contents, customer credits, and brand damage are included).
- The Agreement does not require Supplier to provide certificates of insurance, to maintain the policy with a rated carrier, or to give notice of cancellation or material change.
- "Additional insured" status may not extend to Harrowgate's Affiliates (the policy language would need to be reviewed).
- There is no requirement that the policy include a waiver of subrogation in favor of Customer.

**Recommended fix:**

Strengthen §14.1:

> "14.1 **Insurance.** During the Term and for three (3) years after its end, Supplier shall maintain, with a carrier rated A- or better by A.M. Best, commercial general liability insurance including products liability and product recall coverage, with limits of not less than **$25,000,000** per occurrence and **$50,000,000** in the aggregate, and shall name Customer and its Affiliates as additional insureds. The policy shall include a waiver of subrogation in favor of Customer and its Affiliates. Supplier shall provide certificates of insurance evidencing the foregoing to Customer upon request and shall give Customer at least thirty (30) days' prior written notice of any cancellation, non-renewal, or material reduction in coverage."

## Issue 9 – "Minor Defect" Is Not Defined in the Quality Agreement

**Where:** Quality Agreement §1.2 (Definitions) and §4.3 (AQL Table).

**What it provides:**

Section 1.2 defines "Critical Defect" and "Major Defect" but does **not** define "Minor Defect." However, §4.3 sets an AQL of 2.5 for "Minor Defect."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

Without a definition of "Minor Defect," there is no objective standard for when a defect falls into this category. Supplier could argue that a defect that would be "Major" under Harrowgate's interpretation is merely "Minor," subjecting it to the more lenient AQL of 2.5 rather than 0.65. This creates ambiguity that Supplier could exploit in a quality dispute.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a definition to Quality Agreement §1.2:

> "(f) '**Minor Defect**' means a defect that does not constitute a Critical Defect or a Major Defect and that is unlikely to cause a failure on Customer's filling or packaging lines or to materially impair the appearance or performance of the Product, including minor cosmetic imperfections that do not affect the structural integrity or functionality of the Product."

## Issue 10 – Closures May Be Captured by the Exclusivity Obligation

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§1.2, 3.1.

**What it provides:**

- §1.2 defines "Containers" to include "associated closures **where supplied by Supplier**."
- §3.1 prohibits Customer from purchasing "any such containers" (i.e., glass and PET containers) from any third party.

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

If Supplier currently supplies closures (caps, crowns, etc.) to Harrowgate, those closures are "Containers" by definition, and the exclusivity in §3.1 would prohibit Harrowgate from sourcing closures from any other supplier. The Term Sheet made no reference to closures. If Harrowgate wishes to source closures from a specialized closure manufacturer (which is common in the beverage industry), the current language may prevent it.

**Recommended fix:**

Either:
- Add an express carve-out in §3.1: "For the avoidance of doubt, the exclusivity obligation in this Section 3.1 does not apply to closures unless specifically identified in a Purchase Order as Products supplied by Supplier."
- Or amend the definition in §1.2 to exclude closures from the exclusivity scope: "Containers means bottles, jars and other containers for holding beverage products. Closures supplied by Supplier are Products but are not subject to the exclusivity obligation in Section 3.1."

**Information needed from client:** Does Veltmar currently supply closures to Harrowgate? Does Harrowgate wish to retain the freedom to source closures from third parties?

## Issue 11 – No Explicit Remedy for Supplier's Failure to Maintain Required Capacity

**Where:** Supply Agreement §3.3.

**What it provides:**

> "Supplier shall maintain production capacity sufficient to meet one hundred fifteen percent (115%) of Customer's most recent forecast. Supplier shall notify Customer promptly if it anticipates any shortfall in its ability to supply."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

The obligation to maintain 115% capacity is stated, but the Agreement does not specify:
- What constitutes a breach of this obligation (is it a one-time shortfall or a pattern?).
- What Customer's remedy is if Supplier fails to maintain capacity (other than the general material breach termination in §13.1, which requires a 30-day cure period).
- Whether Customer may source from a third party if Supplier is short on capacity (the force majeure carve-out in §15.7 only applies to force majeure events, not to ordinary capacity shortfalls).

In practice, if Supplier's capacity is insufficient for reasons other than force majeure (e.g., equipment failure, labor shortage, demand from other customers), Harrowgate may be forced to accept reduced deliveries with no right to cover from a third party and no liquidated damages.

**Recommended fix:**

Add to §3.3:

> "If Supplier fails to maintain the capacity required by this Section 3.3 for a period exceeding fifteen (15) consecutive days for reasons other than force majeure, Customer may, without breaching Section 3.1, purchase the shortfall from third parties at Supplier's cost for the duration of the shortfall, and Supplier shall reimburse Customer for any price differential between the third-party price and the price that would have applied under this Agreement."

## Issue 12 – Deemed Acceptance Mechanism for Price Adjustments Creates Asymmetric Risk

**Where:** Supply Agreement §5.4.

**What it provides:**

> "Any price adjustment to which Customer does not object within the period stated in this Section 5.4 is deemed accepted by Customer and takes effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice."

**Why it matters for Harrowgate:**

The 30-day objection window is short for a procurement function that may need to model the impact of a price increase, seek internal approvals, and potentially negotiate. The consequence of missing the deadline is automatic acceptance with no cure. This is an asymmetric mechanism: Supplier has no obligation to justify the increase beyond "reasonable supporting detail," and Customer bears all the risk of inaction.

This issue is not hypothetical—it has already materialized (see Issue 5).

**Recommended fix:**

If the client wishes to retain the current structure but reduce the risk:

> "5.4 **Objection.** Customer may object to a Price Adjustment Notice by written notice to Supplier given within **forty-five (45)** days after Customer's receipt of a Price Adjustment Notice. If Customer objects in time, the Parties shall negotiate in good faith for thirty (30) days, and the existing prices shall remain in effect during that period. If the Parties do not agree within that period, either Party may refer the matter for resolution under Section 15.6. **No price adjustment shall be deemed accepted by Customer unless Customer has affirmatively confirmed in writing that it accepts the adjustment or has failed to object within the period stated in this Section 5.4 after receiving a written reminder from Supplier at least five (5) business days before the expiration of the objection period.**"

The reminder requirement ensures that a missed deadline is not purely the result of an internal oversight.
