## ISSUE 1 — Auto-Renewal Window Has Likely Passed; Harrowgate May Be Locked In for Five More Years

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 2.2.

**Analysis.** The Initial Term is three years from March 1, 2024, expiring approximately February 28, 2027. Section 2.2 provides that the Agreement "shall automatically renew for successive five-year Renewal Terms" unless a Party gives written notice of non-renewal "not more than 270 days and not less than 240 days before the expiration of the Initial Term." That is a 30-day window falling approximately between mid-June and mid-July 2026.

Amendment No. 1 is dated September 21, 2026 — well after that window closed. Nothing in the matter folder indicates that a non-renewal notice was delivered. If none was given, the Agreement will auto-renew on March 1, 2027 for a five-year term (through approximately February 28, 2032), and the next non-renewal window will not open until approximately mid-June 2031.

The Term Sheet (§ 3.2) stated only that "any renewal mechanics will be negotiated in the Definitive Agreement." The five-year auto-renewal with a 30-day notice window was never part of the agreed commercial terms.

**Why it matters.** Harrowgate may be contractually committed to an exclusive supply relationship (see Issue 4) for five additional years without having affirmatively chosen that outcome. The narrow 30-day window creates a recurring trap: missing it by a single day locks the Company in for another five years.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Immediate:** Confirm with the client whether a non-renewal notice was given in the June–July 2026 window. If not, the Company should evaluate whether to negotiate a short-term extension or amendment that resets the renewal mechanics, or to accept the renewal and plan for the 2031 window.
- **Going forward (if the relationship continues):** Amend § 2.2 to (a) extend the notice window to a reasonable period (e.g., "not less than 180 days and not more than 270 days before expiration") and (b) reduce the renewal term from five years to one or two years. Proposed replacement language:

> "2.2 **Renewal.** At the end of the Initial Term, this Agreement shall automatically renew for successive one-year Renewal Terms unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal at least one hundred eighty (180) days before the expiration of the Initial Term or the then-current Renewal Term."

## ISSUE 2 — 14% Price Increase Is Deemed Accepted and Takes Effect October 2, 2026

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recital C; Supply Agreement §§ 5.3–5.4.

**Analysis.** Recital C of Amendment No. 1 states that Supplier issued a Price Adjustment Notice on July 30, 2026, providing a 14% increase effective October 2, 2026. Customer received the notice on August 3, 2026. Under § 5.4, Customer had 30 days from receipt (i.e., until September 2, 2026) to object. The Amendment is dated September 21, 2026, and Recital C confirms: "No objection to the Price Adjustment Notice has been delivered by Customer." Under § 5.4, a Price Adjustment Notice to which Customer does not object in time "is deemed accepted by Customer and takes effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice."

The 60-day minimum lead time under § 5.3 is satisfied (August 3 + 60 days = October 2, 2026).

**Why it matters.** The 14% increase is now contractually binding and takes effect October 2, 2026. Harrowgate cannot dispute it under the Agreement's objection mechanism. The client should be aware that its cost base is increasing by 14% on the next billing cycle and should confirm whether this has been reflected in its pricing or margin models.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Immediate:** Confirm with the client whether the failure to object was an oversight or a deliberate business decision. If it was an oversight, assess whether there is any basis to argue the notice was defective (e.g., insufficient "reasonable supporting detail" under § 5.3) — though this is a weak position given the Amendment's ratification language.
- **Going forward:** Consider amending § 5.3 to (a) impose a cap on any single adjustment (e.g., "no price adjustment shall exceed five percent (5%) in any twelve-month period") and (b) extend the objection window from 30 to 45 days to reduce the risk of missing the deadline.

## ISSUE 3 — Amendment No. 1 Strips Recall Costs and Most Indemnity Claims from the Liability-Cap Carve-Outs

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 2.1 (replacing Supply Agreement § 11.2); Supply Agreement §§ 11.1, 11.3.

**Analysis.** The original § 11.2 excluded the following from the liability cap and consequential-damages waiver:

| Carve-out | Original § 11.2 | Amended § 11.2 (Amendment No. 1, § 2.1) |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier's indemnity (§ 9.1) | All of § 9.1 | Only § 9.1(c) (IP claims) |
| Recall costs (§ 10) | Yes | **Removed** |
| Breach of confidentiality (§ 12) | Yes | Yes |
| Gross negligence, fraud, willful misconduct | Yes | Yes |

The practical effect: if a product defect causes a recall costing $20 million, or a defective bottle causes personal injury giving rise to a third-party claim, Harrowgate's recovery is now capped at the fees paid in the six months before the claim arose (Supply Agreement § 11.1) and subject to the consequential-damages exclusion (§ 11.3). Only IP infringement claims under § 9.1(c) remain uncapped.

**Why it matters.** For a beverage bottler, product recalls and personal-injury claims are the most severe and most likely risk scenarios. Capping these at six months of fees could leave Harrowgate significantly under-compensated. The Term Sheet (§ 7.2) expressly provided that the cap "will not apply to Supplier's indemnity obligations, recall costs, breaches of confidentiality, or gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct" — the Amendment reverses what was the agreed commercial position.

**Recommended fix.**

- Negotiate to restore the original carve-outs. Proposed replacement for § 11.2:

> "11.2 **Exclusions.** The limitation in Section 11.1 and the exclusion in Section 11.3 do not apply to (a) Supplier's indemnity obligations under Section 9.1; (b) Recall costs under Section 10; (c) either Party's breach of Section 12; or (d) a Party's gross negligence, fraud or willful misconduct."

- If Supplier resists full restoration, as a fallback, carve out at minimum (i) recall costs under § 10 and (ii) Supplier's indemnity for personal injury or death under § 9.1(b).

## ISSUE 4 — Exclusivity Scope Is Far Broader Than the Term Sheet

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 3.1; Term Sheet §§ 2.1–2.2.

**Analysis.**

| Dimension | Term Sheet | Supply Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Product type | Amber glass bottles | All glass **and PET** containers |
| Beverage scope | Beer brands | Any beverage product |
| Geographic scope | U.S. and Canadian facilities | Worldwide |
| Entity scope | Customer | Customer **and its Affiliates** |
| Carve-outs | Express right to source all other containers, non-beer, non-amber-glass, and non-U.S./Canada (§ 2.2) | None |

The final Agreement eliminated every carve-out that the Term Sheet preserved. Harrowgate is now contractually barred from purchasing any glass or PET container — for any beverage, anywhere in the world, for itself or any Affiliate — from any source other than Veltmar.

**Why it matters.** If Harrowgate launches a new beverage line, acquires a company with its own packaging supply chain, or wants to use PET for a product currently in glass, it must use Veltmar. The exclusivity runs for the full Term (potentially 8+ years given Issue 1). This is a significant operational and strategic constraint that appears to go well beyond what was commercially agreed.

**Recommended fix.**

- Amend § 3.1 to restore the Term Sheet's scope or a negotiated middle ground. For example:

> "3.1 **Exclusive Purchase.** During the Term, Customer shall purchase from Supplier, and Supplier shall be Customer's exclusive source of, amber glass containers for beer products manufactured, filled, bottled or packaged by Customer at its facilities in the United States and Canada. Customer may purchase all other containers, including containers for non-beer products, containers made from materials other than amber glass, and containers for facilities outside the United States and Canada, from any supplier."

- If the broader scope was a deliberate business decision, document the commercial rationale and ensure the client understands the strategic lock-in, particularly in combination with the auto-renewal (Issue 1).

## ISSUE 5 — No Right to Cover for Late Delivery (Outside Force Majeure)

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 3.1, 4.5, 15.7.

**Analysis.** Section 4.5 gives Customer the right to cancel the late portion of a Purchase Order if delivery is more than five business days late. Section 15.7 permits Customer to buy from third parties only if a force majeure event affecting Supplier continues for more than 30 days. There is no provision allowing Customer to source from a third party (or claim cover damages) when Supplier simply fails to deliver on time for reasons other than force majeure — even if the failure is chronic.

Because of the exclusivity obligation in § 3.1, Customer cannot simply buy from another supplier to make up a shortfall without breaching the Agreement.

**Why it matters.** If Veltmar experiences a production issue (short of force majeure) that delays delivery by two weeks, Harrowgate's only contractual remedy is to cancel the affected PO portion. It cannot buy replacement containers from a third party, and it has no liquidated-damages or cover-damages claim. For a bottling operation with fixed production schedules, this could mean idle filling lines and lost revenue with no contractual recourse.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add a cover/procurement right for chronic or significant delivery failures. Proposed new § 4.6:

> "4.6 **Cover.** If Supplier fails to deliver Products in accordance with an accepted Purchase Order for reasons other than force majeure, and the failure continues for more than ten (10) business days, Customer may, without relieving Supplier of its obligations under this Agreement, purchase equivalent Products from a third party for the duration of the delay. Supplier shall reimburse Customer for the reasonable cost differential between the third-party purchase price and the price under this Agreement, up to the cap in Section 11.1."

- Alternatively, add a delivery-performance service level (e.g., 97% on-time delivery measured monthly) with a price-credit remedy for failure to meet it.

## ISSUE 6 — Supplier May Assign the Agreement Without Customer's Consent

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 4.1 (adding Supply Agreement § 14.4).

**Analysis.** New § 14.4 permits Supplier to assign the Agreement, in whole or in part, without Customer's consent to (a) any Affiliate of Supplier or (b) any successor by merger, consolidation, change of control, or sale of substantially all assets. The only protection is that Supplier must give Customer written notice "within thirty (30) days after it takes effect" — i.e., after the fact.

**Why it matters.** Harrowgate could find itself in an exclusive, multi-year supply relationship with an unknown entity — a newly acquired subsidiary, a financial-sponsor-owned successor, or a competitor's affiliate — with no right to object or terminate. The after-the-fact notice provides no opportunity to assess creditworthiness, quality capability, or strategic compatibility before the assignment occurs.

**Recommended fix.**

- Amend § 14.4 to require **prior** notice and to give Customer a termination right if the assignee does not meet minimum criteria. Proposed replacement:

> "14.4 **Assignment by Supplier.** Notwithstanding Section 14.3, Supplier may assign this Agreement to (a) an Affiliate of Supplier that is a glass or container manufacturer, or (b) a successor to Supplier by merger, consolidation, change of control, or sale of all or substantially all of the related business or assets, provided that Supplier gives Customer written notice at least thirty (30) days before the assignment takes effect, identifying the assignee and confirming that the assignee (i) has manufacturing capacity sufficient to meet Customer's requirements under this Agreement, and (ii) is not in bankruptcy or insolvency proceedings. If the assignee does not meet these conditions, Customer may terminate this Agreement by written notice within sixty (60) days after the assignment without liability."

## ISSUE 7 — No Cap on the Percentage of a Price Adjustment

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 5.3.

**Analysis.** Section 5.3 permits Supplier to adjust prices "not more than once in any twelve (12) month period" with no stated ceiling on the percentage of increase. The current 14% increase (Issue 2) is within this unlimited right. There is no requirement that the adjustment be tied to a specific index (e.g., PPI, raw material costs) or capped at a maximum percentage.

**Why it matters.** In a period of input-cost inflation, Supplier could seek a 20% or 30% increase in the next 12-month window. Harrowgate's only protection is the 30-day objection and good-faith negotiation process, which (as Issue 2 demonstrates) can be missed.

**Recommended fix.**

- Amend § 5.3 to add a cap and/or indexation. For example:

> "5.3 **Price Adjustments.** Supplier may adjust the prices in Exhibit B not more than once in any twelve (12) month period by giving Customer written notice of the adjustment, the effective date of the adjustment and reasonable supporting detail of the cost changes on which it is based (a 'Price Adjustment Notice'). No single price adjustment shall exceed five percent (5%), and the cumulative increase over the Initial Term shall not exceed fifteen percent (15%) unless the adjustment is directly attributable to a change in applicable law or a documented increase in the Supplier's cost of raw materials (cullet, soda ash, limestone) as evidenced by published industry indices. No price adjustment shall take effect earlier than sixty (60) days after Customer's receipt of the Price Adjustment Notice."

## ISSUE 8 — No Consequence for Supplier's Failure to Maintain Quality Certifications

**Where:** Quality Agreement § 3.1.

**Analysis.** Section 3.1 requires Supplier to maintain ISO 9001:2015 certification and a GFSI-recognized food safety management system "throughout the Term." However, no provision in the Quality Agreement or the Supply Agreement specifies a remedy if Supplier's certification lapses. There is no termination right, no cure period, and no explicit breach designation.

**Why it matters.** A lapsed certification could indicate systemic quality-management failures. Without a contractual hook, Harrowgate would need to argue that the lapse constitutes a "material breach" under Supply Agreement § 13.1 — a fact-intensive and uncertain argument.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add to Quality Agreement § 3.1:

> "If Supplier's ISO 9001:2015 or GFSI certification lapses or is suspended, Supplier shall notify Customer within five (5) business days and shall restore the certification within ninety (90) days. If the certification is not restored within that period, Customer may terminate the Supply Agreement by written notice under Section 13.1, and the lapse shall be deemed a material breach for purposes of that Section."

## ISSUE 9 — No Transition Assistance on Termination

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 13.3.

**Analysis.** Section 13.3 provides that on termination or expiration, "Supplier shall fulfil all accepted Purchase Orders unless Customer instructs otherwise." There is no obligation on Supplier to provide transition assistance — such as making available excess inventory, sharing tooling or mold access, providing a wind-down supply period, or cooperating with a successor supplier's qualification.

**Why it matters.** Given the exclusivity and the multi-year term, if the relationship ends (by termination or non-renewal), Harrowgate needs time to qualify a new supplier. Without a transition obligation, Supplier could deliver the last accepted PO and walk away, leaving Harrowgate with a supply gap while it sources and qualifies a replacement.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add a new § 13.4:

> "13.4 **Transition Assistance.** For a period of twelve (12) months following termination or expiration of this Agreement, Supplier shall, at Customer's request and at the then-current prices, continue to supply Products in quantities not exceeding Customer's average monthly purchases during the final six (6) months of the Term. During this period, Supplier shall cooperate reasonably with Customer's qualification of a successor supplier, including providing specifications, test data and sample Products as reasonably requested."

## ISSUE 10 — Ambiguity in "Firm and Binding" Forecast Obligation

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 3.2, 4.1.

**Analysis.** Section 3.2 states that the first three months of each rolling forecast are "firm and binding on Customer." Section 4.1 provides that Supplier "shall not reject a Purchase Order that is consistent with the firm portion of Customer's forecast." However, the Agreement does not state what happens if Customer's actual Purchase Orders fall below the firm forecast quantity. There is no express take-or-pay obligation, no minimum purchase commitment, and no liquidated-damages provision tied to forecast shortfalls.

**Why it matters.** The ambiguity cuts both ways, but in practice it is more likely to be asserted against Harrowgate. If Supplier argues that "firm and binding" means Customer is obligated to purchase the full forecasted quantity (and is liable for the difference if it orders less), Harrowgate could face a claim for the value of un-ordered containers. Conversely, if the term means only that Supplier cannot reject conforming POs (as § 4.1 suggests), then Customer has no purchase obligation beyond the POs it actually places.

**Recommended fix.**

- Clarify in § 3.2 that the firm forecast creates no purchase obligation beyond actual Purchase Orders. Proposed addition:

> "The firm portion of the forecast is binding on Customer solely in the sense that Supplier shall not reject Purchase Orders that are consistent with it. The forecast does not obligate Customer to purchase any minimum quantity of Products, and Customer shall have no liability for the difference between the forecasted quantity and the quantity actually ordered."

## ISSUE 11 — Interaction Between Liability Cap and Supplier's Insurance Obligation

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 11.1, 14.1; Amendment No. 1 § 2.1.

**Analysis.** Section 14.1 requires Supplier to maintain $10 million per occurrence in products liability and product recall insurance, naming Customer as an additional insured. However, as noted in Issue 3, the Amendment now subjects recall costs and most indemnity claims to the six-month-fee cap in § 11.1. If a recall costs $30 million but the six-month fee cap is $8 million, Harrowgate's contractual recovery is limited to $8 million. The insurance policy (with its $10 million limit) may provide a direct claim path as an additional insured, but the interaction between the contractual cap and the insurance policy is not addressed in the Agreement.

**Why it matters.** Harrowgate may face a situation where its contractual recovery is capped below the actual loss, and it must pursue the insurance policy directly — a process that may be slower, more adversarial, and subject to the insurer's own coverage defenses.

**Recommended fix.**

- This is best resolved by fixing Issue 3 (restoring the carve-outs). If the cap remains, add a provision clarifying that the insurance obligation is in addition to (not in lieu of) Supplier's indemnity and recall-cost obligations, and that the cap does not limit Customer's rights as an additional insured under the policy.

## ISSUE 12 — 30-Day Inspection Window May Be Tight for Stored Inventory

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 7.1.

**Analysis.** Customer has 30 days after delivery to inspect and reject nonconforming Products. For a bottler that receives containers and stores them before the filling run, 30 days may be insufficient to complete inspection, particularly if the product is in a storage queue.

**Mitigation.** Section 7.2 provides that "Acceptance of Products does not waive any warranty or other right of Customer," and the product warranty in § 8.1 runs for 18 months after delivery. So even if the 30-day rejection window passes, Harrowgate can still assert a warranty claim. The practical difference is that the 30-day rejection right is the cleaner remedy (immediate replacement or credit under § 7.2), while a warranty claim may involve more dispute over causation and timing.

**Recommended fix.**

- Extend the inspection window to 60 days, or tie it to the date of use rather than delivery:

> "7.1 **Inspection.** Customer may inspect the Products within sixty (60) days after delivery, or within thirty (30) days after first use of the Products in Customer's filling or packaging operations, whichever is later."

## ISSUE 13 — "Containers" Definition Includes Closures, but Exclusivity Refers Only to "Glass and PET Containers"

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 1.2, 3.1.

**Analysis.** Section 1.2 defines "Containers" to include "associated closures where supplied by Supplier." However, § 3.1's exclusivity obligation covers "all glass and PET containers" — a phrase that does not incorporate the defined term "Containers" and that on its face would not include metal or plastic closures. If Supplier currently supplies closures (e.g., crown caps), it is unclear whether those are within the exclusivity scope.

**Why it matters.** If Harrowgate wants to source closures from a third party while buying bottles from Veltmar, the current drafting likely permits this (closures are not "glass or PET containers"). But if the commercial intent was for Veltmar to be the exclusive source of closures as well, the Agreement does not achieve that. Conversely, if Harrowgate intends to source closures independently, the ambiguity could invite a dispute.

**Recommended fix.**

- Confirm with the client whether closures are in scope. If they should be excluded, add to § 3.1: "For purposes of this Section 3.1, 'containers' does not include closures." If they should be included, amend § 3.1 to use the defined term: "all Containers (as defined in Section 1.2)."

## ISSUE 14 — No Delivery Performance Metrics or Chronic-Underperformance Remedy

**Where:** Supply Agreement, generally.

**Analysis.** The Agreement contains no service-level targets (e.g., on-time delivery percentage, fill-rate) and no remedy for chronic underperformance that falls short of a "material breach" under § 13.1. A supplier that is 90% on-time but misses critical delivery windows repeatedly may not be in material breach, yet the operational impact on Harrowgate's production schedule could be significant.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add a delivery-performance clause with a price-credit remedy:

> "4.7 **Delivery Performance.** Supplier shall deliver at least ninety-seven percent (97%) of Products by the agreed delivery date, measured on a rolling monthly basis. For each month in which the on-time delivery rate falls below ninety-seven percent (97%), Customer shall be entitled to a credit of one percent (1%) of the value of Products delivered in that month for each percentage point below the target, up to a maximum credit of five percent (5%) of the monthly value."
