## Summary

I have reviewed the Asset Purchase Agreement (APA), Bill of Sale and Assignment and Assumption Agreement (Bill of Sale), Indemnity Escrow Agreement (Escrow Agreement), and Transition Services Agreement (TSA) for the asset sale from Marrowind Packaging Corp. ("Marrowind" or "Seller") to Tessaly Holdings LLC ("Tessaly" or "Buyer"). I have identified **eleven issues** requiring attention before we return the package to the other side. The three most significant are: (1) a likely drafting error in the APA that eliminates the indemnity cap and basket for all breach-of-representation claims; (2) the Bill of Sale's definition of "Purchased Assets" is broader than the APA's and fails to exclude the Excluded Assets; and (3) the TSA is governed by Delaware law while every other document is governed by New York law.

---

## Issue 1 – APA § 9.4(c): Cap and Basket Carve-Out Appears to Apply to All Representation Breaches, Not Just Fundamental Representations

**Documents and Sections:** APA § 9.4(a) (cap), § 9.4(b) (basket), § 9.4(c) (exceptions).

**The Problem.** Section 9.4(c) provides:

> "The limitations set forth in Section 9.4(a) and Section 9.4(b) shall not apply to **any claim under Section 9.2(a)**, or to any claim arising from fraud."

Section 9.2(a) covers *any* breach of or inaccuracy in *any* representation or warranty made by Seller. Read as written, this means the 10% cap ($1,840,000) and the $184,000 basket do not apply to **any** breach-of-representation claim—not just Fundamental Representations. The cap and basket would protect Marrowind only against covenant breaches (§ 9.2(b)) and Excluded Asset/Liability claims (§ 9.2(c)), which are far less common.

In a standard structure, the carve-out from the cap and basket is limited to Fundamental Representations (defined in APA § 1.1(m) as Organization, Authority, Title, and Brokers) and fraud. The current drafting almost certainly reflects a drafting error—the drafter likely intended to write "any claim under Section 9.2(a) **based on a Fundamental Representation**" or "any claim arising from a breach of a Fundamental Representation."

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** As drafted, Marrowind's indemnity exposure for the most common category of post-closing claim (inaccurate representations) is **uncapped and has no basket**. A single material misstatement in the financial statements representation (§ 4.7), the condition-of-assets representation (§ 4.6), or the compliance-with-laws representation (§ 4.10) could expose Marrowind to a claim well in excess of the $1,840,000 escrow, with no threshold to clear. Combined with APA § 9.6 (source of recovery: escrow first, then Seller directly), Marrowind's direct, uncapped exposure begins the moment the escrow is exhausted.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend § 9.4(c) to:

> "(c) *Exceptions.* The limitations set forth in Section 9.4(a) and Section 9.4(b) shall not apply to (i) any claim under Section 9.2(a) based on a breach of a Fundamental Representation, or (ii) any claim arising from fraud."

This preserves the negotiated protections while carving out only the categories where unlimited liability is market-standard.

## Issue 2 – Bill of Sale § 1.1(b): "Purchased Assets" Defined More Broadly Than in the APA and Without an Excluded-Assets Carve-Out

**Documents and Sections:** Bill of Sale § 1.1(b); APA § 2.1 (definition of Purchased Assets), § 2.2 (Excluded Assets), § 10.12 (order of precedence).

**The Problem.** The APA defines "Purchased Assets" in § 2.1 as "all assets **used or held for use in the Business**, **other than the Excluded Assets**." The Bill of Sale redefines the term in § 1.1(b) as:

> "all of Seller's right, title and interest in, to and under **all assets, properties and rights of every kind and nature, whether real, personal or mixed, tangible or intangible, wherever located**, used or held for use in the Business."

Two discrepancies:

1. **No Excluded-Assets carve-out.** The Bill of Sale definition omits the "other than the Excluded Assets" language. The Excluded Assets (APA § 2.2) are Seller's cash, the Albany manufacturing facility, and the HOLLIS trademark. As drafted, the Bill of Sale could be read to convey all three to Buyer.

2. **Broader scope.** The Bill of Sale adds "whether real, personal or mixed" and "wherever located," which are not in the APA definition. The reference to "real" property is particularly concerning because the Albany facility is real property. If the facility is "used or held for use in the Business" (which it almost certainly is, being a manufacturing facility for the Business), the Bill of Sale definition sweeps it in.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** The Bill of Sale is the operative conveyance document. If its definition of "Purchased Assets" controls over the APA's (see Issue 7 below regarding the order-of-precedence provision), Marrowind could inadvertently convey its cash, its Albany manufacturing facility, and its HOLLIS trademark to Buyer. Even if the APA's definition ultimately controls, the ambiguity creates litigation risk and could be exploited by Buyer in a post-closing dispute.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend Bill of Sale § 1.1(b) to track the APA definition exactly:

> "(b) '**Purchased Assets**' means all of Seller's right, title and interest in, to and under all assets used or held for use in the Business, **other than the Excluded Assets**, including: …"

Additionally, add a new sentence at the end of § 1.1(b):

> "For the avoidance of doubt, the Excluded Assets (as defined in Section 2.2 of the Purchase Agreement) are not Purchased Assets and are not conveyed by this Bill of Sale."

## Issue 3 – TSA § 9.3: Governing Law and Jurisdiction Inconsistent with All Other Transaction Documents

**Documents and Sections:** TSA § 9.3; APA § 10.8 (NY governing law), § 10.9 (NY County jurisdiction); Bill of Sale § 5.5 (NY), § 5.6 (NY County); Escrow Agreement § 7.1 (NY), § 7.2 (NY County).

**The Problem.** Every document in the deal package is governed by New York law and submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of New York County courts, **except** the TSA, which selects **Delaware** law and **Delaware** courts (TSA § 9.3). There is no apparent business reason for this discrepancy—Marrowind is a New York corporation, the Business operates in New York, and the TSA services (ERP, payroll, procurement, customer service) will be performed from Marrowind's New York facilities.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.**

- *Litigation cost and inconvenience.* Any dispute over the TSA (e.g., a fee dispute, a service-level disagreement, or a data-return dispute under § 6.2) would require Marrowind to litigate in Delaware, incurring travel and local-counsel costs.
- *Inconsistent interpretation.* Delaware and New York courts may interpret key terms (e.g., "material breach" in the termination provision, "reasonable" in the cooperation covenants, or the scope of the liability cap) differently. A fact pattern could yield one result in a New York APA claim and a different result in a Delaware TSA claim arising from the same underlying facts.
- *Strategic disadvantage.* Buyer, a Delaware LLC, may have a home-court advantage in Delaware proceedings.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend TSA § 9.3 to:

> "**9.3 Governing Law; Jurisdiction.** This Agreement, and all claims or causes of action arising out of or relating to this Agreement or the Services, shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of **New York**, without giving effect to any choice or conflict of law provision or rule that would cause the application of the laws of any other jurisdiction. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the State and federal courts located in **New York County, New York** for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement or the Services. Each Party waives any objection to venue in those courts, including any objection based on inconvenient forum."

## Issue 4 – Bill of Sale § 1.1(b)(iv): Express Reference to "Trademarks, Trade Names" Without Carving Out HOLLIS

**Documents and Sections:** Bill of Sale § 1.1(b)(iv); APA § 2.1(d), § 2.2.

**The Problem.** The APA's IP provision (§ 2.1(d)) says "all intellectual property used or held for use in the Business, including customer dies and print plates"—it does not expressly mention trademarks. The Excluded Assets (§ 2.2) then carve out "the HOLLIS trademark." The Bill of Sale, however, states in § 1.1(b)(iv):

> "all intellectual property, **including trademarks, trade names**, customer dies and print plates"

By expressly naming "trademarks" and "trade names" in the conveyance definition without any reference to the HOLLIS exclusion, the Bill of Sale creates a direct textual conflict with APA § 2.2. A Buyer's counsel could argue that the specific reference to "trademarks" in the Bill of Sale overrides the general exclusion of HOLLIS in the APA.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** The HOLLIS trademark is presumably a brand Marrowind intends to use with its retained Albany facility or in a new line of products. If the Bill of Sale is read to convey HOLLIS, Marrowind loses its brand identity at the most critical transition period.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend Bill of Sale § 1.1(b)(iv) to:

> "(iv) all intellectual property used or held for use in the Business, including customer dies and print plates, **other than the HOLLIS trademark (which is an Excluded Asset)**;"

## Issue 5 – Bill of Sale § 3.4: Non-Assignable Contracts Create Ongoing Exposure for Marrowind

**Documents and Sections:** Bill of Sale § 3.4; APA § 2.3 (Assumed Liabilities), § 6.3 (consent efforts), § 9.2(c) (indemnity for Excluded Liabilities).

**The Problem.** For Assigned Contracts that cannot be assigned without third-party consent, § 3.4 provides that:

- Seller "shall hold the Assigned Contract for the benefit of Buyer";
- "Buyer shall perform Seller's obligations under that contract arising after the Closing, **to the extent those obligations would have been Assumed Liabilities**"; and
- Seller continues to use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain consent.

Two concerns:

1. **Marrowind remains the contractual obligor.** Until consent is obtained, Marrowind is still the party to the contract. If Buyer fails to perform (or disputes whether an obligation "would have been" an Assumed Liability), the counterparty can enforce against Marrowind directly. Marrowind would then need to seek indemnity from Buyer under APA § 9.3, subject to the Buyer cap in § 9.4(d) ($1,840,000) and the exclusive-remedy provision in § 9.8.

2. **"Would have been Assumed Liabilities" is ambiguous.** APA § 2.3 defines Assumed Liabilities as (a) post-Closing liabilities under Assigned Contracts (other than pre-Closing breaches) and (b) trade accounts payable in the ordinary course reflected in the Closing statement. The phrase "would have been" invites disputes about hypothetical classification—e.g., if a contract obligation is ambiguous as to whether it arises "under the Assigned Contract" or is a separate obligation.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** Marrowind could be on the hook to third-party counterparties for Buyer's non-performance on contracts that were supposed to be assigned. The indemnity from Buyer is capped and subject to the exclusive-remedy provision, so Marrowind's recovery may be incomplete.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend Bill of Sale § 3.4 to add:

> "Buyer shall indemnify and hold harmless Seller from and against all Losses arising from any claim by a counterparty to a non-assignable Assigned Contract to the extent caused by Buyer's failure to perform its obligations under this Section 3.4. This indemnity is in addition to, and not in limitation of, Buyer's indemnification obligations under Section 9.3 of the Purchase Agreement."

Also, consider replacing "to the extent those obligations would have been Assumed Liabilities" with "to the extent those obligations constitute Assumed Liabilities under Section 2.3 of the Purchase Agreement" to eliminate the hypothetical framing.

## Issue 6 – Escrow Agreement § 2.4: Buyer Treated as Tax Owner of Escrow Funds

**Documents and Sections:** Escrow Agreement § 2.4; APA § 2.6(a).

**The Problem.** Section 2.4 provides that "Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds until they are distributed" and that all interest and earnings "shall be reported to Buyer on IRS Form 1099." The Escrow Amount ($1,840,000) is sourced from the Purchase Price that would otherwise have been paid to Marrowind. As drafted, Marrowind's money earns interest that is taxed to Buyer, and Marrowind receives no economic benefit from the time value of the escrowed funds during the 18-month hold period.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** On $1,840,000 invested in a Treasury money market fund (per Schedule B), at current rates of approximately 4–5%, the interest over 18 months would be roughly $138,000–$171,000. All of that income is reported to and taxed by Buyer. When the escrow is released to Marrowind at month 18, Marrowind receives only the principal (less any claims), not the earnings.

**Recommended Fix.** This is partly a **business decision** (see below), but if Marrowind wants to share in the earnings, propose:

> "All interest and other earnings on the Escrow Funds shall be allocated 50% to Seller and 50% to Buyer for tax purposes. The Escrow Agent shall issue separate IRS Form 1099-INT (or equivalent) to each Party for its share."

Alternatively, if Marrowind is willing to concede the interest to Buyer as the cost of having its indemnity security held in a neutral account, no change is needed—but the client should make that decision knowingly.

> **Business Decision Flag:** Confirm with Marrowind whether it wants to negotiate for a share of escrow interest or accept the current allocation. The dollar amount is modest (~$70K–$85K to Marrowind if split), so this may not be worth the negotiating capital.

## Issue 7 – APA § 10.12: Circular Order-of-Precedence Provision

**Documents and Sections:** APA § 10.12.

**The Problem.** Section 10.12 states:

> "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, **this Agreement controls** over the Ancillary Agreements, **except that each Ancillary Agreement controls as to its specific subject matter**."

The second clause effectively negates the first. If the Bill of Sale "controls as to its specific subject matter" (i.e., the conveyance of assets), then its broader definition of "Purchased Assets" (Issue 2) would control over the APA's narrower definition. The provision provides no objective standard for determining when an ancillary document is addressing its "specific subject matter" versus when the APA should control.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** In a dispute over whether the HOLLIS trademark or the Albany facility was conveyed, Buyer's counsel would argue the Bill of Sale controls (it is the conveyance document and thus addresses its "specific subject matter"). Marrowind's counsel would argue the APA controls (it is the master agreement and defines the scope of the transaction). The current language gives no clear answer.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend § 10.12 to:

> "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, **this Agreement shall control**, except that an Ancillary Agreement shall control solely to the extent it sets forth mechanics or procedures for carrying out a specific obligation of this Agreement (e.g., the mechanics of escrow disbursement under the Escrow Agreement or the specific services and fees under the Transition Services Agreement). In no event shall a definition in an Ancillary Agreement override a definition in this Agreement unless the Ancillary Agreement expressly states that it is modifying the definition."

This preserves the APA's primacy for substantive terms (including definitions) while giving ancillary documents appropriate authority over their operational mechanics.

## Issue 8 – Escrow Agreement § 3.1: Broad Pool of Potential Claimants Against the Escrow

**Documents and Sections:** Escrow Agreement § 3.1; APA § 9.2 (indemnitees).

**The Problem.** Escrow Agreement § 3.1 permits "Buyer, **on its own behalf or on behalf of any other Buyer indemnitee**," to deliver a Claim Notice. APA § 9.2 defines the Buyer indemnitees as "Buyer and its Affiliates, and their respective officers, directors, managers and employees." This means a current or former employee of a Tessaly Affiliate could, in theory, trigger a draw on the escrow for a personal injury claim or employment claim related to the Business, reducing the funds available to Marrowind at release.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** The $1,840,000 escrow is sized to the indemnity cap. If multiple claims are made by various indemnitees (not just Buyer itself), the escrow could be depleted by claims that Marrowind would not have anticipated. Marrowind's only protection is the 30-day objection window in § 3.2.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend Escrow Agreement § 3.1 to limit Claim Notices to claims "on behalf of Buyer or its Affiliates" and delete "their respective officers, directors, managers and employees" from the class that can trigger escrow draws. Alternatively, add:

> "A Claim Notice may be delivered only by or on behalf of Buyer or an Affiliate of Buyer. Claims by individual officers, directors, managers, or employees must be brought directly against Seller under Section 9.2 of the Purchase Agreement and shall not give rise to a draw on the Escrow Funds."

## Issue 9 – Escrow Agreement §§ 5.4–5.5: Marrowind Shares Escrow Agent Indemnity and Fees

**Documents and Sections:** Escrow Agreement § 5.4 (indemnity), § 5.5 (fees), Schedule C.

**The Problem.** Marrowind is jointly and severally liable with Buyer to indemnify the Escrow Agent (§ 5.4) and must pay one-half of all Escrow Agent fees and expenses (§ 5.5). Based on Schedule C, Marrowind's expected cost share over the 18-month term is approximately:

| Item | Total | Marrowind's 50% |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance fee | $2,500 | $1,250 |
| Annual administration (18 months) | $6,000 | $3,000 |
| Wire transfers (est. 2–3) | $50–$75 | $25–$38 |
| **Total** | **~$8,575** | **~$4,288** |

The indemnity in § 5.4 is more concerning than the fees. If the Escrow Agent suffers a loss due to, e.g., a wire fraud or a dispute over instructions, Marrowind is on the hook for half even if the loss was caused entirely by Buyer's instructions.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** The fee split is a modest cost (~$4,300) and is market-standard. The indemnity, however, creates a contingent liability that is not capped and is joint and several. The mitigating provision ("A Party whose conduct gave rise to the indemnified amount shall bear all of it") helps but requires Marrowind to prove it was not the cause.

**Recommended Fix.** The fee split is acceptable. For the indemnity, add a cap:

> "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Seller's aggregate liability under this Section 5.4 shall not exceed $50,000."

This is a **business decision** in the sense that the client should confirm whether the ~$4,300 fee share is acceptable (it likely is) and whether the indemnity cap is worth raising with the other side (it is a small ask and the Escrow Agent will likely accept it).

> **Business Decision Flag:** Confirm Marrowind is comfortable with ~$4,300 in escrow agent fees.

## Issue 10 – TSA Schedule A, Service 4: Marrowind Assumes Inventory and Supplier Risk for Procurement Support

**Documents and Sections:** TSA Schedule A, Service 4; TSA § 3.1 (Seller responsible for performance); APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities include pre-Closing employment liabilities).

**The Problem.** Service 4 requires Marrowind to continue "purchasing of linerboard, medium and starch under Seller's existing supplier arrangements" for four months post-closing, with "costs passed through at cost." Marrowind is the purchaser of record. This means:

- Marrowind takes title to and bears the risk of loss for raw materials purchased for Buyer's benefit.
- If a supplier disputes an order, delivers defective material, or files a claim, Marrowind is the counterparty.
- If Buyer fails to reimburse the pass-through costs, Marrowind has an unsecured receivable (subject to the TSA's 1% monthly late-payment interest under § 5.5, but no security).
- Marrowind's existing supplier agreements may have volume commitments, price-protection clauses, or non-assignment provisions that are triggered by purchasing for a third party's account.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** Corrugated packaging raw materials (linerboard, medium, starch) are significant-cost items. A four-month procurement run for a business generating $18.4M in asset value could represent several million dollars in inventory purchases. Marrowind is effectively acting as an unsecured financing vehicle for Buyer's working capital during the transition.

**Recommended Fix.** Add to TSA Schedule A, Service 4:

> "Buyer shall prepay all pass-through material costs at the time of order or shall provide Marrowind with an irrevocable letter of credit or cash collateral in an amount equal to 110% of the estimated monthly material costs. Title to all materials purchased under this Service shall pass to Buyer upon delivery to Buyer's facility, not upon purchase by Seller."

Also, Marrowind should confirm with its suppliers that the existing agreements permit purchasing for a third party's account (see Business Decision Flag below).

> **Business Decision Flag:** Marrowind should (a) confirm its supplier agreements permit this arrangement, and (b) decide whether to require prepayment or a letter of credit for pass-through costs. If the supplier relationships are a source of pricing advantage that Marrowind wants to preserve for its own post-closing operations, it should consider whether Service 4 is worth providing at all.

## Issue 11 – APA § 6.5: Non-Compete Creates Tension with Retained Albany Facility

**Documents and Sections:** APA § 6.5 (non-compete), § 2.2 (Excluded Assets include Albany facility).

**The Problem.** The non-compete prohibits Marrowind from "engag[ing] in the manufacture or sale of corrugated packaging products within the United States" for three years, "except through the ownership of the Excluded Assets **in a manner that does not compete with the Business**." The Excluded Assets include the Albany manufacturing facility—a facility that, by its nature, is designed to manufacture corrugated packaging products.

The exception is circular: Marrowind may use the Albany facility only in a way that "does not compete with the Business," but the Business *is* the manufacture and sale of corrugated packaging products. If Marrowind manufactures corrugated boxes at Albany, it is almost certainly competing with the Business (now owned by Buyer). The only permissible use of the facility might be as a warehouse, a facility for non-corrugated products, or a site for a completely different business.

**Why It Matters for Marrowind.** Marrowind is retaining a manufacturing facility that it may be unable to use for its original purpose. This affects the value of the Excluded Assets and Marrowind's post-closing business strategy. If Marrowind's plan was to continue operating Albany in a limited capacity (e.g., for a niche product line or for a specific customer), the non-compete may prohibit that.

**Recommended Fix (drafting).** If Marrowind intends to use the Albany facility for a specific, non-competing purpose, the exception should be made specific:

> "…except through the ownership and operation of the Albany manufacturing facility for [describe specific permitted use, e.g., 'the manufacture of non-corrugated packaging products' or 'the manufacture of corrugated packaging products for [specific customer] under a supply agreement dated [date]']."

> **Business Decision Flag (critical):** Marrowind must tell us what it plans to do with the Albany facility post-closing. The non-compete as drafted may prohibit any manufacturing use. This is not a drafting problem we can solve without knowing the client's business plan. If Marrowind wants to continue manufacturing corrugated products at Albany (even at a reduced scale or for a limited customer base), we need to negotiate a carve-out in § 6.5. If Marrowind plans to lease or sell the facility, the current language may be sufficient.

## Additional Observations (Lower Priority)

**A. Escrow Agreement § 3.2 – 30-Day Objection Window Is Short.** Marrowind has only 30 days from receipt of a Claim Notice to object. Given that claims may involve complex factual or legal analysis (e.g., whether a representation was "materially" inaccurate), 30 days may be insufficient. Consider negotiating 45 or 60 days.

**B. TSA § 7.3 – Vague Cross-Reference.** The indemnification provision incorporates "the procedures in Section 9.5 of the Purchase Agreement … with necessary changes." The phrase "with necessary changes" is undefined and could lead to disputes about which procedures apply. Consider either incorporating § 9.5 by reference without the qualifier or restating the key procedures (notice, defense, settlement consent) directly in the TSA.

**C. Bill of Sale § 2.3 – Irrevocable Power of Attorney.** The POA is broad (allows Buyer to "institute and prosecute any proceedings" in Marrowind's name) and irrevocable. This is market-standard for asset purchases, but Marrowind should be aware that Buyer can litigate in Marrowind's name post-closing without further consent. No change recommended, but flagging for client awareness.

**D. APA § 2.7 – Working Capital Adjustment.** The target is $2,150,000. If closing-date net working capital falls below this, Marrowind pays the shortfall. This is a potential post-closing cash outflow that could be material. Marrowind should model its expected closing-date working capital before the Closing Date to assess exposure.

> **Business Decision Flag:** Confirm with Marrowind's finance team what the expected closing-date net working capital is and whether there is a risk of a significant shortfall below $2,150,000.
