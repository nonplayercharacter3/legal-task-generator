## SUMMARY

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (including Disclosure Schedule), the Master Supply Agreement between Corbel Fluidics and Vantrell Polymer Corp., the Seller Indemnification Side Letter, and the Escrow Agreement. I have identified **thirteen issues** ranging from critical deal-threatening problems to moderate drafting concerns. ## ISSUE 1 — Supply Agreement Change-of-Control Consent Not Disclosed; Vantrell May Terminate Upon Closing

**Documents and Sections:**
- *corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md*, § 14.2 (Change in Ownership of Customer)
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 4.4 (No Conflicts; Consents) and Disclosure Schedule 4.4

**The Problem:**

Section 14.2 of the Supply Agreement provides:

> "Any change in the direct or indirect ownership of more than fifty percent (50%) of the outstanding voting stock of Customer, whether by sale or transfer of stock, merger, consolidation or otherwise, in a single transaction or a series of related transactions, shall be deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Customer and shall require the prior written consent of Supplier. If any such change occurs without the prior written consent of Supplier, Supplier may terminate this Agreement upon thirty (30) days' written notice to Customer."

The transaction is a 100% stock purchase of Corbel Fluidics. This is unambiguously a change in ownership of more than 50% of the outstanding voting stock. Vantrell's prior written consent is therefore required.

However, Disclosure Schedule 4.4 to the SPA states simply: **"None."** This means Seller has represented that no third-party consents are required in connection with the transaction. That representation is false as of the signing date.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- The Supply Agreement is the Company's **sole source** for all resin inputs (Supply Agreement § 2.2 requires Customer to purchase 100% of its requirements from Vantrell). If Vantrell terminates, the Company has no contractual supply of its key raw materials.
- Because Schedule 4.4 is empty, the closing condition in SPA § 7.2(c) (receipt of all consents listed on Schedule 4.4) is technically satisfied without any consent actually being obtained. The buyer could close and *then* discover that Vantrell has the right to terminate.
- The inaccuracy of the § 4.4 representation gives Buyer a termination right under SPA § 8.1(b), but more practically it means we are being asked to close a deal where the target's most critical commercial relationship can be terminated at the seller's supplier's sole discretion.
- Post-closing, a breach of the § 4.4 representation would be subject to the deductible and cap (it is not a Fundamental Representation), limiting recovery to $8.45M minus $633,750.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Before responding to Seller:** Confirm with the client whether they are aware of the change-of-control provision and what their commercial position is regarding Vantrell. The client needs to decide whether Vantrell consent is a condition to closing or whether they are willing to proceed with an indemnity.

2. **Require Seller to correct Schedule 4.4** to list the Vantrell consent and to deliver Vantrell's written consent as a closing deliverable. Add to SPA § 3.2 (Seller Closing Deliverables):

> "(h) the written consent of Vantrell Polymer Corp. to the transactions contemplated by this Agreement, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer, pursuant to Section 14.2 of the Master Supply Agreement between the Company and Vantrell Polymer Corp. dated December 15, 2021."

3. **Add Vantrell consent to the closing conditions** in SPA § 7.2(c) by amending Schedule 4.4.

4. **If Vantrell consent is not obtained before closing**, negotiate a specific indemnity from Seller for any losses arising from Vantrell's termination of the Supply Agreement, carved out from the deductible and cap, with survival of not less than the remaining term of the Supply Agreement (i.e., through December 31, 2026, or longer if a replacement supply agreement is negotiated).

**⚠️ CLIENT CALL NEEDED:** The client needs to understand that without Vantrell's consent, they are acquiring a company whose sole supply agreement can be terminated within 30 days of closing. This may be a deal-breaker or may require a price adjustment.

## ISSUE 2 — Supply Agreement Extension Option Has Already Lapsed; Agreement Expires December 31, 2026

**Documents and Sections:**
- *corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md*, § 3.1 (Initial Term) and § 3.2 (Extension Option)
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, dated September 21, 2026

**The Problem:**

The Initial Term of the Supply Agreement runs through **December 31, 2026** (§ 3.1). The Customer's one-time option to extend for 36 months must be exercised by written notice delivered **not less than 120 days before the end of the Initial Term** (§ 3.2).

120 days before December 31, 2026 is **September 2, 2026**. The SPA is dated **September 21, 2026**. The extension deadline passed 19 days before the SPA was signed.

The option is now irrevocably lost. Unless Vantrell agrees to a new arrangement, the Supply Agreement expires on December 31, 2026 — approximately two to three months after the expected Closing (which, under SPA § 3.1, would occur on the third Business Day after all conditions are satisfied, likely October or November 2026).

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- The Company is 100% dependent on Vantrell for its resin inputs (Supply Agreement § 2.2).
- Post-closing, the Company will have **no supply agreement** for its primary raw material.
- The Company also has a minimum annual volume commitment of 1,800,000 kg (Supply Agreement § 2.4) with an 8% shortfall penalty — but this obligation terminates with the agreement.
- Securing alternative supply or negotiating a new agreement with Vantrell on favorable terms will take time and may not be possible on the same economic terms (the current pricing includes a raw-material index adjustment formula and the extension pricing was fixed at a premium of roughly 5% over initial-term prices).
- This is not merely a legal issue — it is a fundamental business continuity issue that affects the value of the target.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Before responding to Seller:** Confirm with the client whether they have conducted any diligence on the Company's supply chain alternatives and whether the purchase price reflects the risk of losing the Vantrell relationship.

2. **Require as a condition to closing** that Seller negotiate and deliver either (a) a new supply agreement with Vantrell (or an amendment extending the current agreement) on terms no less favorable to the Company than the existing agreement, or (b) Vantrell's written consent to the transaction coupled with an agreement to extend or renew.

3. **If Seller cannot deliver a replacement supply agreement**, the client should consider whether to:
   - Reduce the purchase price to reflect the cost of re-sourcing;
   - Increase the escrow amount or add a specific indemnity for supply disruption;
   - Walk away.

4. **Add to the SPA's conduct-of-business covenant** (Article VI) an express obligation on Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to negotiate a supply agreement extension or replacement with Vantrell prior to Closing, with Buyer's approval over the terms.

**⚠️ CLIENT CALL NEEDED:** The client needs to understand that they are buying a company that will have no supply agreement for its primary raw material within months of closing, and that the contractual right to extend has already been lost. This materially affects valuation and risk.

## ISSUE 3 — Side Letter Narrows "Fundamental Representations," Stripping Capitalization and Taxes from Enhanced Protection

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 1.1 (definition of "Fundamental Representations")
- *seller_indemnity_side_letter.md*, § 2 (Fundamental Representations)
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 11.11 (Order of Precedence)

**The Problem:**

SPA § 1.1 defines "Fundamental Representations" as:

> "the representations and warranties set forth in Sections 4.1, 4.2, **4.3 (Capitalization)** and **4.12 (Taxes)** of this Agreement."

Side Letter § 2 provides:

> "For all purposes of the Purchase Agreement, including Sections 7.2(a), 9.1 and 9.5(b) thereof, 'Fundamental Representations' means **solely Sections 4.1 and 4.2** of the Purchase Agreement, and the definition of that term in Section 1.1 of the Purchase Agreement shall be read accordingly."

Under SPA § 11.11, the Side Letter has **first priority** in the order of precedence. The Side Letter therefore controls, and the effective definition of Fundamental Representations is only Organization/Good Standing (§ 4.1) and Authority/Enforceability (§ 4.2).

**Consequences of the Narrowing:**

| Protection | With 4.3 and 4.12 as Fundamental (SPA as drafted) | Without 4.3 and 4.12 (Side Letter controls) |
|---|---|---|
| **Survival** (§ 9.1) | 60 days after expiration of applicable statute of limitations (potentially 6+ years for tax) | 18 months |
| **Deductible** (§ 9.5(a)/(b)) | Not subject to $633,750 deductible | Subject to $633,750 deductible |
| **Cap** (§ 9.5(a)/(b)) | Not subject to 10% cap ($8.45M) | Subject to 10% cap ($8.45M) |
| **Bring-down standard** (§ 7.2(a)) | Must be true "in all respects" at Closing | Must be true "in all material respects" at Closing |

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- **Tax exposure:** The Company's tax representation (§ 4.12) covers all pre-closing tax periods. Tax audits can arise years after closing. With only 18-month survival and a cap of $8.45M, the buyer is exposed to unlimited tax liability (the indemnity in § 9.2(c) for pre-closing taxes is not subject to the cap per § 9.5(b)(iv) — *but only if we read "Section 9.2(c)" as referring to the tax indemnity in the SPA*). **However**, there is an ambiguity here: § 9.5(b) excludes "any claim under Section 9.2(c)" from the deductible and cap. If this exclusion survives the Side Letter's narrowing (which it should, since the Side Letter only redefines "Fundamental Representations" and does not amend § 9.5(b)), then tax claims under § 9.2(c) remain uncapped. But the *survival period* for the § 4.12 representation is still only 18 months under § 9.1, because it is no longer a Fundamental Representation. The § 9.2(c) tax indemnity is a separate indemnity obligation (not a representation), so it may survive independently — but this is uncertain and should be clarified.

- **Capitalization exposure:** If there are undisclosed options, warrants, or encumbrances on the Shares, the buyer's claim under § 4.3 would be subject to the deductible and cap and would expire 18 months post-closing.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Push to restore the SPA's original definition.** Delete Side Letter § 2 in its entirety, or amend it to read:

> "For all purposes of the Purchase Agreement, including Sections 7.2(a), 9.1 and 9.5(b) thereof, 'Fundamental Representations' means Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement."

2. **If Seller resists**, negotiate a compromise: at minimum, restore § 4.12 (Taxes) as a Fundamental Representation given the long-tail nature of tax exposure. As an alternative, add a specific tax indemnity with survival equal to the applicable statute of limitations plus 60 days, carved out from the cap and deductible.

3. **Clarify the interaction between § 9.2(c) (tax indemnity) and § 9.1 (survival).** Add language to the SPA making clear that the tax indemnity in § 9.2(c) survives until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations for the relevant tax period, regardless of the survival period for representations and warranties.

**⚠️ CLIENT CALL NEEDED:** The client should understand that, as currently drafted, their protection against pre-closing tax liabilities and capitalization defects is significantly weaker than the SPA on its face would suggest. The Side Letter (which the client may not have flagged as significant) materially changes the risk allocation.

## ISSUE 4 — Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction for Indemnification Claims

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, §§ 11.8 (Governing Law) and 11.9 (Jurisdiction) — New York
- *seller_indemnity_side_letter.md*, §§ 6.1 (Governing Law) and 6.2 (Jurisdiction) — Delaware
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 11.11 (Order of Precedence)

**The Problem:**

The SPA provides that New York law governs and New York County courts have exclusive jurisdiction (§§ 11.8–11.9). The Side Letter provides that **Delaware law** governs and the **Delaware Court of Chancery** has exclusive jurisdiction for "all claims, disputes and causes of action arising out of or relating to indemnification under the Purchase Agreement or this Letter Agreement" (§ 6.1–6.2).

Because the Side Letter has first priority (§ 11.11), indemnification claims are governed by Delaware law and must be brought in Delaware, while all other claims (e.g., specific performance, pre-closing disputes, the purchase price adjustment) are governed by New York law and must be brought in New York.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- **Litigation complexity:** A single post-closing dispute may implicate both indemnification and non-indemnification theories. For example, if Seller breaches a covenant and the buyer seeks both indemnification damages and specific performance, the claims may need to be brought in different forums.
- **Substantive differences:** Delaware and New York differ on issues relevant to indemnification, including the interpretation of materiality qualifiers, the business judgment rule's application to post-closing disputes, and the availability of certain equitable remedies.
- **Cost and delay:** Maintaining parallel proceedings in two jurisdictions increases cost and creates the risk of inconsistent outcomes.
- **The Chancery Court** is a court of limited jurisdiction (primarily equity and internal corporate matters). It is unclear whether all indemnification claims (particularly those sounding in contract) fall within its subject matter jurisdiction. The Side Letter addresses this with a fallback to the Delaware Superior Court or federal court (§ 6.2), but this adds uncertainty.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Unify the governing law and jurisdiction** across all Transaction Documents. Propend for New York law and New York County courts throughout, deleting Side Letter §§ 6.1–6.2 and replacing with a reference to SPA §§ 11.8–11.9.

2. **If Seller insists on Delaware** (perhaps because Tarrant Peak GP LLC is a Delaware entity), at minimum add a provision permitting the buyer to elect to bring all related claims (indemnification and non-indemnification) in a single forum to avoid split proceedings.

## ISSUE 5 — Escrow Amount Equals the Cap; Maximum Practical Recovery Is Limited

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 2.5 (Escrow Amount = $8,450,000)
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 9.5(a) (Cap = 10% of Purchase Price = $8,450,000)
- *indemnity_escrow_agreement.md*, § 4.5 (Insufficient Funds)

**The Problem:**

The Escrow Amount ($8,450,000) is exactly equal to the indemnification Cap (10% of the $84,500,000 Purchase Price = $8,450,000). This means:

- The maximum amount the buyer can recover under the indemnification (for non-Fundamental, non-Fraud, non-covenant, non-tax claims) is exactly what is in escrow.
- If the escrow is partially depleted by earlier claims, the remaining cap is correspondingly reduced.
- Escrow Agreement § 4.5 confirms that if the escrow is insufficient, the Escrow Agent has "no further obligation with respect to the shortfall" — the buyer must pursue Seller directly under SPA § 9.6.
- Seller is a limited partnership (Tarrant Peak Partners LP). Post-closing, if the partnership distributes its assets to its partners, collecting a direct judgment could be difficult.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

The buyer's practical recovery for representation breaches (other than Fundamental Reps, Fraud, covenant breaches, and taxes) is effectively capped at the escrow amount. If Seller's partnership is wound down post-closing, the buyer may be unable to collect beyond the escrow even if a judgment is entered.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Increase the Escrow Amount** to exceed the Cap (e.g., 12–15% of the Purchase Price) to provide a buffer for the deductible and to ensure the full cap is available.

2. **Alternatively or additionally**, require Seller to provide a parent guarantee or letter of credit for the amount by which the Cap exceeds the Escrow Amount.

3. **Add a covenant** requiring Seller (or its general partner) to maintain sufficient assets or net worth to satisfy its indemnification obligations through the expiration of the survival period.

## ISSUE 6 — True Deductible (Not a Tipping Basket)

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 9.5(a)

**The Problem:**

Section 9.5(a) provides:

> "Seller shall not be liable to the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(a) until the aggregate amount of all Losses in respect thereof exceeds $633,750 (the 'Deductible'), **in which event Seller shall be liable only for Losses in excess of the Deductible**."

This is a **true deductible** (also called a "franchise"): the buyer recovers only the amount *above* $633,750. It is not a **tipping basket** (or "threshold"), under which once aggregate losses exceed the threshold, the buyer recovers from dollar one.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- If the buyer's aggregate losses from representation breaches are $700,000, the buyer recovers only $66,250 under a true deductible, but $700,000 under a tipping basket.
- The deductible amount ($633,750 = 0.75% of the Purchase Price) is within the market range, but the true-deductible structure is less favorable to the buyer than a tipping basket.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Negotiate a tipping basket.** Amend § 9.5(a) to read:

> "Seller shall not be liable to the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(a) until the aggregate amount of all Losses in respect thereof exceeds $633,750 (the 'Threshold'), **in which event Seller shall be liable for all such Losses from the first dollar**."

2. **If Seller resists**, accept the true deductible but reduce the amount (e.g., to 0.5% of the Purchase Price = $422,500).

## ISSUE 7 — No Extended Survival for Environmental or Employee Matters

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 4.15 (Environmental), § 4.13 (Employee Benefits), § 9.1 (Survival)

**The Problem:**

Environmental and employee benefit representations are non-Fundamental Representations. They survive only 18 months post-closing (§ 9.1) and are subject to the deductible and cap (§ 9.5(a)).

- Environmental liabilities (contamination, regulatory claims) often surface years after acquisition, particularly for manufacturing companies.
- Employee benefit plan issues (ERISA claims, underfunding, benefit disputes) can also have long tails.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

Corbel Fluidics is a manufacturing company with a leased facility (Schedule 4.8). If environmental contamination is discovered after 18 months, the buyer has no indemnification recourse (absent Fraud). Similarly, if a 401(k) plan deficiency or health plan claim emerges after 18 months, the buyer bears the loss.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Add a specific environmental indemnity** in the SPA with survival of not less than six (6) years (or the applicable statute of limitations), carved out from the deductible and cap. Proposed language:

> "Section 9.9. Environmental Indemnity. From and after the Closing, Seller shall indemnify and hold harmless the Buyer Indemnitees from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to (a) any violation of any environmental Law by the Company or any of its properties prior to the Closing Date, or (b) any contamination, release, or threat of release of Hazardous Substances at or from any property owned, leased, or operated by the Company prior to the Closing Date. This Section 9.9 shall survive the Closing until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (including any extensions) and shall not be subject to the limitations in Section 9.5."

2. **Add a specific employee benefit indemnity** with survival of not less than six (6) years, similarly carved out from the deductible and cap.

## ISSUE 8 — Supply Agreement Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for Supply Failure

**Documents and Sections:**
- *corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md*, § 12.2 (Cap)

**The Problem:**

Section 12.2 caps each party's aggregate liability at "the total amounts paid and payable by Customer to Supplier under this Agreement during the twelve (12) months preceding the event giving rise to the claim."

Given the 100% requirements obligation and the minimum annual volume of 1,800,000 kg, annual payments could be substantial (at a blended average price of roughly $60–$100/kg depending on the product mix, annual spend could range from approximately $108M to $216M). However, the cap is measured by *actual* payments in the preceding 12 months, which may be lower if volumes were below the minimum.

More critically, if Vantrell fails to supply (e.g., due to a capacity issue or force majeure), the Company's lost profits from inability to manufacture and sell its products are excluded by § 12.1 (consequential damages exclusion). The Company's recovery would be limited to direct damages (e.g., cost of cover under § 7.3) subject to the 12-month cap.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

The Company's entire business depends on a continuous supply of resin. A supply disruption could halt production entirely. The combination of the consequential damages exclusion and the 12-month cap may leave the Company significantly under-compensated for a prolonged supply failure.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Negotiate an amendment to the Supply Agreement** (as part of the consent/extension process under Issue 1) that:
   - Increases the cap to at least 24 months of payments;
   - Carves out lost profits from the consequential damages exclusion for supply failures exceeding 30 consecutive days;
   - Adds a specific liquidated damages provision for supply failure.

2. **If the Supply Agreement cannot be amended**, ensure the SPA includes a specific indemnity from Seller for any supply disruption caused by the change of control or the expiration of the Supply Agreement.

## ISSUE 9 — Escrow Release Mechanics Create Ambiguity

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 2.5 (Escrow release "shall be released to Seller on the date that is eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date")
- *seller_indemnity_side_letter.md*, § 4.2 (Joint instructions required within 5 Business Days after the 18-month date)
- *indemnity_escrow_agreement.md*, § 4.4 (Release "upon receipt of a Joint Written Instruction")

**The Problem:**

SPA § 2.5 uses mandatory language ("shall be released"), suggesting the escrow releases automatically at 18 months. However, the Escrow Agreement § 4.4 requires a Joint Written Instruction to effect the release, and Side Letter § 4.2 requires both parties to execute such instructions within 5 Business Days after the 18-month date.

Under the order of precedence (§ 11.11), the Side Letter controls. So the release is **not** automatic — it requires joint action.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

- This is actually **protective of the buyer**: Seller cannot unilaterally trigger the release. If the buyer has a pending claim, it can withhold its signature on the joint instruction (the Pending Claim Amount is retained under Escrow Agreement § 4.4).
- However, the ambiguity could create a dispute if Seller argues the SPA mandates automatic release. The buyer should ensure the documents are harmonized to avoid any argument.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Amend SPA § 2.5** to clarify that the release is subject to the Escrow Agreement and Side Letter:

> "Unless earlier disbursed, the remaining balance of the Escrow Amount, less the aggregate amount of any then-pending claims, shall be released to Seller on the date that is eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date, **in each case in accordance with the Escrow Agreement and the Side Letter**."

## ISSUE 10 — No Representation Specifically Addressing Change-of-Control Triggers in Material Contracts

**Documents and Sections:**
- *stock_purchase_agreement.md*, § 4.10 (Material Contracts), § 4.4 (No Conflicts; Consents)

**The Problem:**

Section 4.10(b) represents that each Material Contract is valid, binding, and not in breach. Section 4.4 represents that the transaction does not conflict with or require consent under any Material Contract. However, there is no representation that specifically addresses whether the transaction triggers any change-of-control, anti-assignment, or consent provisions in the Material Contracts.

The failure to disclose the Vantrell change-of-control provision (Issue 1) is an example of the gap. There may be similar provisions in the other Material Contracts (the Ostrander Distribution Agreement, the First Meridian Revolving Credit Agreement, or the Parkway lease) that have not been identified.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

The buyer is relying on the § 4.4 representation and Schedule 4.4 to identify all required consents. If other Material Contracts contain similar change-of-control or consent provisions that were not disclosed, the buyer could face the same termination risk as with Vantrell.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **Request that Seller confirm in writing** that it has reviewed all Material Contracts for change-of-control, anti-assignment, and consent provisions, and that Schedule 4.4 is complete and accurate.

2. **Add a specific representation** to the SPA:

> "Section 4.4A. Change of Control. Seller has identified on Schedule 4.4 all Material Contracts that contain any provision requiring the consent of a third party to, or providing a right of termination or modification in connection with, a change of control of the Company or an assignment of the contract. Except as set forth on Schedule 4.4, no such provision exists in any Material Contract."

3. **Review the other Material Contracts** (Ostrander Distribution Agreement, First Meridian Revolving Credit Agreement, Parkway lease) for similar provisions. Note: the First Meridian facility is to be repaid at closing, so its change-of-control provision (if any) is less critical, but the payoff should be confirmed.

## ISSUE 13 — Supply Agreement Section 16.1 Restricts Future Restructuring

**Documents and Sections:**
- *corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md*, § 16.1 (Assignment)

**The Problem:**

Section 16.1 prohibits Customer from assigning the Supply Agreement without Supplier's consent. While a stock purchase does not technically constitute an "assignment" (the contracting entity remains the same), any future restructuring by the buyer — such as merging Corbel into another Halvorsen entity, transferring the business to a subsidiary, or selling the business — would require Vantrell's consent.

Notably, the provision is asymmetric: Supplier may assign freely to any person, including in connection with a sale of its resin business (§ 16.1, second sentence). Customer has no such flexibility.

**Why It Matters to Halvorsen:**

If Halvorsen's acquisition strategy involves integrating Corbel into its existing operating structure (e.g., merging it with another portfolio company), the Supply Agreement would need to be assigned, requiring Vantrell's consent. This could complicate or delay post-closing integration.

**Recommended Fix:**

1. **In negotiating the Vantrell consent** (under Issue 1), request that the consent also include a pre-approved assignment right to Halvorsen and its Affiliates.

2. **Alternatively**, negotiate an amendment to § 16.1 permitting Customer to assign to its Affiliates without consent (mirroring the right Supplier already has).

The tax indemnity is a separate obligation from the representations, but it is not clear whether it has its own survival period or is subsumed by the 18-month survival for non-Fundamental Representations. This should be clarified (see Issue 3, Recommended Fix 3).
