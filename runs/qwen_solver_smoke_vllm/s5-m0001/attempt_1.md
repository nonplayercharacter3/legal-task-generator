## ISSUES REQUIRING A CALL WITH THE CLIENT BEFORE WE RESPOND

### 1. Supply Agreement Change-of-Control Consent Not Disclosed; Schedule 4.4 Is Incorrect

**Documents:** corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md § 14.2; stock_purchase_agreement.md § 4.4 and Disclosure Schedule 4.4

**Issue.** Section 14.2 of the Master Supply Agreement provides that any change in direct or indirect ownership of more than 50% of Corbel's voting stock "shall be deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Customer and shall require the prior written consent of Supplier." If the change occurs without consent, Vantrell may terminate on 30 days' notice. This transaction is a 100% stock purchase and will unambiguously trigger Section 14.2.

Yet Disclosure Schedule 4.4 to the SPA states "None" in response to the consent requirement in Section 4.4. This is flatly incorrect. The SPA's Section 4.4 representation that no consent is required (except as set forth on Schedule 4.4) is therefore false as of the signing date.

**Why it matters.** The Supply Agreement is Corbel's sole-source supply contract for four resin grades that are inputs to its core products (valve bodies, manifolds, pump housings). It is a 100% requirements contract (Supply Agreement § 2.2) with a minimum annual volume of 1,800,000 kg (§ 2.4). Losing this agreement would be existentially threatening to the business we are acquiring for $84.5 million. If we close without Vantrell's consent, Vantrell holds a termination right exercisable on 30 days' notice, giving it enormous leverage over us post-closing.

Additionally, because Schedule 4.4 lists no consents, the closing condition in SPA § 7.2(c) (all consents on Schedule 4.4 received) is technically satisfied without Vantrell ever being asked. We could close cleanly under the SPA and then face termination.

**Recommended fix.**

- Before we respond, confirm with the client whether they are aware of this provision and whether any informal discussions with Vantrell have occurred.
- We should demand that Seller (a) correct Schedule 4.4 to list the Vantrell consent, (b) use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain Vantrell's written consent prior to Closing, and (c) add the receipt of that consent as an express closing condition in Section 7.2.
- Proposed language for a new Section 7.2(g):

> "(g) Seller shall have obtained the prior written consent of Vantrell Polymer Corp. to the transactions contemplated by this Agreement in accordance with Section 14.2 of the Supply Agreement, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer, and a duly executed copy thereof shall have been delivered to Buyer."

- If Vantrell refuses consent or is unresponsive, we need to assess whether the deal is viable. The client should understand that without this consent, we are acquiring a company whose primary supply relationship can be terminated at will by the supplier within 30 days of Closing.

### 2. Supply Agreement Extension Option Deadline Has Likely Already Passed

**Documents:** corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md §§ 3.1, 3.2

**Issue.** The Initial Term of the Supply Agreement ends December 31, 2026 (§ 3.1). Customer may extend for 36 months by written notice delivered "not less than one hundred twenty (120) days before the end of the Initial Term" (§ 3.2). One hundred twenty days before December 31, 2026 is approximately September 3, 2026. The SPA is dated September 21, 2026. The deadline has therefore already passed.

**Why it matters.** If the extension was not exercised by approximately September 3, 2026, the Supply Agreement expires on December 31, 2026—only 16 days after the SPA's drop-dead date of December 15, 2026 (SPA § 8.1(d)). Even if we close on the last possible day, Corbel would be operating without its sole-source supply agreement within three weeks. If Closing is delayed even slightly past December 31, the Company has no supply contract at all.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Call the client immediately** to confirm whether the extension notice was delivered to Vantrell before September 3, 2026, and if so, to obtain a copy.
- If it was not exercised, this must be raised with Seller as a material issue. Seller's representation in SPA § 4.10(b) that each Material Contract is "in full force and effect" may be technically true (an unexercised option is not a breach), but the imminent expiration is a material fact that should have been disclosed.
- If the extension was not exercised, we should require Seller to negotiate a new supply agreement or an extension with Vantrell as a condition to Closing, or alternatively seek a specific indemnity for the loss of the supply relationship.

### 3. Side Letter Narrows "Fundamental Representations," Significantly Reducing Buyer's Protection

**Documents:** seller_indemnity_side_letter.md § 2; stock_purchase_agreement.md § 1.1, § 7.2(a), § 9.1, § 9.5(b)

**Issue.** The SPA's Section 1.1 defines "Fundamental Representations" as Sections 4.1 (Organization), 4.2 (Authority), 4.3 (Capitalization), and 4.12 (Taxes). Side Letter Section 2 overrides this and provides that "Fundamental Representations" means "solely Sections 4.1 and 4.2." Because SPA § 11.11 gives the Side Letter first priority in the order of precedence, the Side Letter controls.

**Why it matters.** The consequences of removing Capitalization and Taxes from the Fundamental Reps are threefold:

| Protection | With 4.3 and 4.12 as Fundamental | Without (current Side Letter) |
|---|---|---|
| Bring-down standard at Closing (§ 7.2(a)) | True and correct in all respects | True and correct in all material respects |
| Survival period (§ 9.1) | 60 days after expiration of applicable statute of limitations (potentially 6+ years for tax) | 18 months |
| Deductible and Cap (§ 9.5(b)) | Carved out—no deductible, no cap | Subject to $633,750 deductible and 10% cap ($8.45M) |

A tax indemnity that expires in 18 months is inadequate. Tax audits can be opened years after the relevant period, and the SPA's own Section 9.2(c) provides a separate tax indemnity, but that indemnity is subject to the same 18-month survival under Section 9.1 (it is a covenant, not a representation, but the survival provision in 9.1 covers "representations and warranties"; covenants survive "until fully performed" per the last sentence of 9.1—this creates ambiguity about whether the tax indemnity in 9.2(c) survives beyond 18 months).

Similarly, a capitalization representation that is subject to a materiality qualifier at Closing and to the deductible and cap post-Closing provides weaker protection against, e.g., undisclosed options or encumbrances on the Shares.

**Recommended fix.**

- Push to restore the original SPA definition. Proposed language: delete Side Letter § 2 in its entirety, or replace with:

> "For all purposes of the Purchase Agreement, 'Fundamental Representations' means the representations and warranties set forth in Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement."

- If Seller resists, as a fallback, restore at minimum Section 4.12 (Taxes) to the Fundamental Reps and negotiate a separate survival period for the tax indemnity in Section 9.2(c) of at least six years (to cover the federal statute of limitations plus a buffer).

## OTHER SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 4. Conflicting Governing Law and Jurisdiction for Indemnification Claims

**Documents:** seller_indemnity_side_letter.md § 6.1–6.2; stock_purchase_agreement.md § 11.8–11.9; indemnity_escrow_agreement.md § 8.1–8.2

**Issue.** The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The Escrow Agreement is likewise governed by New York law with New York County jurisdiction. But the Side Letter— which by the order of precedence (SPA § 11.11) controls over the SPA—selects Delaware law and exclusive jurisdiction in the Court of Chancery of Delaware for all indemnification disputes.

**Why it matters.** Indemnification is the primary post-Closing remedy. Having the substantive contract governed by New York law but the indemnification claims governed by Delaware law and litigated in a different forum creates:

- Uncertainty about which state's law governs interpretation of the SPA's indemnification provisions (the Side Letter says Delaware law governs "all claims, disputes and causes of action arising out of or relating to indemnification under the Purchase Agreement," which arguably encompasses the SPA's Article IX).
- Practical complexity: a single dispute could implicate both the Escrow Agreement (New York) and the indemnification claim (Delaware), potentially requiring parallel proceedings.
- The Court of Chancery has limited subject-matter jurisdiction (it does not hear all contract disputes); the Side Letter's fallback to the Superior Court or federal court in Delaware adds another layer.

**Recommended fix.**

- Seek to conform the Side Letter's governing law and jurisdiction to New York, consistent with the SPA and Escrow Agreement. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps for familiarity with its own counsel), at minimum clarify that the SPA's general provisions (definitions, interpretation, the MAE definition, etc.) remain governed by New York law, and only the procedural aspects of indemnification enforcement are in Delaware.
- Alternatively, accept the split but add a provision that the parties shall litigate all related claims (including escrow disputes) in a single forum to the extent permitted.

### 5. Escrow Release Date Coincides Exactly with General Rep Survival Period—No Buffer

**Documents:** stock_purchase_agreement.md §§ 2.5, 9.1; indemnity_escrow_agreement.md § 4.4; seller_indemnity_side_letter.md § 4.2

**Issue.** General representations survive 18 months post-Closing (SPA § 9.1). The escrow is released 18 months post-Closing (SPA § 2.5). The Escrow Agreement (§ 4.4) provides that the Escrow Agent holds only the "Pending Claim Amount" after the Release Date. The Side Letter (§ 4.2) requires joint written instructions for release within 5 Business Days after the 18-month mark.

**Why it matters.** If a problem is discovered at month 17, Buyer has approximately one month to (a) investigate, (b) quantify losses, (c) deliver a claim notice with reasonable specificity (SPA § 9.1 last sentence; Side Letter § 3.1), and (d) notify the Escrow Agent (Escrow Agreement § 4.1). Any delay means the escrow is released and Buyer must pursue Seller directly—a limited partnership whose assets may be limited post-distribution.

**Recommended fix.**

- Extend the escrow release to 21 or 24 months post-Closing, or extend the general rep survival to 24 months with the escrow released at 18 months (giving a 6-month tail during which claims can be filed against a still-secured obligation).
- Alternatively, add a provision that the Escrow Agent shall hold the full Escrow Amount for an additional 90 days after the Release Date if Buyer delivers a written statement that it is investigating a potential claim, absent a Final Order directing release.

### 6. Deductible Is a True Deductible, Not a Tipping Basket

**Documents:** stock_purchase_agreement.md § 9.5(a)

**Issue.** Section 9.5(a) provides that Seller is "liable only for Losses in excess of the Deductible." This is a true deductible (also called a "deductible" or "excess loss" threshold). Once aggregate losses exceed $633,750, Seller pays only the amount above that threshold.

**Why it matters.** If, for example, aggregate non-fundamental rep losses total $900,000, Buyer recovers only $266,250. Under a tipping basket (or "threshold"), Buyer would recover the full $900,000 once the threshold is crossed. The true deductible structure systematically reduces Buyer's recovery for moderate-claim scenarios.

**Recommended fix.**

- Negotiate to convert to a tipping basket. Proposed language:

> "Seller shall not be liable to the Buyer Indemnitees under Section 9.2(a) until the aggregate amount of all Losses in respect thereof exceeds $633,750 (the 'Threshold'), in which event Seller shall be liable for all such Losses from the first dollar."

- If Seller resists, the current structure is acceptable given the relatively low threshold (0.75% of Purchase Price), but the client should understand the practical effect.

### 7. Asymmetric Assignment Rights in Supply Agreement

**Documents:** corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md § 16.1

**Issue.** Customer (Corbel) may not assign the Supply Agreement without Supplier's prior written consent. Supplier (Vantrell) may assign to "any Person without the consent of Customer, including in connection with a merger, consolidation, or sale of all or any part of Supplier's resin business."

**Why it matters.** Post-Closing, Vantrell can sell its business (or the specific product lines) to a competitor, a less reliable supplier, or a strategic acquirer with no obligation to obtain Corbel's consent. The new supplier would step into the agreement with the same pricing and terms, but Corbel would have no say in who its sole-source supplier is. Combined with the 100% requirements obligation and the lack of a termination-for-convenience right, this is a significant operational risk.

**Recommended fix.**

- This is a pre-existing contract issue, not something the SPA can fix directly. However, we should:
  - Raise it in the Vantrell consent negotiation (see Issue 1) and seek to modify Section 16.1 to require Customer's consent for Supplier assignments, or at minimum a right for Customer to terminate without penalty if Supplier assigns to a direct competitor.
  - Ensure the SPA's indemnification covers any loss arising from a Vantrell assignment that adversely affects the Company (this would fall under the general rep in SPA § 4.10(b) that Material Contracts are in full force and effect, though the practical remedy is limited).
  - Flag to the client as a post-Closing integration risk.

### 8. No Termination for Convenience; 100% Requirements Obligation; Minimum Volume

**Documents:** corbel_vantrell_supply_agreement.md §§ 2.2, 2.4, 14

**Issue.** Corbel is locked into Vantrell: it must purchase 100% of its requirements (§ 2.2), must buy at least 1,800,000 kg per year or pay an 8% shortfall fee (§ 2.4), and has no right to terminate for convenience (only for cause under § 14.1).

**Why it matters.** If post-Closing Halvorsen's strategic plans change (e.g., vertical integration, product line changes, or a decision to source from an alternative supplier), Corbel cannot exit the Supply Agreement without breaching. The minimum volume commitment creates a fixed cost floor.

**Recommended fix.**

- This is a business/commercial issue for the client. Flag it for the client call.
- If the Vantrell consent negotiation (Issue 1) is successful, use that as leverage to negotiate a termination-for-convenience right (perhaps with a declining notice period and fee) or a reduction in the minimum volume commitment.

### 9. Ambiguity in Survival of Tax Indemnity (Section 9.2(c))

**Documents:** stock_purchase_agreement.md §§ 9.1, 9.2(c), 9.5(b)

**Issue.** Section 9.1 provides that "representations and warranties" survive 18 months (or longer for Fundamental Reps) and that "covenants that by their terms contemplate performance after the Closing shall survive until fully performed." Section 9.2(c) is an indemnification obligation for pre-Closing Taxes. It is structured as part of the indemnification article, not as a covenant. It is unclear whether it survives 18 months (as a representation-based claim) or indefinitely (as a post-Closing performance obligation).

Section 9.5(b) carves out "any claim under Section 9.2(c)" from the deductible and cap, suggesting the parties intended it to have a different (more favorable) treatment. But the survival period is not explicitly addressed.

**Why it matters.** If the tax indemnity is read as surviving only 18 months, Buyer is exposed to pre-Closing tax liabilities (e.g., unfiled returns, underpayments, employment tax issues) that surface after 18 months with no recourse. Tax audits routinely open 2–4 years after the relevant period.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add express language to Section 9.1:

> "The indemnification obligations of Seller under Section 9.2(c) shall survive until the later of (a) the date that is six (6) years after the Closing Date, or (b) sixty (60) days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (including any extensions) for the taxable period to which the relevant Taxes relate."

- Alternatively, restore Section 4.12 to the Fundamental Reps (see Issue 3), which would give the tax representation survival until 60 days after the statute of limitations expires.

### 11. Collection Risk Against Seller Post-Escrow Release

**Documents:** stock_purchase_agreement.md §§ 2.5, 9.5(a), 9.6

**Issue.** The indemnification cap is 10% of the Purchase Price ($8,450,000), which equals the Escrow Amount. Once the escrow is released (18 months), any further recovery depends on Seller's ability to pay. Seller is Tarrant Peak Partners LP, a Delaware limited partnership. There is no personal guarantee, no parent company, and no representation regarding Seller's net worth or financial condition.

**Why it matters.** For Fundamental Reps (currently only Organization and Authority under the Side Letter), there is no cap, so in theory Buyer could recover more than the escrow. But collecting from an LP that has distributed its proceeds may be difficult.

**Recommended fix.**

- Consider requesting a representation from Seller as to its net worth or financial condition sufficient to satisfy uncapped indemnification obligations.
- Alternatively, negotiate a longer escrow tail or a parent/sponsor guarantee for uncapped claims.
- At minimum, flag to the client that the practical value of uncapped indemnification (for Fraud and the two remaining Fundamental Reps) depends on Seller's post-Closing solvency.

### 12. Materiality Qualifier on Ordinary Course Liabilities (Section 4.6)

**Documents:** stock_purchase_agreement.md § 4.6

**Issue.** Section 4.6 represents that the Company has no liabilities except those on the balance sheet and those "incurred in the ordinary course of business since June 30, 2026, none of which is material in amount." The materiality qualifier means that individually immaterial ordinary course liabilities are disclosed by the qualifier and do not constitute a breach.

**Why it matters.** If the Company has, say, 50 individual liabilities of $50,000 each (totaling $2.5 million) incurred in the ordinary course since June 30, 2026, none is individually "material" and the representation is not breached. Yet the aggregate is significant.

**Recommended fix.**

- Add an aggregate materiality qualifier: "none of which, individually or in the aggregate, is material in amount."
- Alternatively, require Seller to deliver a schedule of all ordinary course liabilities exceeding a de minimis threshold (e.g., $25,000) since June 30, 2026.
