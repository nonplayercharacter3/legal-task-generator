Before we respond to the other side, I recommend a call with the client on the following three points. Each one goes to whether the deal as papered is acceptable or whether we need to restructure conditions or walk.

### A. The Supply Agreement Extension Option Deadline Has Already Passed

**Documents:** Supply Agreement §3.1, §3.2; SPA §1 (dated September 21, 2026)

The Initial Term of the Master Supply Agreement expires on **December 31, 2026** (Supply Agreement §3.1). The Customer's extension option requires written notice delivered **not less than 120 days before the end of the Initial Term** (Supply Agreement §3.2). Counting back 120 days from December 31, 2026, the deadline was **September 2, 2026**. The SPA is dated **September 21, 2026**—nineteen days after the deadline passed.

Unless the extension was already exercised before September 2 (which the Disclosure Schedule does not indicate and which we have no evidence of), the Supply Agreement **will expire on December 31, 2026**, approximately three months after the expected Closing. The Company would then have no contractual supply source for its sole-source resin inputs (see Supply Agreement §2.2, 100% requirements buy; SPA §4.10(a)(ii), sole-source supplier).

**Why this matters to Halvorsen:** We would be acquiring a company that, by operation of the existing contract, loses its critical supply agreement within 90 days of Closing. The business model appears to depend on this supply. This is not a negotiable indemnification issue—it is a fundamental deal-structure problem.

**Recommended action:**
- Confirm immediately with the client whether the extension notice was delivered before September 2, 2026. If it was, request a copy.
- If it was not delivered, we need to (i) make Closing conditional on Vantrell's written agreement to extend (or enter a new supply agreement) on terms no less favorable than the Extension Pricing in Schedule B, Part 2, and (ii) add a specific condition to Section 7.2 of the SPA to that effect.
- Proposed new condition (add as Section 7.2(g)):

> "(g) Seller shall have delivered to Buyer a written agreement between the Company and Vantrell Polymer Corp., in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer, extending the term of the Supply Agreement for a period of not less than thirty-six (36) months at prices no greater than the Extension Pricing set forth in Part 2 of Schedule B thereto, or a replacement supply agreement providing for the supply of equivalent products on terms no less favorable to the Company."

- If Vantrell will not agree to extend, the client needs to understand that the asset is materially impaired and the Purchase Price should be adjusted accordingly, or the deal should not proceed.

### B. Change-of-Control Consent Required by Vantrell but Not Disclosed

**Documents:** Supply Agreement §14.2; SPA §4.4 and Schedule 4.4

Supply Agreement §14.2 provides:

> "Any change in the direct or indirect ownership of more than fifty percent (50%) of the outstanding voting stock of Customer, whether by sale or transfer of stock, merger, consolidation or otherwise, in a single transaction or a series of related transactions, shall be deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Customer and shall require the prior written consent of Supplier. If any such change occurs without the prior written consent of Supplier, Supplier may terminate this Agreement upon thirty (30) days' written notice to Customer."

This transaction is a 100% change of ownership of the Company. It squarely triggers §14.2. Vantrell's prior written consent is required.

Yet **Schedule 4.4 of the Disclosure Schedule states "None"**—meaning Seller represents that no third-party consents are required (SPA §4.4). This is either:
- A **misrepresentation** by Seller (the representation in §4.4 is false), or
- A **failure to disclose** a material consent requirement.

**Why this matters to Halvorsen:**
1. If we close without Vantrell's consent, Vantrell has the right to terminate the Supply Agreement on 30 days' notice. Combined with the expiration issue above, this gives Vantrell enormous leverage.
2. The false Schedule 4.4 entry means Seller's representation in §4.4 is breached at signing. This is a claim under SPA §9.2(a), but it is subject to the $633,750 deductible (SPA §9.5(a)) and, more importantly, the practical damage (loss of supply) far exceeds any indemnification recovery.
3. The bring-down condition in SPA §7.2(a) requires Seller's reps to be true at Closing. If Schedule 4.4 remains "None" at Closing, the condition is not satisfied and we have the right to walk. But we should not rely on this as our primary protection—we should fix the deal.

**Recommended action:**
- Make Closing conditional on delivery of Vantrell's written consent to the change of control (this should be added to Schedule 4.4 and to the conditions in Section 7.2(c)).
- Proposed amendment to Schedule 4.4:

> "1. Consent of Vantrell Polymer Corp. to the change in ownership of the Company pursuant to Section 14.2 of the Master Supply Agreement dated December 15, 2021."

- Proposed amendment to Section 7.2(c) (which already requires consents on Schedule 4.4 to be received): no textual change needed if Schedule 4.4 is corrected, but confirm the condition is tracked.
- If Vantrell withholds consent or conditions it on terms unacceptable to the client, this is a deal-killer unless the Purchase Price is adjusted or a replacement supply source is identified.

**Client call point:** The client needs to understand that the two issues above (expiration + consent) are interconnected. Vantrell has both the right to require consent and the benefit of the agreement expiring soon. This gives Vantrell significant negotiating leverage that the current documents do not address.

### C. Side Letter Silently Narrows "Fundamental Representations"

**Documents:** Side Letter §2; SPA §1.1; SPA §11.11

The SPA defines "Fundamental Representations" as **Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 (Capitalization) and 4.12 (Taxes)** (SPA §1.1).

The Side Letter provides (Side Letter §2):

> "For all purposes of the Purchase Agreement, including Sections 7.2(a), 9.1 and 9.5(b) thereof, 'Fundamental Representations' means **solely Sections 4.1 and 4.2** of the Purchase Agreement, and the definition of that term in Section 1.1 of the Purchase Agreement shall be read accordingly."

Because the SPA's order-of-precedence clause gives the Side Letter **first priority** (SPA §11.11), the Side Letter's narrower definition controls. The practical consequences are:

| Protection | Under SPA alone (4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.12) | Under Side Letter (4.1, 4.2 only) |
|---|---|---|
| Survival period | 60 days after applicable statute of limitations (SPA §9.1) | **18 months** (SPA §9.1) |
| Deductible ($633,750) | Does not apply (SPA §9.5(b)(ii)) | **Applies** |
| Cap ($8,450,000) | Does not apply (SPA §9.5(b)(ii)) | **Applies** |
| Bring-down at Closing | Must be true "in all respects" (SPA §7.2(a)) | Must be true only "in all material respects" (SPA §7.2(a)) |

**Why this matters to Halvorsen:**
- **Capitalization (4.3):** If there is an undisclosed encumbrance on the Shares, an outstanding option, or a subsidiary that was not disclosed, the claim is now subject to the deductible and cap, and expires in 18 months. For an $84.5M transaction, a capitalization defect could be catastrophic, but the buyer's recovery would be capped at $8.45M and only after the first $633,750 of loss.
- **Taxes (4.12):** If the Company has unpaid pre-Closing taxes, undisclosed tax liabilities, or unfiled returns, the claim is subject to the same limitations. Tax statutes of limitations can run 3–6 years or longer. An 18-month survival period means we lose the right to indemnification for tax problems discovered after 18 months, even though the underlying tax liability may not surface for years.
- The net effect is that the two categories of reps most likely to produce significant post-Closing losses (hidden tax liabilities and capitalization defects) have been downgraded from "fundamental" (uncapped, long survival) to ordinary reps (capped, short survival).

**Recommended action:**
- Push to restore the SPA's original definition of Fundamental Representations (4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.12) in the Side Letter.
- If Seller resists, propose a compromise: retain 4.3 (Capitalization) as a Fundamental Representation (this is standard in most transactions) and, at minimum, extend the survival period for 4.12 (Taxes) to the applicable statute of limitations plus 60 days, even if the cap applies.
- Proposed revised Side Letter §2:

> "For all purposes of the Purchase Agreement, including Sections 7.2(a), 9.1 and 9.5(b) thereof, 'Fundamental Representations' means Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3 of the Purchase Agreement. Notwithstanding the foregoing, the representations and warranties in Section 4.12 (Taxes) shall survive the Closing until the date that is sixty (60) days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (giving effect to any waiver, mitigation or extension thereof), and the limitations in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement shall not apply to Losses based upon any inaccuracy in or breach of Section 4.12."

**Client call point:** The client should understand that this was likely a negotiated concession that may not have been flagged internally. We need to confirm whether the client's deal team was aware of this change and whether it was a deliberate trade-off for something else. If it was not intentional, we should push back firmly.

## ADDITIONAL ISSUES (IN ORDER OF SIGNIFICANCE)

### D. Governing Law and Jurisdiction Conflicts Among Transaction Documents

**Documents:** SPA §11.8 (New York law), §11.9 (New York courts); Side Letter §6.1 (Delaware law), §6.2 (Delaware Chancery Court); Escrow Agreement §8.1 (New York law), §8.2 (New York courts)

The Side Letter governs indemnification claims under Delaware law and in the Delaware Court of Chancery, while the SPA and Escrow Agreement are governed by New York law and subject to New York jurisdiction. Because the Side Letter has first priority (SPA §11.11), indemnification disputes will be litigated in Delaware under Delaware law, while other disputes (e.g., purchase price adjustment under §2.4, specific performance of closing conditions) will be in New York under New York law.

**Why this matters:** This split creates (i) potential for inconsistent interpretations of overlapping provisions (e.g., the definition of "Losses," the scope of "Fraud," the operation of the deductible), (ii) increased litigation cost and complexity, and (iii) the risk that a threshold issue (such as whether a claim falls within the Side Letter's scope or the SPA's scope) becomes itself a contested jurisdictional question.

**Recommended action:** Conform the Side Letter to New York law and New York jurisdiction, consistent with the SPA and Escrow Agreement. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps because it is a Delaware LP), at minimum add a provision clarifying that the SPA's definitions and the Escrow Agreement's mechanics are incorporated by reference and shall be interpreted consistently.

Proposed Side Letter §6.1:

> "This Letter Agreement, and all claims, disputes and causes of action arising out of or relating to indemnification under the Purchase Agreement or this Letter Agreement, shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the internal laws of the State of New York."

### E. 18-Month Survival Period Is Short for a Transaction of This Size

**Documents:** SPA §9.1; Side Letter §2

For an $84.5M acquisition, 18 months is at the short end of market for non-fundamental representations. Combined with the Side Letter's narrowing of Fundamental Reps (Issue C above), the vast majority of Seller's reps—including Financial Statements (4.5), Undisclosed Liabilities (4.6), Material Contracts (4.10), Litigation (4.11), Employee Benefits (4.13), Compliance (4.14), and Environmental (4.15)—expire in 18 months.

Many of these issues (e.g., a latent environmental condition, an undisclosed employee benefit liability, a contract dispute that has not yet been asserted) may not manifest within 18 months.

**Recommended action:** Seek to extend the general survival period to **24 months**. If Seller resists, 21 months is a reasonable fallback. At minimum, the survival period for the Financial Statements rep (§4.5) should be tied to the outside date for filing the Company's first post-Closing annual report (i.e., at least 18 months after Closing plus the filing deadline).

### F. Deductible and Cap Mechanics

**Documents:** SPA §9.5(a)

- **Deductible:** $633,750 (0.75% of Purchase Price). This is a true deductible (not a tipping basket)—Seller is liable only for Losses *in excess of* the deductible, not for the full amount once the threshold is crossed.
- **Cap:** $8,450,000 (10% of Purchase Price), which equals the Escrow Amount.

The deductible is at the higher end of market (typically 0.5%–1%). More concerning is the interaction with the Escrow: because the Cap equals the Escrow Amount, if the full Escrow is consumed by indemnification claims, Seller has no further secured obligation, and any excess (for Fundamental Reps or Fraud, which are uncapped) would be an unsecured claim against a Delaware LP whose assets post-Closing may be limited.

**Recommended action:**
- Negotiate the deductible down to $422,500 (0.5% of Purchase Price) or convert it to a tipping basket (once the threshold is crossed, Seller is liable for the full amount from dollar one).
- Confirm that Seller (or its general partner, Tarrant Peak GP LLC) has sufficient net worth to satisfy uncapped claims (Fraud, Fundamental Reps) beyond the Escrow. Consider requiring a representation of net worth or a personal guarantee from the GP.

### G. Supply Agreement: Asymmetric Assignment Rights

**Documents:** Supply Agreement §16.1

Customer (the Company) may not assign without Supplier's consent. Supplier may assign **freely**, including in connection with a sale of its resin business, without Customer's consent.

**Why this matters:** Post-Closing, Halvorsen owns the Company. If Vantrell sells its business to a competitor of the Company or to a less reliable supplier, the Company is stuck with the new owner as its sole-source supplier with no right to object. This is a significant operational risk.

**Recommended action:** Seek an amendment to §16.1 requiring Supplier to obtain Customer's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) before assigning to a direct competitor of Customer or to any entity that does not maintain ISO 9001:2015 certification. If this is not achievable pre-Closing, add a post-Closing covenant in the SPA requiring Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to negotiate such an amendment within 90 days after Closing, with a specific indemnity for losses if the amendment is not obtained.

### H. Supply Agreement: 100% Requirements Buy with No Alternative Source

**Documents:** Supply Agreement §2.2

The Company is contractually obligated to purchase 100% of its U.S. requirements for the specified resin grades from Vantrell. There is no right to source from an alternative supplier except in the limited circumstances of Supplier's failure to supply or Force Majeure (and even then, only for the affected quantities under §15.2).

**Why this matters:** This creates a single-point-of-failure in the Company's supply chain. Any disruption at Vantrell (financial distress, quality failure, capacity constraint, or the termination right in §14.2) directly impacts the Company's ability to operate.

**Recommended action:** This is a business issue for the client rather than a legal drafting issue, but we should flag it clearly. If the client's business plan for the Company includes diversifying supply, that plan is contractually constrained for the duration of the Supply Agreement (and any extension). The client should factor this into its integration planning and valuation.

### I. Supply Agreement: Minimum Volume Commitment

**Documents:** Supply Agreement §2.4

The Company must purchase at least 1,800,000 kg of Products per calendar year. Shortfalls (other than due to Supplier failure or Force Majeure) trigger a penalty of 8% of the then-applicable average price per kilogram multiplied by the shortfall.

At the Initial Term prices (Schedule B, Part 1), the weighted average price depends on the product mix, but using a rough blend, the minimum commitment represents approximately $25–35M in annual purchases. The shortfall penalty, while modest at 8%, is a fixed cost if the Company's volumes decline post-acquisition.

**Why this matters:** If Halvorsen's post-Closing business plan involves rationalizing product lines or reducing volumes, the minimum commitment becomes a cost drag. The client should confirm that its operating plan supports at least 1.8M kg of annual purchases.

**Recommended action:** Confirm with the client's operating team that the minimum volume is achievable under their business plan. If not, negotiate a reduction in the minimum or a lower shortfall penalty as a condition to Closing (tied to the Vantrell consent we are already seeking).

### K. Escrow Release Mechanics and Pending Claims

**Documents:** SPA §2.5; Escrow Agreement §4.4; Side Letter §4.2

The Escrow is released 18 months after Closing (the "Release Date"), less the "Pending Claim Amount." A "Pending Claim Amount" is defined as the aggregate amount of Losses claimed in Claim Notices delivered to the Escrow Agent that have not been "resolved and paid or released" (Escrow Agreement §1.1).

**Potential gap:** If a claim is asserted at month 17 (within the survival period), the Escrow Agent holds back the Pending Claim Amount. But if the claim is complex and requires litigation that extends beyond the Release Date, the Escrow Agent continues to hold the funds (Escrow Agreement §4.4, second sentence). However, the Side Letter's joint-instruction requirement (Side Letter §4.1) means that releasing the held-back funds requires **both** parties to sign. If Seller refuses to sign (e.g., it disputes the claim), Buyer cannot unilaterally access the held-back Escrow funds without a Final Order.

**Why this matters:** In a contested claim, Buyer may be forced to litigate to obtain a Final Order before accessing the Escrow, even though the funds are already segregated and held for the purpose of paying that claim. This delays recovery and increases cost.

**Recommended action:** Add a provision to the Escrow Agreement (or Side Letter) stating that if the parties cannot agree on a Joint Written Instruction within 30 days after a claim is finally resolved in the parties' favor (by agreement or by a non-final order that is not subject to appeal within the survival period), the prevailing party may direct the Escrow Agent to disburse unilaterally. Alternatively, provide that the Escrow Agent may disburse upon delivery of a certified copy of any order or judgment (whether or not final) that awards the claim, subject to Seller's right to post a bond to restrain disbursement.

### L. Section 4.6 (Undisclosed Liabilities) Materiality Qualifier

**Documents:** SPA §4.6

The representation states that the Company has no liabilities except those on the balance sheet and those "incurred in the ordinary course of business since June 30, 2026, **none of which is material in amount**."

The materiality qualifier means that individually immaterial undisclosed liabilities (e.g., a $50,000 regulatory fine, a $30,000 contract dispute) do not constitute a breach. Combined with the $633,750 deductible, there is a significant range of undisclosed liabilities (up to $633,750 in the aggregate) for which Buyer has no indemnification recovery.

**Recommended action:** Seek to delete "none of which is material in amount" or replace it with "none of which exceeds $100,000 in the aggregate." If Seller resists, at minimum confirm that the deductible is reduced (see Issue F) to narrow the gap.

### M. Order of Precedence Creates Interpretive Risk

**Documents:** SPA §11.11

The SPA provides: "first, the Side Letter; second, this Agreement; and third, the Escrow Agreement."

This means that in the event of any conflict, the Side Letter controls over the SPA, and the SPA controls over the Escrow Agreement. We have already identified the Fundamental Representations redefinition as a critical example (Issue C). There is also the governing law conflict (Issue D). The broad "any conflict or inconsistency" language could be invoked by Seller to argue that other Side Letter provisions (e.g., the notice requirements in Side Letter §3, which are more detailed than SPA §9.4) override corresponding SPA provisions, potentially in ways that are unfavorable to Buyer.

**Recommended action:** Add a "no implied conflict" provision to the Side Letter:

> "Except as expressly set forth in this Letter Agreement, the Purchase Agreement shall remain in full force and effect, and no provision of this Letter Agreement shall be construed to modify, amend or supplement any provision of the Purchase Agreement unless this Letter Agreement expressly states that it does so."

(Side Letter §7.1 contains a similar "Effect" provision, but it is weaker because it does not expressly state that the Side Letter does not modify the SPA except as expressly provided.)

### N. Tax Treatment of Indemnification Payments

**Documents:** SPA §9.7; Escrow Agreement §5.1

SPA §9.7 provides that indemnification payments are treated as **purchase price adjustments** for tax purposes. Escrow Agreement §5.1 provides that **Buyer** is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds (and reports interest income) until disbursement.

**Why this matters:** If indemnification payments are purchase price adjustments, they reduce Buyer's tax basis in the Shares. This is generally the intended treatment, but the client's tax advisors should confirm that this is consistent with their position and that the Escrow interest income (reported to Buyer under §5.1) does not create an inconsistent characterization.

**Recommended action:** Flag to the client's tax team for confirmation. No drafting change recommended unless tax advises otherwise.
