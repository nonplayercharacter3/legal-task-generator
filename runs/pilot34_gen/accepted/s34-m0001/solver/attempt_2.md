## Summary

The licensing package (Agreement, Order Form No. 1, and the proposed Side Letter) contains several material deviations from the commercial deal captured in the Term Sheet, along with a number of one-sided provisions that expose Steireis to significant operational and financial risk. The proposed Side Letter compounds the problem by asking Steireis to concede a late non-renewal notice and accept a weakened data-security liability provision in exchange for a modest 8% fee discount. The single most urgent issue is the exclusivity clause in Order Form No. 1, Section 5, which is far broader than what the Term Sheet contemplated.

The Ardmoor Linen Services Agreement (March 1, 2024) is an unrelated vendor contract for linen and laundry services. It has no bearing on the software licensing and I have not analyzed it further.

## Issues in Order of Priority

### 1. Exclusivity: Order Form §5 Dramatically Expands Beyond the Term Sheet

**Documents:** Order Form No. 1, §5.1–5.4; Term Sheet, §4.1–4.2; Agreement, §1.3 (definition of "Covered Properties")

**What the Term Sheet agreed:**
- Exclusivity limited to the **property management module only** (Term Sheet §4.1).
- Limited to the **seven Hudson Valley properties listed** in the Term Sheet (Term Sheet §4.1).
- Steireis expressly "remains free to license, use and evaluate third-party software for guest messaging, point-of-sale, revenue management and any other function" (Term Sheet §4.2).
- Steireis is "free to use any property management system at any property not listed in Section 2.2, including properties it acquires, opens or brings under management after the date of the Definitive Agreements" (Term Sheet §4.2).

**What the Order Form actually provides:**
- Exclusivity covers **all four software categories**: property management, point-of-sale (food, beverage, spa, retail), revenue management/rate optimization, and guest messaging/communications (Order Form §5.2).
- Exclusivity applies to **all Covered Properties**, which the Agreement defines as "any hotel, inn, resort or other lodging property owned, managed or franchised by Licensee or any of its Affiliates, at any location, including any property acquired, opened or brought under management or franchise after the Effective Date" (Agreement §1.3).
- Any newly acquired property using competing software must be **transitioned to Nesesk within 180 days** (Order Form §5.3).
- Breach is a **material breach** entitling Nesesk to **injunctive relief** (Order Form §5.4).

**Why it matters:**
This transforms a limited PMS exclusivity at seven properties into a blanket prohibition on using any competing hospitality software—across four functional categories—at every property Steireis owns, manages, or franchises anywhere in the world, including properties it has not yet acquired. For a growing boutique hotel group, this eliminates the ability to pilot or deploy superior products in any category (e.g., a better revenue management engine or a different POS for a new spa concept) and imposes a forced-migration obligation on every future acquisition. The 180-day transition window in §5.3 could create operational disruption at newly acquired properties. The injunctive relief provision in §5.4 means Nesesk could enjoin Steireis from using a competing product even before a damages claim is resolved.

**Recommended fix:**
Strike Order Form §5 in its entirety and replace with language tracking the Term Sheet:

> "During the Subscription Term, Licensee shall use Nesesk PMS as the sole property management system at the seven Initial Deployment Properties listed in Section 1.2. Licensee remains free to license, use, evaluate, or implement any third-party software for point-of-sale, revenue management, guest messaging, or any other function at any Covered Property. Licensee is free to use any property management system at any property not listed in Section 1.2, including properties acquired, opened, or brought under management after the date of this Order Form."

If Nesesk insists on some exclusivity consideration, the maximum concession should be PMS-only exclusivity at the seven listed properties for the Initial Term only (not Renewal Terms).

### 2. Side Letter: Concession of Late Non-Renewal Notice for Inadequate Consideration

**Documents:** Side Letter, Recitals B–C and §1.1; Agreement §3.2; Order Form §2.1

**The problem:**
The Initial Term ends November 30, 2026 (36 months from the December 1, 2023 Subscription Start Date). Agreement §3.2 requires non-renewal notice "at least ninety (90) days before the end of the then-current term." Ninety days before November 30, 2026 is **September 1, 2026**. Steireis's non-renewal letter is dated **September 18, 2026**—seventeen days after the deadline.

The Side Letter asks Steireis to "acknowledge and agree" that the Agreement has renewed for a 36-month First Renewal Term (Side Letter §1.1). In exchange, Steireis receives an 8% discount on Subscription Fees (Side Letter §1.2), saving approximately **$13,158 per year** or **~$39,473 over the 36-month Renewal Term**.

**Why it matters:**
The 8% discount is modest relative to what Steireis is giving up:
- A 36-month lock-in (through November 30, 2029) during which the broad exclusivity in Order Form §5 continues to apply.
- The right to argue that the notice was timely (there is a colorable argument that "gives notice" in §3.2 means the notice must be *sent* rather than *received* 90 days prior, and that a 17-day delay in a commercial relationship of this kind should be excused under equitable principles or the doctrine of substantial performance—though this is a fact-intensive argument).
- Leverage to negotiate other terms (see Issue 3 below and the exclusivity fix in Issue 1).

Additionally, the Side Letter is silent on the exclusivity problem. By acknowledging the renewal, Steireis would be bound by Order Form §5 for another 36 months unless it separately negotiates a fix.

**Recommended fix / negotiating position:**
- Do **not** sign the Side Letter as drafted.
- If Steireis is willing to accept the renewal (which may be the pragmatic choice given the 17-day delay), the Side Letter must be expanded to include:
  - A reduction or elimination of the Order Form §5 exclusivity (per Issue 1 above);
  - Rejection of the proposed amendment to §12.3(a) (per Issue 3 below);
  - A larger fee concession (the 8% discount should be the floor, not the ceiling, given the other concessions Steireis is making);
  - A mutual release of any claims related to the timing of the non-renewal notice, so that the issue is fully closed.
- If Steireis wishes to preserve the argument that the notice was timely, it should **not** sign §1.1 of the Side Letter and should instead send a reservation-of-rights letter acknowledging receipt of the Side Letter but disputing the renewal. This preserves the argument for potential litigation or further negotiation, but carries the risk that a court finds the 17-day delay dispositive. **Flag to client:** this is a business judgment call that depends on how much Steireis values the ability to walk away versus the cost of litigating or negotiating from a position of weakness.

### 3. Side Letter: Proposed Amendment to Data Security Liability Cap (§12.3(a))

**Documents:** Side Letter §2.1; Agreement §12.3(a); Agreement §9 (Data Security)

**Current provision:**
Agreement §12.3(a) provides that the liability cap in §12.2 (12 months of Fees, ~$164,472) does **not** apply to "Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security)." This means any breach of data security obligations—whether caused by ordinary negligence, gross negligence, or willful misconduct—is **uncapped**.

**Proposed amendment:**
The Side Letter would replace §12.3(a) with: "Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security), **to the extent resulting from Licensor's gross negligence or willful misconduct**."

**Why it matters:**
The amendment would subject ordinary-negligence data security breaches to the ~$164,472 cap. Given that the Licensed Software processes Guest Data including payment card data, identification documents, and reservation histories (Agreement §1.6), and that Steireis is subject to the NY SHIELD Act (referenced in Agreement §9.4), a data breach caused by even ordinary negligence on Nesesk's part could generate:
- Regulatory fines and penalties (SHIELD Act, PCI DSS);
- Class-action exposure from guests;
- Credit monitoring costs for affected individuals;
- Reputational damage.

A cap of ~$164,472 is grossly inadequate for these exposures. The current uncapped provision for *any* data security breach is the appropriate allocation of risk: Nesesk controls the security architecture, and Steireis cannot mitigate Nesesk's security failures.

**Recommended fix:**
**Reject Side Letter §2.1 in its entirety.** Maintain the current §12.3(a) (uncapped for any breach of Section 9). If Nesesk insists on some limitation, the maximum concession should be a "super-cap" of 3x annual Fees (approximately $493,416) for data security breaches resulting from ordinary negligence, with gross negligence and willful misconduct remaining uncapped. Drafting:

> "(a) Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security); provided that, to the extent such breach results from Licensor's ordinary negligence (and not gross negligence or willful misconduct), Licensor's aggregate liability under this Section 9 shall not exceed three (3) times the annual Subscription Fees in effect for the then-current contract year."

### 4. Guest Data Indemnity: Licensee Indemnifies Licensor for Claims "Arising Out of or Relating to" Guest Data

**Documents:** Agreement §11.2; Agreement §12.3(b) (excludes this indemnity from the cap)

**The provision:**
Licensee shall defend, indemnify and hold harmless Licensor from "any and all third-party claims, actions, regulatory investigations, losses, damages, fines, penalties, costs and expenses (including reasonable attorneys' fees) **arising out of or relating to Guest Data processed through the Licensed Software**."

**Why it matters:**
This indemnity is not limited to claims caused by Licensee's acts or omissions. The phrase "arising out of or relating to Guest Data processed through the Licensed Software" is broad enough to encompass claims resulting from **Licensor's own data security breach**. For example, if Nesesk suffers a breach due to a misconfigured firewall, and guests sue Steireis (as the hotel operator) for the exposure, Steireis would be required to indemnize Nesesk against any claim Nesesk faces in connection with that same Guest Data. This inverts the expected allocation of risk.

The indemnity is also **uncapped** by virtue of §12.3(b), which excludes "Licensee's obligations under Section 11.2" from the liability cap.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend §11.2 to limit the Guest Data indemnity to claims **to the extent arising from Licensee's acts, omissions, or breach of this Agreement**. Add a carve-out making clear that Licensee has no obligation to indemnify Licensor for claims resulting from Licensor's breach of Section 9 (Data Security) or Section 10 (Warranties). Suggested drafting:

> "Licensee shall defend, indemnify and hold harmless Licensor... from and against any and all third-party claims... arising out of or relating to Guest Data processed through the Licensed Software, **to the extent such claims result from Licensee's acts, omissions, or breach of this Agreement; provided that Licensee shall have no obligation under this Section 11.2 to the extent any such claim results from Licensor's breach of Section 9 or Section 10.**"

### 5. Go-Live Risk: Fees Accrue Regardless of Implementation Completion

**Documents:** Order Form §2.3; Order Form §3.3; Agreement §4.1

**The provision:**
"Subscription Fees accrue from the Subscription Start Date **whatever the go-live date of any property**" (Order Form §2.3). The targeted go-live was February 29, 2024, but the obligation is only "commercially reasonable efforts."

**Why it matters:**
Steireis was paying $164,472/year (plus $45,500 in implementation fees) from December 1, 2023, even if properties were not live. There is no fee abatement, service credit, or termination right if go-live is materially delayed. "Commercially reasonable efforts" is a low standard that is difficult to enforce. For a hotel group, running on a legacy PMS while paying for a new one that isn't operational represents a direct financial loss with no contractual recourse.

**Recommended fix (if this is still relevant given the 2026 timeline):**
This issue is likely moot at this point (go-live was targeted for February 2024 and the Initial Term is ending in November 2026). However, if the Renewal Term includes any new properties or modules, ensure that fees for those additions do not accrue until go-live. For the existing properties, no action needed unless go-live was in fact delayed and Steireis has not yet raised the issue.

**Flag to client:** Confirm whether all seven properties were actually live by February 29, 2024, and whether any implementation issues were resolved. If go-live was materially delayed, there may be a claim (albeit a weak one given the "commercially reasonable efforts" standard) that should be assessed.

### 6. Service Credits: Modest Remedy and Short Claim Window

**Documents:** Order Form §6.1–6.3; Agreement §4.3

**The provisions:**
- Maximum credit is 20% of the monthly Nesesk PMS fee (~$1,578/month) (Order Form §6.1).
- Credits must be claimed within **30 days** after the end of the affected month (Order Form §6.2).
- Chronic failure (below 98% in 3 of 6 rolling months) is the only service-level trigger for a material breach/termination right (Order Form §6.3).

**Why it matters:**
For a property management system that handles reservations, front desk operations, folios, and night audit across seven properties, a 20% credit on the PMS fee is a token remedy. The 30-day claim window is administratively tight—Steireis would need to track monthly availability and file claims promptly. The chronic failure threshold (98%) is quite low; Nesesk could have 98.1% availability every month and never trigger a termination right.

**Recommended fix:**
- Increase the maximum service credit to 50% of the monthly PMS fee for availability below 98%.
- Extend the claim window to 60 days.
- Lower the chronic failure threshold to 99.0% (i.e., below 99.0% in 3 of 6 months triggers a material breach).
- Add a provision that if availability falls below 95% in any single month, Licensee may terminate the affected Order Form with a pro-rata refund.

### 7. 36-Month Renewal Terms: Excessive Lock-In

**Documents:** Agreement §3.2; Term Sheet §3.3

**The provision:**
Automatic renewal for 36-month terms unless 90-day non-renewal notice is given.

**Why it matters:**
While the Term Sheet contemplated 36-month renewals, the combination of (a) 36-month renewal periods, (b) the broad exclusivity in Order Form §5, and (c) the 90-day notice window creates a significant trap. Steireis must remember to give notice 90 days before the end of each 36-month term, or it is locked in for another three years with no ability to use competing software. The fact that Steireis already missed the deadline by 17 days for the first renewal underscores the operational risk.

**Recommended fix:**
- Reduce renewal terms to 12 months.
- If Nesesk insists on 36-month renewals, extend the non-renewal notice period to 180 days (giving Steireis more time to make the decision and send notice) and add a requirement that Nesesk send a written reminder of the upcoming renewal and the non-renewal deadline at least 120 days before the end of the term.
- In any event, **calendar the next non-renewal deadline immediately** (if the First Renewal Term begins December 1, 2026 and runs 36 months, it ends November 30, 2029; the 90-day non-renewal deadline would be **September 1, 2029**).

### 8. Liability Cap: 12 Months of Fees May Be Inadequate

**Documents:** Agreement §12.2; Term Sheet §7.1

**The provision:**
Each Party's aggregate liability is capped at Fees paid or payable in the 12 months preceding the claim (~$164,472).

**Why it matters:**
The Term Sheet contemplated this cap (Term Sheet §7.1), so it is not a deviation. However, for a hotel group with seven properties and 232 rooms, a cap of ~$164K is low. If Nesesk's software failure causes a multi-property operational disruption (e.g., loss of reservation data, inability to process check-ins), Steireis's actual losses (lost revenue, guest rebooking costs, reputational harm) could far exceed the cap. The consequential damages waiver in §12.1 compounds this by excluding lost profits and revenue.

**Recommended fix:**
- Increase the cap to 2x annual Fees (~$329,000) or, better, 12 months of Fees plus a fixed amount (e.g., $500,000).
- Carve out from the consequential damages waiver any losses resulting from Licensor's breach of Section 4 (Implementation and Support) or Section 9 (Data Security), to the extent such losses are direct and foreseeable.

### 9. Licensee Responsibility for Authorized User Acts

**Documents:** Agreement §5.1

**The provision:**
"Licensee is responsible for the acts and omissions of its Authorized Users in using the Licensed Software."

**Why it matters:**
"Authorized Users" includes "employees, contractors and agents of Licensee or its Affiliates" (Agreement §1.2). This is a broad group. If a contractor at one of Steireis's properties uses the software in a way that causes a data breach or violates the license restrictions, Steireis is on the hook. There is no corresponding obligation on Nesesk to provide adequate training, access controls, or user activity monitoring to prevent such events.

**Recommended fix:**
Add: "provided that Licensee shall not be responsible for acts or omissions of its Authorized Users to the extent resulting from a failure of the Licensed Software's access controls, authentication mechanisms, or security features as required under Section 9."

### 10. Order of Precedence: Term Sheet Is Not in the Chain

**Documents:** Agreement §15.4

**The provision:**
Order of precedence: (a) side letter; (b) Order Form; (c) Agreement. The Term Sheet is not included.

**Why it matters:**
The Term Sheet is expressly non-binding (Term Sheet §8.1), so its exclusion from the precedence order is technically correct. However, this means that if there is a conflict between the Term Sheet's commercial deal (e.g., the limited exclusivity in Term Sheet §4) and the Order Form (e.g., the broad exclusivity in Order Form §5), the Order Form controls. The Term Sheet cannot be used as a interpretive aid to resolve ambiguities in the Order Form because it is not part of the agreement.

**Recommended fix:**
This is not fixable by amendment (the Term Sheet was never intended to be binding). The practical fix is to ensure the Order Form is corrected to match the Term Sheet's commercial terms (see Issue 1). In any negotiation with Nesesk, the Term Sheet should be referenced as evidence of the parties' original commercial understanding, even if it is not contractually binding.
