## Issues in Priority Order

### 1. Exclusivity Scope Is Dramatically Broader Than the Term Sheet

**Where:** Order Form No. 1, §§ 5.1–5.4; Agreement, § 1.3 (definition of "Covered Properties"). Compare Term Sheet, § 4.

**The problem:** The Term Sheet (which both parties acknowledged on October 20, 2023) limited exclusivity to the PMS module only, at the seven listed Hudson Valley properties, and expressly preserved Steireis's freedom to use third-party software for guest messaging, point-of-sale, and revenue management, as well as to use any PMS at properties not on the list (including future acquisitions). Term Sheet § 4.1–4.2.

The executed Order Form strips all of that away:

- **§ 5.1** prohibits Steireis from licensing, using, implementing, *piloting or evaluating* any "competing hospitality software" at **all Covered Properties** during the Subscription Term **including all Renewal Terms**.
- **§ 5.2** defines "competing hospitality software" to sweep in four categories: (a) property management, (b) point-of-sale, (c) revenue management and rate optimization, and (d) guest messaging and communications.
- **§ 5.3** requires that if a newly acquired property is running competing software, Steireis must transition it to Nesesk within 180 days and may not renew the existing contract.
- **§ 5.4** declares a breach material, acknowledges irreparable harm, and entitles Nesesk to injunctive relief.

Critically, the definition of **"Covered Properties"** in Agreement § 1.3 is not limited to the seven properties in the Order Form. It captures "any hotel, inn, resort or other lodging property owned, managed or franchised by Licensee or any of its Affiliates, **at any location**, including any property acquired, opened or brought under management or franchise after the Effective Date." Read together, §§ 1.3 and 5.1 mean Steireis cannot use or even *evaluate* competing PMS, POS, revenue management, or guest-messaging software at any property it currently owns or will ever acquire, manage, or franchise, worldwide, for the entire 36-month Initial Term plus every 36-month Renewal Term.

**Why it matters:** Steireis is a growing boutique hotel group. The exclusivity as drafted effectively makes Nesesk the sole technology platform for the entire enterprise in perpetuity (as long as auto-renewals continue). It prevents Steireis from piloting a better revenue management tool, adopting a modern POS for a new property's restaurant, or even evaluating a competitor's guest-messaging product. The 180-day forced-transition requirement in § 5.3 adds operational risk on every acquisition. The injunctive-relief acknowledgment in § 5.4 means a single misstep could enjoin Steireis from using alternative software across its portfolio.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a rewrite of Order Form § 5 to conform to the Term Sheet: exclusivity limited to the PMS module, limited to the seven properties listed in Order Form § 1.2, and expressly excluding POS, revenue management, and guest-messaging categories.
- If Nesesk insists on broader exclusivity as the price of the renewal discount in the Side Letter, cap the scope at the properties listed in the Order Form (not "Covered Properties" as defined in § 1.3) and exclude the "pilot or evaluate" language—Steireis should be free to evaluate alternatives so long as it does not deploy them.
- Delete or narrow § 5.3: if a newly acquired property is on a different PMS, Steireis should have the option to either (a) transition within a reasonable period (e.g., 12 months, not 180 days) or (b) pay a per-room fee for that property without being forced to migrate.
- Delete the irreparable-harm acknowledgment in § 5.4 or limit it to actual deployment of a competing PMS at a listed property.

**Flag for client:** Confirm whether Steireis has any near-term acquisition or management-contract pipeline. If so, the § 5.3 transition obligation and the "Covered Properties" definition become acutely time-sensitive.

### 2. Side Letter: Acknowledgment of Auto-Renewal Locks Steireis In for 36 More Months

**Where:** Side Letter, Recitals A–C and § 1.1. Agreement, § 3.2.

**The problem:** The Initial Term runs from December 1, 2023 to November 30, 2026 (36 months per Agreement § 3.1). Section 3.2 requires written notice of non-renewal "at least ninety (90) days before the end of the then-current term." Ninety days before November 30, 2026 is **September 1, 2026**. According to Side Letter Recital B, Steireis sent its non-renewal notice on **September 18, 2026**—seventeen days late. Nesesk's position is that the notice was untimely and the Agreement auto-renewed.

Side Letter § 1.1 asks Steireis to "acknowledge and agree" that the renewal has occurred. Signing the Side Letter would foreclose any argument that the notice was timely or that the renewal is voidable.

**Why it matters:** If Steireis signs, it is contractually bound through November 30, 2029, with the full exclusivity package described in Issue 1. The 8% fee discount (≈ $13,158/year) is the only concession Nesesk offers in exchange for this acknowledgment. The client needs to understand that it is likely locked in regardless, and the question is whether to sign the Side Letter as-is, negotiate better terms, or (less likely) challenge the renewal.

**Recommended fix / negotiating position:**

- **Do not sign the Side Letter as drafted.** The acknowledgment in § 1.1 should be conditioned on material revisions to the exclusivity scope (Issue 1) and the liability-cap amendment (Issue 3).
- If the client wishes to preserve the argument that the September 18 notice was timely (e.g., by arguing the notice was "given" when deposited with the courier rather than when received, or by raising a waiver/estoppel argument based on Nesesk's conduct), that argument is forfeited by signing § 1.1. **Flag for client:** confirm the exact date the September 18 letter was dispatched vs. received, and whether Nesesk raised the timeliness issue only after seeing the content of the notice.
- If the client accepts that the renewal is valid, use the Side Letter as the vehicle to negotiate: (a) narrowed exclusivity, (b) rejection of the § 12.3(a) amendment (Issue 3), (c) a longer data-export window, and (d) a more meaningful fee concession given the 36-month lock-in.
- Consider whether to request a shorter Renewal Term (e.g., 12 months instead of 36) or a one-time break right (e.g., Steireis may terminate on 90 days' notice after month 18 of the Renewal Term with a pro-rata fee refund).

**Flag for client:** The client should confirm the dispatch and receipt dates of the September 18, 2026 letter and whether it was sent by the method required under Agreement § 15.6 (hand delivery, overnight courier, or certified mail). If it was sent by email alone, § 15.6 provides that "email alone does not constitute notice," which would independently invalidate the non-renewal notice.

### 3. Side Letter: Amendment to Data-Security Liability Cap

**Where:** Side Letter, § 2.1 (proposed replacement of Agreement § 12.3(a)).

**The problem:** As originally drafted, Agreement § 12.3(a) carves out "Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security)" from both the consequential-damages exclusion (§ 12.1) and the 12-month-fee cap (§ 12.2). This means *any* breach of the data-security obligations—whether caused by ordinary negligence or a deliberate act—exposes Nesesk to uncapped liability.

The Side Letter would replace this with: "Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security), **to the extent resulting from Licensor's gross negligence or willful misconduct**."

The effect: a data breach caused by Nesesk's *ordinary* negligence (e.g., a misconfigured firewall, a failed patch, a social-engineering success against under-trained staff) would fall back under the 12-month-fee cap of approximately $164,472 (or $151,314 with the proposed discount). Given that a single guest-data breach involving payment card data and identification documents across 232 rooms could generate regulatory fines (NY SHIELD Act, state AG enforcement), class-action exposure, and credit-monitoring costs well in excess of $164,000, this is a material risk transfer from Steireis to Nesesk.

**Why it matters:** Steireis is the entity that faces the guests, the regulators, and the class plaintiffs. Section 11.2 (see Issue 4) already requires Steireis to indemnify Nesesk for claims arising from Guest Data. If the data-security liability cap is lowered, Steireis could be on the hook for the full cost of a breach while its recourse against Nesesk is capped at one year of fees.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Reject the Side Letter § 2.1 amendment.** Maintain the original § 12.3(a) language (uncapped for any breach of Section 9).
- If Nesesk pushes back, an acceptable compromise is to exclude from the cap any breach of Section 9 that results in an actual unauthorized access to or acquisition of Licensee Data (i.e., an actual Security Incident as defined in § 9.4), while leaving non-incident breaches (e.g., failure to deliver a SOC 2 report) subject to the cap. Drafting: *"Section 12.2 does not apply to Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 to the extent such breach results in a Security Incident as defined in Section 9.4."*
- Alternatively, if Nesesk insists on a negligence standard, raise the threshold to "negligence" (not "gross negligence"): *"to the extent resulting from Licensor's negligence or willful misconduct."*

**Flag for client:** Confirm whether Steireis carries its own cyber-liability insurance and what the policy's subrogation and consent-to-settle provisions say. If Steireis's insurer would subrogate against Nesesk after a breach, the cap amendment directly impairs the insurance recovery.

### 4. Guest Data Indemnity Is Overly Broad and Uncapped

**Where:** Agreement, § 11.2; § 12.3(b).

**The problem:** Section 11.2 requires Steireis to "defend, indemnify and hold harmless Licensor and its Affiliates, and their respective officers, directors and employees" from "any and all third-party claims, actions, regulatory investigations, losses, damages, fines, penalties, costs and expenses (including reasonable attorneys' fees) **arising out of or relating to Guest Data processed through the Licensed Software**."

This is not limited to claims caused by Steireis's own acts or omissions. Read literally, if Nesesk suffers a data breach due to its own security failure and guests sue Nesesk (or regulators fine Nesesk), Steireis must indemnify Nesesk. The indemnity is also uncapped: § 12.3(b) expressly excludes "Licensee's obligations under Section 11.2" from the liability cap in § 12.2.

The Term Sheet (§ 7.2) contemplated only an IP indemnity from Nesesk and stated that "[a]ny other indemnities will be agreed in the Definitive Agreements." There is no indication the parties discussed a broad, uncapped Licensee indemnity for Guest Data at the term-sheet stage.

**Why it matters:** Steireis processes Guest Data that includes payment card data, identification documents, and reservation history for thousands of guests across seven properties. A single significant breach could generate seven-figure regulatory and litigation exposure. As drafted, Steireis bears that exposure even where Nesesk is at fault, and has no cap on its indemnity obligation.

**Recommended fix:**

- Narrow § 11.2 to claims "arising out of or relating to Guest Data **to the extent caused by the act, omission, or negligence of Licensee or its Authorized Users**."
- Add a carve-out: "Licensee shall have no obligation under this Section 11.2 to the extent the claim arises from or is attributable to Licensor's breach of its obligations under Section 9 (Data Security) or Section 10 (Warranties)."
- Cap the indemnity: add to § 12.3(b) a proviso that the exclusion from the cap applies only to the first two sentences of § 11.2 (the Guest Data indemnity) and not to the second sentence (breach of § 2.2 restrictions), or alternatively set a super-cap (e.g., 3× annual fees) for the Guest Data indemnity.
- If Nesesk resists, at minimum require that the indemnity not apply where Licensor is found to have breached its data-security obligations.

### 5. Subscription Fees Accrue Before Go-Live

**Where:** Order Form No. 1, § 2.3.

**The problem:** "Subscription Fees accrue from the Subscription Start Date whatever the go-live date of any property." The Subscription Start Date is December 1, 2023. The target go-live is February 29, 2024. If implementation is delayed (which is common in multi-property PMS migrations), Steireis pays for up to three or more months of a system it cannot yet use.

**Why it matters:** For seven properties with 232 rooms, three months of PMS fees alone is approximately $23,664 ($94,656 ÷ 4). If go-live slips to April or May, the dead-time cost grows. Steireis is also paying implementation fees ($45,500) that are invoiced on the Order Form date and are non-refundable.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend § 2.3 to provide that Subscription Fees for each property accrue from the later of (a) the Subscription Start Date or (b) the date that property goes live. If a property has not gone live by a date certain (e.g., 90 days after the Subscription Start Date), Steireis may terminate that property's subscription without penalty and receive a pro-rata refund of prepaid fees.
- Alternatively, add a "longstop date": if any Initial Deployment Property has not gone live by [date], Steireis may terminate the Order Form in its entirety with a full refund of prepaid Subscription Fees and 50% of implementation fees.

**Flag for client:** Confirm the actual go-live dates for all seven properties. If any property went live significantly after February 29, 2024, Steireis may have a factual basis to argue that the fee-accrual provision was not the deal as understood, particularly given the Term Sheet's silence on the issue.

### 6. Warranty Remedy Is Binary: Fix or Terminate

**Where:** Agreement, § 10.2.

**The problem:** The exclusive remedy for the performance warranty (software not performing materially in accordance with Documentation) is: (a) Licensor corrects the non-conformity, or (b) if it cannot do so within 30 days, Licensee terminates the affected Order Form and receives a refund of prepaid fees for the remainder of the term. There is no middle ground—no service credits, no partial refund, no damages for the period of non-conformance.

**Why it matters:** If the PMS has a persistent bug that causes folio errors at two properties for two months, Steireis's only options are to wait for a fix or walk away from the entire contract (all seven properties, all modules). Walking away mid-contract is operationally devastating for a hotel group. Waiting means absorbing the cost of the bug with no compensation.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a tiered remedy: (1) Licensor corrects within 30 days; (2) if not corrected within 30 days, Licensee receives a service credit of [X]% of the monthly fee for the affected module for each week of continued non-conformance beyond 30 days; (3) if not corrected within 90 days, Licensee may terminate the affected Order Form with a pro-rata refund.
- Clarify that "materially in accordance with the Documentation" does not encompass minor cosmetic issues, so the remedy is proportionate.

### 7. Force Majeure Excuses Internet and Utility Failures

**Where:** Agreement, § 15.7.

**The problem:** Force majeure includes "failures of public utilities or the internet." For a cloud-based SaaS provider, internet connectivity is not an external force—it is the medium through which the service is delivered. Nesesk could argue that a backbone internet failure at its data center is force majeure, excusing it from the 99.5% availability SLA and from any liability for the resulting downtime.

**Why it matters:** If Nesesk's data center loses internet for 48 hours, Steireis's front desks cannot check in guests, process payments, or access reservation data. Under the current SLA, that might earn a 5–20% service credit. Under a force majeure claim, it earns nothing and Nesesk has no obligation to make up the time.

**Recommended fix:**

- Delete "or the internet" from the force majeure clause, or add: "provided that a failure of internet connectivity at or between Licensor's data centers shall not constitute force majeure."
- Add: "Force majeure shall not relieve Licensor of its obligation to use commercially reasonable efforts to maintain redundant network paths and failover capabilities."

### 8. Support Policy Is Undefined and Unilaterally Controllable

**Where:** Agreement, § 4.2.

**The problem:** Licensor must provide support "in accordance with its standard support policy." That policy is not attached to the Agreement, not defined, and not subject to change control. Nesesk could revise its support policy at any time (e.g., reduce Severity 1 response times, eliminate phone support, add fees for after-hours calls) and argue it is still performing under § 4.2.

**Why it matters:** The only firm commitment is 24/7 phone and online support for Severity 1 incidents. There is no defined response time, no escalation path, and no commitment for Severity 2 or 3 issues. For a hotel operating a PMS, a "Severity 2" issue (e.g., channel bridge stops syncing with OTA) could mean lost bookings for days.

**Recommended fix:**

- Attach Nesesk's current support policy as an exhibit to the Order Form and provide that any material reduction in support levels requires Steireis's written consent.
- Define severity levels and response/resolution targets in the Order Form (e.g., Severity 1: response within 1 hour, resolution target 4 hours; Severity 2: response within 4 business hours, resolution target 2 business days).
- Add a chronic-support-failure termination right analogous to the chronic-availability-failure right in Order Form § 6.3.

### 9. Suspension Right Lacks Adequate Protection

**Where:** Agreement, § 6.3.

**The problem:** If undisputed amounts remain unpaid more than 30 days after written notice of non-payment, Nesesk may suspend access to the Licensed Software. There is no requirement for a second notice, no grace period after the first notice beyond the 30 days, and no proportionality requirement (e.g., suspending only the affected property rather than all seven).

**Why it matters:** A billing dispute over a single property's prorated fee could, if mishandled, give Nesesk the right to cut off the PMS at all seven properties. For hotels, a PMS outage means front desks cannot check in guests, housekeeping cannot see assignments, and revenue is lost in real time.

**Recommended fix:**

- Require 60 days' written notice (not 30) before suspension, with a second notice 10 days before the effective suspension date.
- Limit suspension to the property or module for which fees are unpaid, not the entire subscription.
- Add: "Licensor shall not suspend access during any period in which Licensee has invoked Section 6.4 (Disputed Invoices) in good faith."

### 10. Service Credits Are Narrow and Low-Capped

**Where:** Order Form No. 1, §§ 6.1–6.3.

**The problem:**

- Credits apply only to Nesesk PMS, not to GuestConnect or Channel Bridge. A Channel Bridge outage that stops OTA bookings is arguably more revenue-impactful than a PMS slowdown, yet it earns no credit.
- The maximum credit is 20% of the monthly PMS fee (≈ $789/month at current rates). For a 52-room property, one day of PMS downtime could cost far more in lost revenue and guest dissatisfaction than $789.
- Steireis must request the credit in writing within 30 days after the affected month ends. If operations staff are focused on recovering from the outage, the deadline is easy to miss.
- The chronic-failure termination right (§ 6.3) requires below-98% availability in three months within a rolling six-month period—a high bar.

**Recommended fix:**

- Extend service credits to all three modules, with the same percentage tiers.
- Raise the monthly credit cap to 30% of the affected module's fee.
- Extend the claim window to 60 days.
- Lower the chronic-failure threshold to below 99.0% in three months within a rolling six-month period.

### 11. IP Indemnity Covers Only U.S. Rights

**Where:** Agreement, § 11.1.

**The problem:** The indemnity is limited to "any United States patent, copyright, trademark or trade secret." If Nesesk's software infringes a foreign IP right (e.g., a European patent on a reservation-algorithm feature), Steireis has no indemnity protection.

**Why it matters:** Currently all seven properties are in New York, so immediate exposure is low. However, the "Covered Properties" definition (§ 1.3) is not geographically limited. If Steireis expands internationally, the gap becomes material. Even domestically, a foreign IP holder could bring a U.S. claim based on a foreign patent if the software is used in the U.S. under certain circumstances (though this is less common).

**Recommended fix:**

- Expand to "any patent, copyright, trademark or trade secret of the United States or any other jurisdiction in which Licensee operates a Covered Property."
- If Nesesk resists, at minimum add the United Kingdom, Canada, and the European Union (where Steireis's guests may originate and where OTA partners may operate).

### 12. Data Export Window May Be Insufficient

**Where:** Agreement, § 9.6.

**The problem:** Upon expiration or termination, Licensor must make Licensee Data available for export within 30 days, then delete it 90 days after the export period ends (total: up to 120 days). The export must be in "a standard machine-readable format."

**Why it matters:** Steireis is migrating data for 232 rooms across 7 properties, including reservation history, guest profiles, and open folios. If Steireis needs to load this data into a replacement PMS, 30 days to obtain the export plus time to validate and migrate could create a data gap at go-live of the new system. The "standard machine-readable format" requirement is helpful but does not guarantee compatibility with a specific replacement platform.

**Recommended fix:**

- Extend the export availability window to 60 days.
- Add: "Licensor shall cooperate in good faith with Licensee's designated replacement vendor to ensure the exported data is in a format usable by that vendor, at no additional charge."
- Add a transitional-access right: for 30 days after termination, Licensor shall maintain read-only access to the Licensed Software so Licensee can verify data completeness before the deletion deadline.

### 13. Licensee Data License to Licensor Uses the Word "Use"

**Where:** Agreement, § 7.2.

**The problem:** Steireis grants Nesesk a license to "host, process and **use** Licensee Data solely to provide the Licensed Software and related services to Licensee." The word "use" is broader than "process" and could be argued to permit Nesesk to derive analytics, build aggregate datasets, or improve its product using Steireis's guest data, so long as it claims the purpose is "to provide the Licensed Software."

**Why it matters:** Guest Data includes names, contact details, reservation history, payment card data, identification documents, and preferences (§ 1.6). If Nesesk uses this data to train ML models or build guest-profiling products for other hotel clients, Steireis's guests' privacy is compromised and Steireis could face regulatory exposure under the NY SHIELD Act and other privacy laws.

**Recommended fix:**

- Replace "host, process and use" with "host and process."
- Add: "Licensor shall not use Licensee Data for any purpose other than providing the Licensed Software to Licensee, including without limitation for analytics, machine learning, product development, or benefit to any third party, unless Licensee provides prior written consent."

## Summary of Recommended Negotiating Posture on the Side Letter

The Side Letter should **not** be signed as presented. It bundles three things: (1) an acknowledgment of renewal that forfeits Steireis's best procedural argument; (2) an 8% fee discount that is modest relative to the 36-month lock-in; and (3) a liability-cap amendment that materially increases Steireis's data-breach risk.

The recommended approach is to respond with a counter-proposal that:

1. **Does not include the bare acknowledgment in § 1.1.** Instead, frames the Side Letter as a mutual amendment: "The Parties agree that the Agreement renews effective December 1, 2026, *and* the Parties agree to the modifications set forth below."
2. **Rejects the § 12.3(a) amendment** (Issue 3) or accepts only the "actual Security Incident" compromise.
3. **Adds an exclusivity-scope amendment** conforming Order Form § 5 to the Term Sheet (Issue 1).
4. **Adds a Guest Data indemnity carve-out** for Nesesk-caused breaches (Issue 4).
5. **Raises the fee discount** to 12–15% or adds a one-time break right at month 18 of the Renewal Term, as consideration for Steireis's acceptance of the renewal.
6. **Extends the data-export window** to 60 days (Issue 12).

This positions Steireis as reasonable and business-focused while protecting its most critical operational and financial interests.
