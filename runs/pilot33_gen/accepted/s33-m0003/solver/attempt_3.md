## Issue 1 – Assignment of the Key Employee Agreement Will Trigger a Good Reason Resignation Right and Release of Restrictive Covenants

**Where:** APA, Schedule 2.1(c) item 2 (listing the KEA as an Assigned Contract); KEA, Section 9.2 (prohibiting assignment without Executive's consent, expressly including "a sale of all or substantially all of the Employer's assets"); KEA, Section 6.2(e) (defining Good Reason to include "any assignment or purported assignment of this Agreement in violation of Section 9.2"); KEA, Section 7.5 (releasing the non-compete and non-solicitation covenants upon a Good Reason resignation); KEA, Section 6.3 (18 months' severance plus COBRA upon Good Reason resignation).

**Why it matters:** The APA treats the KEA as an Assigned Contract to be transferred at Closing. But the KEA flatly prohibits assignment in connection with a sale of substantially all assets without Venkataraman's prior written consent. If Sipoun proceeds with the assignment without her consent, three things happen simultaneously: (1) she acquires an immediate Good Reason resignation right; (2) if she resigns, her 18-month non-compete and non-solicitation covenants are released (KEA § 7.5); and (3) she is owed 18 months of base salary ($397,500) plus COBRA premiums (KEA § 6.3).

The operational stakes are high. The KEA recitals state that Venkataraman's knowledge of "cheese-make protocols, culture selection, affinage and cave management" is "central to the Employer's product quality and reputation" (KEA, Recital B). She is the Master Cheesemaker with authority over all cheese production and lot release (KEA § 1.2). Losing her — or having her leave within weeks of closing with no restrictive covenant protection — could undermine the core value of the acquisition.

Even if consent is obtained, the APA's fallback in Section 2.5 (Non-Assignable Assets) contemplates that Seller will "hold that Assigned Contract for Buyer's benefit" if consent is not obtained at Closing. That is an awkward and unstable arrangement: Sipoun would be performing obligations under a contract it does not own, with Seller as nominal employer of record.

**Recommended fix:** Before going back to the other side, make obtaining Venkataraman's written consent to the assignment a condition to Closing (it should be added to APA § 8.2(c), alongside the Landlord and distribution-agreement consents). In parallel, open a direct dialogue with Venkataraman to secure her consent and confirm her willingness to continue with Sipoun. If she is not willing, reassess whether the deal economics still work without the Master Cheesemaker. As a fallback, negotiate a new employment agreement with Sipoun directly (rather than an assignment of the KEA) that includes appropriate restrictive covenants and retention incentives.

## Issue 2 – Sipoun Assumes All Pre- and Post-Closing Product Liability with No Cap

**Where:** APA, Section 2.3(e) (Assumed Liabilities include "all Liabilities arising out of or relating to the Products, including product warranty, product recall, product liability, contamination and organic-certification Liabilities, whether arising before or after the Closing"); APA, Section 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities are everything else); APA, Section 9.3(b) (Buyer indemnifies Seller for Assumed Liabilities); APA, Section 5.6 (Seller's rep listing known recalls and contamination notices); APA, Section 9.5(b) (Seller's indemnification cap of $1,840,000 for rep breaches); APA, Section 9.5(c) ($184,000 deductible before Seller is liable).

**Why it matters:** This is the single largest unbounded financial exposure in the document set. By assuming product liability "whether arising before or after the Closing," Sipoun takes on the risk of any contamination, recall, or organic-certification failure that occurred in the pre-Closing period but manifests after Closing. For a dairy and cheese business, this is a realistic scenario: bacterial contamination (e.g., Listeria, E. coli) or allergen cross-contact can have a latency period of weeks or months before it is detected in the field.

Because this is an Assumed Liability (not an Excluded Liability), Seller's indemnification under APA § 9.2 does not cover it. The only potential recourse against Seller is a claim that Seller breached its representation in § 5.6 (i.e., it failed to disclose a known issue). That claim is subject to the $1,840,000 cap and the $184,000 deductible. A single significant recall in the dairy industry can easily exceed $5 million in direct costs (product destruction, customer credits, regulatory fines, litigation) before reputational harm.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a carve-out from Section 2.3(e) so that pre-Closing product liability is an Excluded Liability (i.e., retained by Seller), with Seller indemnifying Sipoun for it under Section 9.2(c). If Seller resists, at minimum: (a) increase the indemnification cap to a level that reflects realistic product-liability exposure (e.g., $5 million or a percentage of the purchase price that is higher than 10%); (b) convert the $184,000 deductible to a tipping basket (so that once the threshold is crossed, Seller is liable for the full amount, not just the excess); and (c) require Seller to maintain product-liability insurance through the survival period and name Sipoun as an additional insured or loss payee. Also, review the Schedule 5.6 disclosure (not in the folder) to confirm what, if anything, Seller has disclosed.

## Issue 3 – Seller Note Has No Grace Period for Principal Payment

**Where:** Note, Section 2.1 (entire Outstanding Principal due on the Maturity Date, 36 months after Closing); Note, Section 7.1(a) (failure to pay "any principal of this Note when due" is an immediate Event of Default — no cure period); Note, Section 3.3 (default interest at 8.0%); Note, Section 9.4 (Maker pays Holder's collection costs and attorneys' fees upon default).

**Why it matters:** The Note is a bullet: the full $3,200,000 principal is due on a single date 36 months after Closing. There is no grace period for principal (contrast the 10-Business-Day grace period for interest). If Sipoun is even one day late in making the maturity payment — whether due to a wire transfer delay, a banking holiday issue, or a temporary cash-flow problem — it is in default. The consequences are severe: the interest rate jumps from 6.0% to 8.0%, and Sipoun must pay Seller's attorneys' fees and collection costs (Note § 9.4). Given that the Note is subordinated to all future senior debt (Note § 6.1), a cash-flow squeeze from a senior lender could make it difficult to make the Note payment on time.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a grace period for principal (e.g., 10 or 15 Business Days, matching the interest grace period). Alternatively, add a provision that a payment made by wire transfer is deemed timely if initiated before the due date, even if received the next Business Day. At minimum, confirm that Sipoun's treasury function has a process to ensure the wire is initiated well in advance of the Maturity Date.

## Issue 4 – Governing Law and Forum Mismatch Between the APA and the Seller Note

**Where:** APA, Section 11.7 (New York governing law); APA, Section 11.8 (exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts); Note, Section 10.1 (Delaware governing law); Note, Section 10.2 (exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery); APA, Section 11.9 (Ancillary Agreement controls over the APA "as to the subject matter of that Ancillary Agreement"); APA, Section 9.8 and Note, Section 5 (setoff right).

**Why it matters:** The setoff right is the principal mechanism by which Sipoun will enforce its indemnification claims against the $3.2 million Note. A setoff dispute would require (a) determining under New York law whether the underlying indemnification claim is valid and properly calculated (governed by the APA), and (b) determining under Delaware law whether the setoff is properly exercised under the Note. The APA's order-of-precedence clause (§ 11.9) gives the Note control "as to the subject matter of that Ancillary Agreement," which likely means Delaware law governs the setoff mechanics. But the substantive right to set off depends on the validity of the APA indemnification claim, which is a New York law question.

This split creates the risk of inconsistent outcomes, increased litigation cost (two jurisdictions, two bodies of law), and procedural complexity. It also means that if Seller accelerates the Note in Delaware for a technical default, Sipoun's setoff defense must be raised in Delaware, but the underlying indemnification claim may need to be litigated or at least analyzed under New York law.

**Recommended fix:** Conform the Note's governing law and forum to New York (matching the APA). If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps for tax or other reasons), at minimum add a provision to the Note stating that any setoff dispute shall be governed by New York law to the extent it involves the validity or calculation of an indemnification claim under the APA.

## Issue 5 – Office Lease Assignment Requires Sipoun to Meet a Net Worth Threshold We Cannot Verify

**Where:** Lease, Section 10.3 (Landlord "shall not withhold consent" to an assignment in connection with a sale of substantially all assets if the assignee has "a net worth, immediately after the acquisition, at least equal to Tenant's net worth on the date of this Lease"); APA, Section 8.2(c)(i) (Landlord consent is a condition to Closing).

**Why it matters:** The Lease was signed on September 1, 2022. We do not have Seller's balance sheet as of that date. If Seller was well-capitalized in 2022 (e.g., with significant equity or low debt), its net worth could be high. After paying approximately $15.2 million in cash at Closing (APA § 3.2(a) and (c)) and taking on the $3.2 million Note, Sipoun's net worth "immediately after the acquisition" could be lower than Seller's 2022 net worth. If Sipoun cannot meet the threshold, Landlord is not obligated to consent, and Closing is blocked.

This is a condition to Closing that Sipoun cannot currently confirm it will satisfy.

**Recommended fix:** Request Seller's balance sheet as of September 1, 2022 (or the most recent available) to determine the net worth benchmark. Then model Sipoun's post-closing balance sheet to confirm it will exceed that amount. If there is a gap, negotiate with Landlord to modify Section 10.3(b) — for example, replacing the net-worth test with a fixed financial threshold (e.g., "net worth of at least $X") or a letter of credit. Alternatively, add the net worth test as a specific item in the due-diligence checklist and make it a condition in the APA that is outside Sipoun's control (so that if it fails, Sipoun can walk without penalty).

## Issue 6 – Escrow Exhaustion Requirement and Practical Collectability from Seller

**Where:** APA, Section 9.7 (Buyer must first seek recovery from the $920,000 Escrow Amount before going after Seller directly); APA, Section 9.5(b) (Seller's total indemnification cap is $1,840,000); APA, Section 3.2(c) (Escrow Amount is $920,000).

**Why it matters:** Sipoun's maximum recovery under the indemnification is $1,840,000, but the first $920,000 must come from escrow. The remaining $920,000 must be collected from Seller directly. After the Closing, Seller will have received approximately $14.28 million in cash (APA § 3.2(a)) plus the $3.2 million Note. If Seller distributes those proceeds to its shareholders (as is typical after a sale of substantially all assets), the entity may be a hollow shell with no assets to satisfy a judgment. The APA does not contain any provision requiring Seller to maintain a minimum net worth or restricting distributions post-Closing.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a provision requiring Seller to maintain the $920,000 (or the full $1,840,000) in assets available to satisfy indemnification claims through the survival period, or to restrict distributions to shareholders until the survival period expires. Alternatively, increase the escrow amount to the full $1,840,000 cap so that all indemnification recovery comes from escrow and there is no need to chase Seller.

## Issue 7 – Forward-Looking Good Reason Triggers in the KEA Create Integration Risk

**Where:** KEA, Section 6.2(a) (Good Reason includes "a material diminution in the Executive's title, authority, duties or responsibilities, including removal of her authority over cheese production or lot release"); KEA, Section 6.2(c) (Good Reason includes relocation of principal place of performance by more than 50 miles from Kingston); KEA, Section 6.3 (severance upon Good Reason resignation).

**Why it matters:** Even if Sipoun obtains Venkataraman's consent to the assignment and she continues in her role, any post-closing reorganization that changes her title, removes her authority over cheese production or lot release, or relocates her more than 50 miles from Kingston will trigger a Good Reason resignation right. Given that Sipoun is a holding company that may have a different organizational structure than the standalone creamery, this is a realistic risk during integration. The consequence is the same as in Issue 1: loss of the Master Cheesemaker plus release of restrictive covenants plus a severance payment.

**Recommended fix:** In the post-closing integration plan, ensure that Venkataraman's title, reporting structure, and authority over cheese production and lot release remain substantially the same as under the KEA. If any changes are contemplated, obtain her written agreement in advance (which would negate the Good Reason trigger under the "without the Executive's written agreement" language in § 6.2). Confirm that the Kingston office will remain her principal place of performance.

## Issue 8 – Five-Year Non-Compete May Be Difficult to Enforce Against a Dissolved Entity

**Where:** APA, Section 7.6 (Seller shall not engage in organic dairy or cheese production or sale in five states for five years post-Closing).

**Why it matters:** The non-compete binds "Seller" — i.e., Fupurt Valley Creamery, Inc. After selling substantially all of its assets, the corporation may be dissolved or allowed to lapse. If Seller is dissolved, enforcing the non-compete becomes difficult: there is no entity to enjoin, and the individual shareholders or officers are not parties to the covenant. New York courts have enforced non-competes in the sale-of-business context, but a five-year duration is at the outer limit, and enforceability against a dissolved entity is uncertain.

**Recommended fix:** Add the individual principals of Seller (e.g., its officers and shareholders) as parties to the non-compete, or include a provision that the non-compete survives dissolution and binds Seller's successors and the individuals who owned Seller at Closing. Consider whether five years is necessary or whether a shorter period (e.g., three years) would be more likely to be enforced.

## Issue 9 – 18-Month Survival Period May Be Inadequate for Regulatory and Compliance Issues

**Where:** APA, Section 9.1 (representations and warranties survive for 18 months, except fundamental reps which survive longer); APA, Section 5.5 (Seller's compliance and permits representation); APA, Section 5.6 (Seller's product recall/contamination representation).

**Why it matters:** Eighteen months is a relatively short survival period for a regulated food business. USDA organic certification issues, state dairy plant license problems, or food-safety compliance failures that were latent at Closing may not surface until after 18 months. Once the representation has expired, Sipoun has no indemnification recourse (and the exclusive-remedy clause in APA § 9.9 bars other claims, except fraud and specific performance).

**Recommended fix:** Extend the survival period for Sections 5.5 (Compliance; Permits) and 5.6 (Products) to at least 24–36 months, or tie them to the applicable regulatory look-back period. If Seller resists, at minimum extend the survival for the organic-certification representation to cover at least one full certification audit cycle.

## Issue 10 – Seller Note Subordination Is Broad and Could Create Relationship Tension

**Where:** Note, Section 6.1 (payment subordinated to "all indebtedness of Maker for borrowed money owed to banks, insurance companies or other institutional lenders, whether outstanding on the Issue Date or incurred later"); Note, Section 6.2 (block on payments only if a default under Senior Indebtedness is continuing and notice has been given); Note, Section 6.3 (Seller can require Sipoun to enter into a subordination agreement with any future senior lender).

**Why it matters:** While the subordination is protective for Sipoun as Maker (it gives Sipoun the right to delay payments to Seller if it has senior debt), Section 6.3 gives Seller the right to require Sipoun to execute a subordination agreement with any future institutional lender on "terms customary for seller subordinated debt." This could give Seller a veto-like influence over Sipoun's future financing arrangements. If Sipoun needs to raise a new bank loan, the lender may require the Seller Note to be subordinated, and Seller could use Section 6.3 to negotiate terms (or a fee) as a condition.

**Recommended fix:** Limit Section 6.3 to require that the subordination agreement be on terms "reasonably acceptable to Maker" (rather than "customary for seller subordinated debt," which is vague and could be used to extract concessions). Alternatively, delete Section 6.3 and provide that any subordination to future senior debt will be addressed in the loan documents themselves.

Both go to the core value of the acquisition: the people and the regulatory risk profile. Issue 3 (no grace period on the Note) is a fixable drafting point that should be raised in the next turn.
