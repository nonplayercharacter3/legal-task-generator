## Executive Summary

The current document set leaves Daikesk exposed on several fronts, the most serious being a provision in Dr. Ferreol's employment agreement that causes the Company's exclusive license to four core detector-calibration patents to **terminate automatically at closing** unless she consents in writing beforehand. That consent is not a condition to closing, not on the consent schedule, and not addressed anywhere in the SPA. In addition, the Transition Services Agreement omits entire categories of services the LOI promised, caps the Seller's liability for data mishandling at a fraction of what the SPA provides, and permits the Seller to assign its data-handling obligations to any third party without consent.

The issues below are ordered by significance.

## Issue 1 – Ferreol Patent License Terminates Automatically at Closing

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.3 (final two sentences); § 1.1 (definition of "Change of Control"); SPA § 4.9, Schedule 4.5, Article VII.

**The problem.** Section 9.3 grants the Company an exclusive, royalty-free, worldwide license under the Detector Calibration Patents (U.S. Patents 9,846,213; 10,112,587; 10,559,042, and Application 16/904,318 – see Exhibit A to the Key Employee Agreement). The license covers the right to "make, have made, use, sell, offer for sale, import and service Company Products." The patents relate to pixel-level gain/offset calibration, defect-map correction, temperature-compensated dark-field calibration, and automated field recalibration of flat-panel DR detectors – squarely at the heart of the Company's digital radiography product line.

The final sentence of § 9.3 provides:

> "The license and all rights of the Company under this Section 9.3 shall terminate automatically upon a Change of Control unless the Company has obtained the Employee's prior written consent to that Change of Control."

The definition of "Change of Control" in § 1.1(a) expressly includes "any sale of a majority of the Company's stock." Daikesk's acquisition of 100% of the Shares is unambiguously a Change of Control.

**Why it matters.** Absent Ferreol's prior written consent, the Company's right to use these patents evaporates on the Closing Date. Daikesk would pay $186.5 million for a company that, from day one, lacks the license to practice core calibration technology. The SPA's IP representation (§ 4.9) states the Company has "a valid and enforceable right to use" all Company IP "necessary for the conduct of the Business as currently conducted" – but that representation is made as of the signing date and the Closing Date. If the license terminates at Closing, the representation is arguably true at signing but false at closing, creating a messy indemnification claim that is itself subject to the 10% cap and 18-month survival period. More practically, litigating a license-termination indemnity claim while the Company cannot sell its core product is a catastrophic outcome.

The consent is not listed on Schedule 4.5 (which contains only the Meridian Health Purchasing Alliance consent), is not a condition to Buyer's closing obligation (Article VII), and is not a Closing deliverable (Section 3.2). The LOI (§ 6) shows Buyer was on notice that it would review "any intellectual property licensed to the Company by those personnel," yet the definitive documents do not address the consequence of that review.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Add Ferreol's written consent to the Change of Control as a condition to Closing.** Insert new Section 7.2(g):

> "(g) Dr. Tamsin Ferreol shall have delivered to Buyer her prior written consent to the Change of Control contemplated by this Agreement, in form and substance satisfactory to Buyer, which consent shall irrevocably waive the automatic termination of the license under Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement effective as of the Closing Date."

2. **Add the consent as a Closing deliverable.** Expand Section 3.2 to include:

> "(g) the written consent of Dr. Tamsin Ferreol to the Change of Control, in the form required by Section 7.2(g)."

3. **Add the consent to Schedule 4.5** so it is captured by the existing consent condition in Section 7.2(e) and the "No Conflicts" representation in Section 4.4(c).

4. **Require an amendment to the Key Employee Agreement as a condition to Closing.** The cleanest solution is to have Ferreol execute a consent/amendment that (a) consents to this specific Change of Control, (b) confirms the license survives the transaction, and (c) ideally removes or narrows the automatic-termination trigger for future transfers (e.g., limiting it to a sale to a direct competitor). Proposed language for the amendment:

> "Employee hereby consents to the acquisition of all outstanding shares of Company stock by Daikesk Imaging Holdings, Inc. (or its designee) pursuant to the Stock Purchase Agreement dated August 14, 2026. The exclusive license granted under Section 9.3 shall not terminate by reason of such Change of Control. Employee further agrees that the automatic termination provision in Section 9.3 shall not apply to any subsequent transfer of Company stock to an Affiliate of Daikesk Imaging Holdings, Inc. or to a successor in interest to Daikesk Imaging Holdings, Inc."

5. **If Ferreol will not consent**, Daikesk should treat this as a potential walk-away or re-pricing issue. The value of the digital radiography line depends on this license.

**Questions for the client:**
- Has anyone on the deal team spoken with Dr. Ferreol about the consent? What is her posture?
- What is the revenue contribution of the digital radiography detector line (as opposed to ultrasound) to the Company's total? This will inform the downside if the license is lost.
- Is Daikesk prepared to make this a condition to closing, or does it want to proceed and rely on indemnification? (We strongly advise the former.)

## Issue 2 – TSA Omits Field-Service and Ultrasound Transition Services

**Where:** TSA Schedule A (Services 1–6); TSA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); LOI § 5(b)–(c); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Business").

**The problem.** The LOI (§ 5) committed Seller to providing transition services that include:

> "(b) support for the Company's field-service operations, including dispatch, service-ticketing systems, spare-parts logistics and access to installed-base service records; and
> (c) support for the Company's ultrasound operations, including shared transducer sourcing, regulatory and complaint-handling systems, and distributor order processing."

The TSA Schedule A contains six services: IT/ERP hosting, data migration, detector supply chain, procurement, payroll/benefits, and finance/accounting. **Neither field-service operations support nor ultrasound operations support appears in the TSA.**

Compounding this, the TSA defines "Business" (§ 1.1) as "the manufacture of digital radiography detectors as conducted by Recipient as of the Closing Date" – a far narrower scope than the SPA's definition ("the design, manufacture, sale and servicing of digital radiography and ultrasound imaging systems"). Because the TSA's service obligation (§ 2.1) is tied to "the Business," the narrow definition further excludes ultrasound and field-service activities from the scope of services Provider is obligated to support.

**Why it matters.** The LOI is non-binding, but it reflects the commercial understanding on which Daikesk agreed to pay $186.5 million. If the Company's field-service organization (dispatch, ticketing, spare-parts logistics, installed-base records) and ultrasound operations (transducer sourcing, regulatory/complaint handling, distributor order processing) are currently run through Glosiv's systems, Daikesk will lose that support at closing with no contractual right to it under the TSA. The 12-month transition window becomes useless for those functions.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Expand the "Business" definition in the TSA** to match the SPA:

> "'Business' means the design, manufacture, sale and servicing of digital radiography and ultrasound imaging systems as conducted by Recipient as of the Closing Date."

2. **Add the missing services to Schedule A.** At minimum:

| No. | Service | Description | Fees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Field-Service Operations | Dispatch and service-ticketing system access; spare-parts logistics and inventory management; installed-base service records and warranty tracking; field-service reporting. | Fully loaded cost, invoiced monthly |
| 8 | Ultrasound Operations | Shared transducer sourcing and supply; regulatory and complaint-handling system access (including FDA MDR reporting); distributor order processing; ultrasound product software support. | Fully loaded cost, invoiced monthly |

3. **Confirm in the SPA** that the TSA is a Closing deliverable (it is – § 3.2(b)) and that the executed TSA conforms to the expanded scope. Consider adding a representation or covenant that Seller will not degrade or withdraw any service currently provided to the Company that is listed in Schedule A before the Closing.

**Questions for the client:**
- Which of the Company's current systems and processes for field service and ultrasound are actually hosted or administered by Glosiv? We need to confirm the operational dependency before we can scope the services.
- Is there a timeline for when Daikesk expects to stand up its own field-service and ultrasound infrastructure? If 12 months is insufficient, we should negotiate a longer TSA term or a specific extension right for these services.

## Issue 3 – TSA Caps Data-Breach Liability at Six Months of Fees, Overriding the SPA

**Where:** TSA § 10.2 (second sentence); SPA § 9.2, § 9.4(b), § 4.11.

**The problem.** TSA § 10.2 provides:

> "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in the Purchase Agreement, any claim arising from the use or disclosure by Provider or its Affiliates of Company Data or Confidential Information in connection with the Services is subject to, and limited by, this Section 10.2."

Section 10.2 caps Provider's aggregate liability at "the Fees paid by Recipient under this Agreement in the six months preceding the event giving rise to the claim."

Company Data is defined (TSA § 1.1) to include "protected health information processed by or for Recipient." The SPA's data-privacy representation (§ 4.11) and the general indemnification (§ 9.2(a)) would otherwise provide broader protection, and the SPA's confidentiality covenant (§ 6.7) is carved out from the 10% cap (§ 9.4(b)). But the TSA provision expressly overrides the SPA for claims arising "in connection with the Services."

**Why it matters.** If Glosiv suffers a data breach involving Company PHI during the 12-month transition period – or if it negligently discloses Company Data to a third party – Daikesk's recovery is limited to six months of transition fees. If those fees are, say, $500,000/month, the cap is $3 million. A single HIPAA breach involving thousands of patient records could generate regulatory fines, class-action exposure, and remediation costs well in excess of that. The "notwithstanding" language makes this an intentional override, not an ambiguity.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Delete the second sentence of TSA § 10.2** in its entirety. The SPA already provides an appropriate framework: data-privacy breaches tied to pre-closing conduct are covered by the § 4.11 representation (subject to the 10% cap), and post-closing confidentiality breaches by Seller are covered by § 6.7 (uncapped up to the Purchase Price under § 9.4(b)).

2. **If Seller resists deletion**, narrow the override to exclude PHI:

> "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in the Purchase Agreement, any claim arising from the use or disclosure by Provider or its Affiliates of Confidential Information (other than protected health information) in connection with the Services is subject to, and limited by, this Section 10.2. Claims arising from the unauthorized access, use or disclosure of protected health information shall be governed by Article IX of the Purchase Agreement."

3. **Add a specific indemnity in the TSA** for data-security failures:

> "Provider shall indemnify and hold harmless Recipient and its Affiliates (including Buyer) against all Losses arising from any unauthorized access to, use of, or disclosure of Company Data caused by Provider's or its subcontractors' breach of Section 7.3, regardless of the cap in Section 10.2."

**Questions for the client:**
- What is the estimated monthly fee under the TSA? We need this number to quantify the exposure under the current cap.
- Does Daikesk have its own cyber-insurance program that would cover a transition-period data breach? If so, the subrogation rights may be affected by the TSA cap.

## Issue 4 – Ferreol Retention Risk and Short Non-Compete

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 7.2 (Change of Control Termination); § 3.2 (auto-renewal); § 10.1 (non-compete, 12 months); § 9.2 (she retains patent ownership).

**The problem.** Several features of the Ferreol agreement create retention and IP risk post-closing:

- **Enhanced severance on CoC termination (§ 7.2):** If Ferreol is terminated without Cause or resigns for Good Reason within 12 months after the Change of Control, she receives 18 months' salary ($517,500), a lump sum of 1.5× target bonus (~$207,000), and full acceleration of unvested equity. Total cash outlay: approximately $724,500 plus equity.
- **Auto-renewal with 90-day non-renewal notice (§ 3.2):** Her current renewal term runs through March 15, 2027. She can prevent the next renewal by giving notice by approximately December 15, 2026. If she does, her employment (and the patent license, if not terminated by the CoC provision) ends in early 2027.
- **Non-compete is only 12 months (§ 10.1):** After her employment ends (for any reason other than Cause), she is restricted from competing for just 12 months. She remains the owner of the patents throughout.
- **She owns the patents (§ 9.2):** The Company never acquired title. Post-employment, she is free to license the patents to competitors (subject to the 12-month non-compete), develop competing products, or simply let the patents lapse.

**Why it matters.** The combination of (a) automatic license termination at CoC (Issue 1), (b) enhanced severance if she leaves within 12 months post-CoC, (c) a short non-compete, and (d) her retained patent ownership means Ferreol has significant leverage and a low-cost exit path that could strip the Company of its core technology. If she is dissatisfied with Daikesk's post-closing integration, she can resign for Good Reason (triggered by, e.g., a "material diminution in title, authority or duties"), collect ~$725,000, and within 12 months be free to work for a competitor with full ownership of the calibration patents.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Negotiate a new employment or consulting agreement with Ferreol as a condition to Closing.** The new agreement should:
   - Extend the term (e.g., 3 years post-closing) with a meaningful retention bonus or equity grant;
   - Extend the non-compete to at least 24–36 months post-termination;
   - Either (a) assign the Detector Calibration Patents to the Company (with appropriate consideration) or (b) convert the license to a perpetual, irrevocable, survive-Change-of-Control license that is not terminable by her;
   - Remove or narrow the enhanced CoC severance (e.g., limit the 12-month CoC window to 6 months, or reduce the multiplier).

2. **If a new agreement is not feasible before signing**, at minimum require Ferreol's written consent to the CoC (per Issue 1) coupled with a covenant not to terminate her employment or prevent renewal for a specified period (e.g., 18 months post-closing), with a liquidated-damages provision for early departure.

3. **Add a covenant in the SPA** requiring Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to negotiate a retention arrangement with Ferreol satisfactory to Buyer before the Closing.

**Questions for the client:**
- Is Daikesk willing to offer Ferreol new equity or a retention bonus as the quid pro quo for (a) CoC consent, (b) a longer non-compete, and (c) a commitment to stay?
- What is the Company's equity structure? Does Ferreol hold any equity awards that would accelerate under § 7.2(c)? We need to understand the full cost of her departure.
- Is Daikesk open to acquiring the patents outright (assignment) rather than relying on a license?

## Issue 5 – Seller Non-Compete Subject to 10% Indemnification Cap

**Where:** SPA § 6.8 (5-year non-compete); § 9.4(b) (carve-outs from cap).

**The problem.** Section 6.8 imposes a 5-year non-compete and non-solicitation on Seller covering North America and Europe. However, Section 9.4(b) carves out from the 10% cap only: Fraud, Fundamental Representations, and Section 6.7 (Confidentiality). **Section 6.8 (Non-Competition) is not carved out.** This means if Glosiv launches a competing medical imaging business in year two, Daikesk's maximum recovery is $18,650,000 (10% of the Purchase Price) – roughly 10% of what it paid.

**Why it matters.** The non-compete is one of the primary protections for the $186.5 million investment. A 5-year restriction in a $186.5M deal that is backed by only a $18.65M remedy is significantly under-secured. If Glosiv competes, the real economic harm (lost customers, eroded market position, diverted talent) could far exceed $18.65M over five years.

**Recommended fix.**

Add Section 6.8 to the carve-out list in § 9.4(b):

> "(b) The limitations in Section 9.4(a) do not apply to Losses arising out of Fraud, any breach of the Fundamental Representations, any breach of Section 6.7 (Confidentiality), **or any breach of Section 6.8 (Non-Competition; Non-Solicitation)**. Seller's aggregate liability for those Losses shall not exceed the Purchase Price, except in the case of Fraud."

If Seller resists full uncapped treatment, a compromise is to set a separate, higher cap for non-compete breaches (e.g., 25–50% of the Purchase Price) while keeping the general 10% cap for other reps.

## Issue 6 – 18-Month Survival Period for General Representations

**Where:** SPA § 9.1.

**The problem.** General representations and warranties survive for only 18 months post-closing. For a medical device company, this is short. FDA enforcement actions, adverse event reports, and data-privacy incidents often have a lag time. A 510(k) clearance obtained years earlier could be recalled or a quality-system deficiency could be identified by the FDA more than 18 months after closing. Similarly, a data breach that occurred just before closing might not be discovered within 18 months.

**Why it matters.** The 18-month clock starts at Closing. If the deal closes in, say, November 2026, general reps (including § 4.10 FDA compliance and § 4.11 data privacy) expire in May 2028. Any FDA investigation or data-privacy claim arising from pre-closing conduct that surfaces after May 2028 is not indemnifiable (absent Fraud).

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Extend survival for regulatory and data-privacy reps to 24–30 months**, or tie them to the applicable statute of limitations (whichever is shorter). Proposed language:

> "The representations and warranties in this Agreement survive the Closing for 18 months, except that (a) the Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, (b) the representations in Section 4.10 (Regulatory Compliance) and Section 4.11 (Data Privacy) survive until the earlier of (i) 30 months after the Closing Date or (ii) the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, and (c) covenants survive until fully performed."

2. **Alternatively**, negotiate a specific indemnity (outside the survival period) for FDA regulatory matters attributable to pre-closing conduct, capped at a defined amount.

**Questions for the client:**
- What is the Company's FDA inspection history? Any open 483s or warning letters? If so, the 18-month window may be insufficient.
- Does Daikesk's own compliance team expect to identify any data-privacy or regulatory issues in the first 12–18 months post-closing?

## Issue 7 – TSA Permits Provider to Assign Without Recipient Consent

**Where:** TSA § 12.4.

**The problem.** Section 12.4 provides:

> "Provider may assign this Agreement, or delegate the performance of any of the Services, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient."

This means Glosiv can transfer its obligations – including hosting the Company's ERP, processing payroll, handling PHI, and managing the detector supply chain – to any third party it chooses, with no approval right for Daikesk or the Company.

**Why it matters.** Company Data (including PHI) would be in the hands of an unknown entity. The data-security obligations in § 7.3 and the confidentiality obligations in § 7.1 bind "Provider" – if Provider assigns, the assignee steps into those shoes, but Daikesk has no ability to vet the assignee's security posture, financial stability, or competitive relationships. In the medical device space, this is a significant risk.

**Recommended fix.**

Replace § 12.4 with:

> "Neither Party may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of the other Party, such consent not to be unreasonably withheld. Notwithstanding the foregoing, Provider may delegate the performance of any Service to a subcontractor, provided that (a) Provider remains fully responsible for the subcontractor's performance and compliance with this Agreement, (b) the subcontractor is bound by confidentiality and data-security obligations no less protective than those in this Agreement, and (c) Provider gives Recipient at least 30 days' prior written notice identifying the subcontractor and the scope of delegation. Recipient may object to any proposed subcontractor on reasonable grounds related to data security, regulatory compliance, or competitive conflict."

## Issue 8 – Only One Third-Party Consent Identified

**Where:** SPA Schedule 4.5; § 4.4(c); § 4.5; § 7.2(e).

**The problem.** Schedule 4.5 lists a single required consent: Meridian Health Purchasing Alliance under a Group Purchasing Agreement dated September 1, 2023. For a company with an installed base of imaging systems, field-service operations, a supply chain for detector components, and (per the LOI) ultrasound operations with distributors, this appears thin.

The "Material Contracts" definition (§ 4.8) captures contracts involving annual payments over $500,000, IP licenses, officer employment agreements, GPO/distribution arrangements, and non-competes. Any of these could contain change-of-control or assignment consent provisions.

**Why it matters.** If a material contract requires consent that has not been identified, the Company could be in breach at or after closing, or the counterparty could terminate. The SPA's condition in § 7.2(e) only requires the consents "listed on Schedule 4.5" – so unlisted consents are not gating items. This shifts the risk to Daikesk post-closing, where it would need to rely on the (capped) indemnification for a breach of the § 4.4(c) or § 4.5 representation.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Require Seller to update and expand Schedule 4.5** before signing (or as a condition to closing) to include all consents required under Material Contracts. Add a specific representation:

> "Schedule 4.5 lists all consents, approvals, waivers or authorizations required from any counterparty under any Material Contract in connection with the transactions contemplated by this Agreement."

2. **Consider adding a "change of control" covenant** requiring Seller to notify Buyer of any consent request received from a counterparty between signing and closing, and to use best efforts to obtain it.

**Questions for the client:**
- What did due diligence reveal about the Company's material contracts? Are there GPO agreements, distributor agreements, supplier agreements, or service contracts with change-of-control or anti-assignment provisions?
- Is the Meridian Health Purchasing Alliance agreement the Company's primary revenue channel? If so, is the consent likely to be granted?

## Issue 9 – TSA Governing Law and Forum Differ from SPA

**Where:** TSA § 13.2 (New York law; Manhattan courts); SPA § 11.8 (Delaware law); § 11.9 (Delaware Chancery/Superior Court).

**The problem.** The TSA is governed by New York law and subject to exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan, while the SPA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware. Because the TSA expressly overrides the SPA in § 10.2 (see Issue 3), a dispute involving both agreements could require litigation in two forums under two bodies of law.

**Why it matters.** This creates procedural complexity and potential for inconsistent outcomes. For example, if a data breach by Glosiv during the transition period implicates both the TSA (service failure) and the SPA (confidentiality covenant breach under § 6.7), the governing law and forum for each claim could differ.

**Recommended fix.**

Conform TSA § 13.2 to the SPA:

> "This Agreement and all claims arising out of or relating to it are governed by the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to conflict-of-laws principles. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of the State of Delaware (or, if that court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, the Superior Court of the State of Delaware or the United States District Court for the District of Delaware) for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement or the Services."

If Seller insists on New York (perhaps due to its own counsel's preference), at minimum add a provision that claims arising under both the TSA and the SPA may be brought in a single action in the SPA's forum.

## Issue 10 – NDA Non-Solicitation May Restrict Post-Closing Workforce Decisions

**Where:** Mutual NDA § 4.2; § 8.1 (term: 2 years from February 9, 2026 = February 9, 2028).

**The problem.** Section 4.2 prohibits Daikesk from soliciting for employment "any officer or senior technical employee of the Company with whom Daikesk first had contact, or of whom Daikesk first became aware, in connection with the Transaction" for 12 months after the Effective Date (i.e., until February 9, 2027).

While this is narrower than a blanket non-solicit (it covers only employees Daikesk contacted or learned about in connection with the deal), it could still impede post-closing integration if Daikesk wants to move certain Company employees into other roles within the Daikesk organization or if it wants to backfill positions vacated by those employees.

**Why it matters.** The restriction runs until February 9, 2027, which may extend past the Closing Date. If Daikesk closes in, say, October 2026, it would own the Company but still be restricted from "soliciting" its own employees for ~4 months. In practice, this is likely a minor issue (Daikesk can direct its own employees' assignments), but the "solicitation" language could be read broadly if Daikesk makes an offer to a Company employee to move to a different Daikesk entity or role.

**Recommended fix.**

Add a carve-out: "This Section 4.2 shall not apply after the Closing Date, at which time Recipient becomes an Affiliate of Daikesk and its employees are no longer 'employees of the Company' within the meaning of this Section." Alternatively, seek a mutual release of § 4.2 effective at Closing.

This is a lower-priority item and can be addressed in a side letter or at the time of the Ferreol consent negotiation.

## Issue 11 – No Specific Performance Right for Ferreol Consent

**Where:** SPA § 11.10 (specific performance); Article VII (conditions).

**The problem.** Section 11.10 provides for specific performance of "any provision of this Agreement." However, if the Ferreol consent is not made a condition to closing (as it currently is not), Daikesk has no contractual right to compel Ferreol to consent. Specific performance runs only between the parties to the SPA (Daikesk, Glosiv, Sorrelux). Ferreol is not a party to the SPA.

**Why it matters.** If Daikesk adds the Ferreol consent as a condition to closing (per Issue 1) but Seller fails to obtain it, Daikesk can walk away (terminate under § 8.1(c)) but cannot force the consent. If Daikesk does NOT add it as a condition and simply closes, it has no remedy against Seller short of an indemnification claim for breach of the § 4.9 IP representation – which is capped at $18.65M and subject to the exclusive-remedy provision.

**Recommended fix.**

In addition to making the consent a condition to closing (Issue 1), add a specific covenant:

> "Seller shall use its best efforts to obtain Dr. Ferreol's written consent to the Change of Control in the form required by Section 7.2(g) no later than [15] Business Days before the anticipated Closing Date. Seller shall not take any action, or fail to take any action, that would reasonably be expected to prevent or delay such consent."

This gives Daikesk a pre-closing covenant claim (not subject to the post-closing exclusive remedy) if Seller acts in bad faith.

## Issue 12 – Working Capital Definition Excludes Cash (Confirm Alignment with LOI)

**Where:** SPA § 2.3(c) (definition of "Closing Working Capital"); LOI § 2(a).

**The problem.** "Closing Working Capital" is defined as "current assets of the Company (excluding cash and tax assets) less current liabilities of the Company (excluding indebtedness and tax liabilities)." The LOI states the Purchase Price assumes "the Company is delivered on a cash-free, debt-free basis." These are consistent – the exclusion of cash and debt from working capital effectively makes the deal cash-free/debt-free.

**Why it matters.** This is not a defect, but it is worth confirming with the client that the $14,200,000 Target Working Capital was negotiated on this basis. If the target was set including cash (as some practitioners do), the exclusion could shift the effective purchase price. Given the LOI's express "cash-free, debt-free" language, the current formulation appears correct. Flagging for client confirmation only.

**Recommended fix.** None required, but confirm with the client that the $14.2M target was modeled on a cash-excluded, debt-excluded basis.
