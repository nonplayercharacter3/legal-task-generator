## Summary

The current documents contain two deal-critical gaps that should be resolved before we go back to the other side: (1) the core patent license for the digital radiography business terminates automatically upon this transaction unless Dr. Ferreol consents, and no mechanism in the SPA secures that consent; and (2) the Transition Services Agreement omits the ultrasound and field-service transition support that the LOI expressly contemplated, and defines "Business" more narrowly than the SPA. Beyond those, there are several secondary issues around the TSA liability cap for data breaches, the TSA's one-sided assignment right, and the structural ease with which Dr. Ferreol can be lost post-closing.

## Issue 1 – Automatic Termination of the Detector Calibration Patent License on Change of Control

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.3 (last two sentences); SPA § 1.1 (definitions of "Change of Control" incorporated by reference through "Key Employee Agreement"), § 4.9, § 7.2(e), Schedule 4.5.

**The problem.** Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement grants the Company an exclusive, royalty-free license to the four Detector Calibration Patents (Exhibit A: U.S. Patents 9,846,213; 10,112,587; 10,559,042; and Application 16/904,318). The license "shall terminate automatically upon a Change of Control unless the Company has obtained the Employee's prior written consent to that Change of Control." The defined term "Change of Control" in § 1.1 of the Key Employee Agreement expressly includes "any sale of a majority of the Company's stock" – which is precisely this transaction.

The SPA does not list Dr. Ferreol's consent on Schedule 4.5 (which contains only the Meridian Health Purchasing Alliance consent), does not make her consent a closing condition in § 7.2, and does not require delivery of an amended or restated Key Employee Agreement at Closing (§ 3.2). The SPA's IP representation in § 4.9 states that the Company "has a valid and enforceable right to use" all Company Intellectual Property "necessary for the conduct of the Business as currently conducted," made as of both the signing date and the Closing Date. If the license terminates at Closing by its own terms, that representation will be false at Closing.

**Why it matters.** The Recital to the Key Employee Agreement states that Dr. Ferreol is "the sole owner and named inventor" of these patents and that she conceived them outside her employment. The Company does not own them; it has only a license. These patents cover pixel-level gain/offset calibration, adaptive defect-map correction, temperature-compensated dark-field calibration, and automated field recalibration – core enabling technology for flat-panel DR detectors. Without them, the Company likely cannot manufacture or service its principal product line. This is not a contingent risk; it is a self-executing termination that occurs at the moment the Shares change hands.

The indemnification remedy is also inadequate: § 4.9 is a general representation, not a Fundamental Representation (SPA § 1.1), so any claim is subject to the 10% cap ($18.65M) and the $1.4M tipping basket (SPA § 9.4(a)). A loss of the core patent license could impair the entire business value, far exceeding the cap.

**Recommended fix.**

(a) **Require Dr. Ferreol's written consent to the Change of Control as a condition to Closing.** Add to Schedule 4.5:

> "Consent of Dr. Tamsin Ferreol to the Change of Control contemplated by this Agreement, in form and substance reasonably acceptable to Buyer, pursuant to Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement."

And add to § 7.2(e): "the consents listed on Schedule 4.5, including the consent of the Key Employee pursuant to Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement, shall have been obtained."

(b) **Require delivery of a consent or amended agreement at Closing.** Add to § 3.2:

> "(g) a written consent of Dr. Tamsin Ferreol to the Change of Control pursuant to Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement, or an amended and restated employment agreement in form and substance reasonably acceptable to Buyer that removes the automatic termination provision."

(c) **Consider elevating the IP representation.** If the patent license is confirmed (via consent or amendment), the risk is lower. But if for any reason the consent is not obtained pre-signing, we should push to make § 4.9 a Fundamental Representation or at minimum carve it out of the cap in § 9.4(b). Proposed addition to § 9.4(b):

> "The limitations in Section 9.4(a) do not apply to Losses arising out of Fraud, any breach of the Fundamental Representations, any breach of Section 6.7 (Confidentiality), or any breach of Section 4.9 (Intellectual Property) to the extent arising from the termination or impairment of the license under Section 9.3 of the Key Employee Agreement."

(d) **Negotiate a direct agreement with Dr. Ferreol.** Ideally, before or at signing, obtain a tripartite side letter or amendment in which Dr. Ferreol consents to the Change of Control and agrees that the license will survive, potentially with modified terms (e.g., removing the sublicense restriction discussed in Issue 7 below). This is the cleanest solution and should be the primary ask.

**Question for the client:** What is the deal team's understanding of Dr. Ferreol's posture? Has she been approached informally? If she is unwilling to consent, does the client still want to proceed, and at what price adjustment? This may effectively be a condition to the deal's viability.

## Issue 2 – Transition Services Agreement Omits Ultrasound and Field-Service Support Contemplated by the LOI

**Where:** LOI § 5(b) and (c); TSA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); TSA Schedule A; SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Business").

**The problem.** The LOI expressly contemplated that the TSA would cover:

- (b) "support for the Company's field-service operations, including dispatch, service-ticketing systems, spare-parts logistics and access to installed-base service records"; and
- (c) "support for the Company's ultrasound operations, including shared transducer sourcing, regulatory and complaint-handling systems, and distributor order processing."

The executed-form TSA does not include either category. Schedule A lists only: (1) IT/ERP, (2) Data Migration, (3) Detector Supply Chain, (4) Procurement, (5) Payroll/Benefits, and (6) Finance/Accounting.

Compounding this, the TSA defines "Business" in § 1.1 as "the manufacture of digital radiography detectors as conducted by Recipient as of the Closing Date" – which excludes ultrasound imaging systems entirely and excludes the design, sale, and servicing activities covered by the SPA's definition of "Business" ("the design, manufacture, sale and servicing of digital radiography and ultrasound imaging systems").

**Why it matters.** The LOI states that Buyer is acquiring the Company "as a going concern" including "the Company's field-service organization and installed-base service contracts" (LOI § 1). If the field-service infrastructure (dispatch, ticketing, spare parts, installed-base records) was running on Seller's systems pre-closing and is not covered by the TSA, the Company may be unable to service its installed base post-closing – triggering customer contract breaches, revenue loss, and potential termination rights under service agreements. Similarly, if the ultrasound business relies on shared transducer sourcing or regulatory complaint-handling systems operated by Seller, those operations could be disrupted on Day 1.

The TSA's "Omitted Services" mechanism (§ 2.3) allows Recipient to identify gaps within 90 days post-closing, but it only creates an obligation to "negotiate in good faith" – Provider is not required to agree. By then, the operational disruption has already occurred.

**Recommended fix.**

(a) **Expand Schedule A** to add at minimum:

| No. | Service | Description | Fees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Field Service Operations | Dispatch and service-ticketing platform access; spare-parts logistics and warehouse management; installed-base service records and device performance data access; field-service technician scheduling. | Fully loaded cost, invoiced monthly |
| 8 | Ultrasound Operations | Shared transducer sourcing and purchase-order processing; regulatory complaint-handling system access; distributor order processing for ultrasound product lines. | Fully loaded cost, invoiced monthly |

(b) **Conform the "Business" definition** in TSA § 1.1 to the SPA:

> "Business" means the design, manufacture, sale and servicing of digital radiography and ultrasound imaging systems as conducted by Recipient as of the Closing Date.

(c) **Tighten § 2.3 (Omitted Services).** Change "The Parties shall negotiate in good faith to add the Omitted Service to Schedule A" to:

> "Provider shall add the Omitted Service to Schedule A and perform it during the Term at the Fees determined under Section 5.1, unless Provider demonstrates that performing the service would require a material capital expenditure not previously incurred for Recipient's benefit."

This shifts the default from "we'll talk" to "you'll do it unless you have a specific, justified reason not to."

**Question for the client:** Does Daikesk have its own field-service and ultrasound infrastructure that could absorb these functions immediately at Closing, making the TSA gap less critical? If not, the 12-month TSA term is the only bridge, and the missing services are an operational risk on Day 1.

## Issue 3 – TSA Caps Data-Breach Liability at Six Months of Fees, Overriding the SPA

**Where:** TSA § 10.2 (second sentence); SPA § 9.2, § 9.4; TSA § 7.3.

**The problem.** TSA § 10.2 provides:

> "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in the Purchase Agreement, any claim arising from the use or disclosure by Provider or its Affiliates of Company Data or Confidential Information in connection with the Services is subject to, and limited by, this Section 10.2."

Section 10.2 caps Provider's aggregate liability at "the Fees paid by Recipient under this Agreement in the six months preceding the event giving rise to the claim." Company Data is defined to include "protected health information processed by or for Recipient" (TSA § 1.1). The Service Standard requires HIPAA compliance (TSA § 2.2), and a Business Associate Agreement is contemplated (TSA § 7.5).

**Why it matters.** If a data breach occurs while Seller is hosting the Company's ERP, processing payroll, or managing field-service records containing PHI, the potential exposure includes HIPAA civil penalties (up to $2.1M per violation category per year), state AG enforcement, class-action litigation, and notification costs. Six months of transition services fees – likely in the low seven figures at most – is a fraction of that exposure. The "notwithstanding" language expressly overrides the SPA's indemnification, which would otherwise provide a 10% cap ($18.65M) and, for confidentiality breaches under SPA § 6.7, a full-Purchase-Price cap.

**Recommended fix.**

Delete the second sentence of TSA § 10.2 in its entirety. Replace with:

> "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Provider's liability for (a) breach of Section 7 (Confidentiality and Data Protection), (b) gross negligence or willful misconduct, or (c) violation of applicable data protection or privacy laws (including HIPAA) shall not be subject to the cap in this Section 10.2 and shall be governed by the indemnification provisions of the Purchase Agreement."

Alternatively, if Seller resists, raise the TSA cap for data-related claims to a stated dollar amount (e.g., $5M) or to the full SPA cap.

## Issue 4 – TSA Allows Provider to Assign or Delegate to Any Person Without Recipient's Consent

**Where:** TSA § 12.4.

**The problem.** Section 12.4 provides:

> "Recipient may not assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of Provider. Provider may assign this Agreement, or delegate the performance of any of the Services, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient."

**Why it matters.** Provider (Glosiv) could delegate IT hosting, payroll, or data-migration services to a subcontractor – potentially a competitor of Daikesk or an entity with weaker security controls – without any approval right for Daikesk or the Company. Given that the Services involve access to Company Data (including PHI) and Company Intellectual Property, this is a significant security and competitive risk. The one-sidedness is also commercially unusual; standard TSAs require at least notice and a right to object on reasonable grounds.

**Recommended fix.**

> "Recipient may not assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of Provider, not to be unreasonably withheld. Provider may not assign this Agreement without the prior written consent of Recipient. Provider may delegate the performance of any Service to a subcontractor upon at least 15 days' prior written notice to Recipient, provided that (a) the subcontractor is bound by confidentiality and data-protection obligations no less protective than those in this Agreement, (b) Provider remains fully responsible for the subcontractor's performance, and (c) Recipient may object on reasonable grounds related to data security, regulatory compliance, or competitive conflict, in which case the Parties shall negotiate in good faith an alternative arrangement."

## Issue 5 – Key Employee Agreement Structure Makes Dr. Ferreol Easy to Lose

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement §§ 3.1–3.2, 6.2, 6.3, 7.1–7.2; SPA § 1.1 ("Key Employee").

**The problem.** The Initial Term (3 years from March 15, 2021) expired on March 15, 2024. The agreement is now in successive 1-year auto-renewal periods. Either party can prevent a renewal with 90 days' notice (§ 3.2). The Company can terminate without Cause on 30 days' notice (§ 6.2). Dr. Ferreol can resign for Good Reason on effectively 30 days (60-day notice + 30-day cure period, § 1.1 definition of "Good Reason" and § 6.3).

"Good Reason" includes "a material diminution in her title, authority or duties." Post-closing integration – even well-intentioned – could trigger this. If a Good Reason termination occurs within 12 months after the Change of Control, § 7.2 enhances severance to 18 months' salary ($517,500), a 1.5× target bonus lump sum (~$207,000), and full equity vesting.

**Why it matters.** The SPA identifies Dr. Ferreol as the sole "Key Employee." The deal thesis presumably depends on her continued leadership of R&D and her maintenance of the patent license (see Issue 1). But the current agreement gives her a low-friction exit with enhanced economics if the post-closing environment changes in any way she characterizes as a "material diminution." There is no retention bonus, no extended term, and no "double-trigger" structure that would require both a CoC AND an adverse change to her role before enhanced severance kicks in (though § 7.2 is technically double-trigger, the "Good Reason" definition is broad enough to be triggered by ordinary integration).

**Recommended fix.**

Negotiate an amendment to the Key Employee Agreement as a condition to Closing (tied to Issue 1). The amendment should:

(a) Extend the term through at least 24–36 months post-Closing, with auto-renewal;
(b) Add a retention bonus (e.g., $250,000–$500,000, payable 50% at Closing and 50% at 12 months post-Closing, forfeited on voluntary resignation without Good Reason);
(c) Narrow "Good Reason" to exclude changes that do not reduce Base Salary by more than 10% or that are part of a bona fide post-closing integration plan agreed in advance;
(d) Confirm the patent license survives the Change of Control (addressing Issue 1);
(e) Potentially extend the non-compete in § 10.1 from 12 months to 24 months post-termination, given the heightened risk of her departure.

**Question for the client:** What is the client's post-closing plan for Dr. Ferreol's role? Will she report to a Daikesk CEO, or will the Company's CEO role be eliminated? Any change in reporting line or scope could be argued to constitute a "material diminution in title, authority or duties." We need to understand the integration plan to assess Good Reason risk.

## Issue 6 – IP Representation Is Not a Fundamental Representation; Inadequate Remedy for Core IP Risk

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Fundamental Representations"), § 4.9, § 9.4(a)–(b).

**The problem.** "Fundamental Representations" are limited to § 4.1 (Organization), § 4.2 (Authority), § 4.3 (Capitalization/Title), and § 4.14 (Brokers). The IP representation (§ 4.9) is a general rep, subject to the 10% cap ($18.65M) and the $1.4M tipping basket. For a company whose value is substantially derived from its proprietary detector calibration technology (licensed, not owned), a 10% cap is thin protection.

**Why it matters.** If the patent license is validly consented to (resolving Issue 1) but later found to be defective – e.g., Dr. Ferreol's prior employer claims an assignment, a patent is invalidated, or a co-inventor's rights are asserted – the buyer's recovery is capped at $18.65M against a potential loss that could impair the entire DR product line.

**Recommended fix.**

Add § 4.9 to the definition of Fundamental Representations, or at minimum add it to the carve-out in § 9.4(b):

> "The limitations in Section 9.4(a) do not apply to Losses arising out of Fraud, any breach of the Fundamental Representations, any breach of Section 6.7 (Confidentiality), or any breach of Section 4.9 (Intellectual Property)."

If Seller resists making all of § 4.9 fundamental, compromise by carving out only losses arising from defects in title to or enforceability of the Detector Calibration Patents or the license thereunder.

## Issue 7 – Sublicense Restriction in Patent License May Constrain Post-Closing Integration

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.3 (third sentence); SPA definition of "Company Intellectual Property" (§ 1.1).

**The problem.** The license permits the Company to use the Detector Calibration Patents to "make, have made, use, sell, offer for sale, import and service Company Products." Sublicensing is permitted only "to its contract manufacturers and service subcontractors acting on the Company's behalf." "Company Products" is defined as products "that the Company designs, manufactures, sells or services from time to time."

**Why it matters.** If Daikesk's post-closing strategy involves (a) having Daikesk's own manufacturing facilities produce Sorrelux detectors, (b) integrating Sorrelux's calibration technology into Daikesk's broader product line, or (c) using Daikesk's service organization to service Sorrelux installed base, any of these could fall outside the license. A Daikesk affiliate that is not a "contract manufacturer acting on the Company's behalf" would not be covered. Using the technology in a Daikesk-branded product that is not a "Company Product" would not be covered.

**Recommended fix.**

In the amendment negotiated under Issue 1, expand the license to:

> "The Company may sublicense these rights to its Affiliates and their respective contract manufacturers, service providers and subcontractors acting on behalf of the Company or its Affiliates. For purposes of this Section, 'Company Products' includes any product or service that incorporates or utilizes the Detector Calibration Patents and is designed, manufactured, sold or serviced by the Company or its Affiliates."

**Question for the client:** What is the strategic intent for the acquired technology? Is Daikesk buying Sorrelux as a standalone business, or does it plan to integrate the detector calibration IP into its own product portfolio? The answer determines how aggressively we need to push on the sublicense and "Company Products" definitions.

## Issue 8 – TSA Governing Law and Forum Differ from SPA

**Where:** TSA § 13.2 (New York law; Manhattan forum); SPA § 11.8 (Delaware law), § 11.9 (Delaware Chancery/Superior Court forum).

**The problem.** The TSA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan state or federal courts. The SPA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery. The SPA's order of precedence (§ 11.12) gives the Purchase Agreement priority in case of conflict, but does not harmonize the governing law or forum provisions.

**Why it matters.** A dispute involving both the TSA and the SPA (e.g., whether a service failure constitutes a breach of the SPA's conduct-of-business covenant or a TSA service-level failure) could raise threshold jurisdictional and choice-of-law questions. Litigating in two forums increases cost and creates risk of inconsistent rulings.

**Recommended fix.**

Conform TSA § 13.2 to the SPA:

> "This Agreement and all claims arising out of or relating to it are governed by the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to conflict-of-laws principles. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Court of Chancery of the State of Delaware (or, if that court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, the Superior Court of the State of Delaware or the United States District Court for the District of Delaware) for any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement or the Services."

This is a low-friction ask and should be non-controversial.

## Issue 9 – 18-Month Survival Period for General Representations May Be Inadequate for FDA/Regulatory Exposure

**Where:** SPA § 9.1.

**The problem.** General representations (including § 4.10 Regulatory Compliance and § 4.11 Data Privacy) survive only 18 months post-Closing. FDA enforcement actions, warning letters, and quality-system audits can take well over 18 months to surface, particularly if the issue relates to pre-Closing manufacturing or design controls.

**Why it matters.** If the Company received a Form 483 observation or is under an FDA audit cycle that concludes 20 months post-Closing and reveals a pre-Closing quality-system deficiency, the representation in § 4.10 would have expired and Daikesk would have no indemnification claim (absent Fraud).

**Recommended fix.**

Carve out regulatory and data-privacy reps from the 18-month general survival period:

> "The representations and warranties in this Agreement survive the Closing for 18 months, except that (a) the Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, (b) the representations in Section 4.10 (Regulatory Compliance) and Section 4.11 (Data Privacy) survive until the earlier of (i) 24 months after the Closing Date or (ii) 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, and (c) covenants survive until fully performed."

Alternatively, extend all general rep survival to 24 months.

## Issue 10 – No Interim Financials Required Between Signing and Closing

**Where:** SPA § 4.6; § 2.3(a).

**The problem.** The most recent financial statements are as of June 30, 2026 (unaudited). The SPA is signed August 14, 2026. The outside date is December 31, 2026 (§ 8.1(b)). If Closing occurs in November or December, there will be a 5- to 6-month gap between the last delivered financials and Closing. The working capital adjustment (§ 2.3) addresses balance-sheet items at Closing but does not provide visibility into income-statement deterioration (e.g., loss of a major customer, unexpected warranty claims, regulatory fine).

**Why it matters.** The "no Material Adverse Effect" closing condition (§ 7.2(d)) is the primary protection, but MAE is a high bar under Delaware law. A meaningful revenue decline or unexpected expense that doesn't rise to the level of an MAE would not give Daikesk a walk-right, and the working capital adjustment would not capture it.

**Recommended fix.**

Add to § 6.1 (Conduct of Business) or as a new section:

> "Seller shall deliver to Buyer, within 30 days after the end of each calendar quarter following the date of this Agreement and prior to the Closing Date, unaudited balance sheets and statements of income and cash flows of the Company for the quarter then ended, prepared in accordance with GAAP consistently applied."

This gives Daikesk quarterly visibility and a factual basis to assess whether the MAE condition or the bring-down of representations is being met.

## Issue 11 – SPA Non-Compete Does Not Cover Dr. Ferreol Personally

**Where:** SPA § 6.8; Key Employee Agreement § 10.1.

**The problem.** The SPA's non-compete (§ 6.8) binds Seller (Glosiv) and its Affiliates for five years in North America and Europe. Dr. Ferreol is not a party to the SPA and is not an Affiliate of Seller. Her personal non-compete (Key Employee Agreement § 10.1) runs only 12 months post-employment and covers only "digital radiography or ultrasound imaging systems competitive with Company Products."

**Why it matters.** If Dr. Ferreol departs (for any reason) within the first 12 months post-closing, she is free to join a competitor after that period. The SPA's 5-year non-compete against Glosiv does not restrict her personally. Given that she is the sole inventor of the core patents and the CTO, her departure to a competitor would be a significant competitive threat.

**Recommended fix.**

In the Key Employee Agreement amendment (see Issue 5), extend the non-compete in § 10.1 to 24 months post-termination. Additionally, consider requiring Dr. Ferreol to execute a separate non-compete and non-solicitation agreement in favor of Daikesk (as buyer/successor) with a 3-year term, as a condition to Closing. This is a stronger ask and may require additional consideration (the retention bonus in Issue 5 could serve that purpose).
