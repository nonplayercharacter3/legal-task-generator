## ISSUE 1: Assumption of 10-Year Warranty Obligations and Halvorsen Class Action

**Where:** APA § 2.3(c); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.3(c) §§ 4.1–4.2. Compare Prior Draft APA § 2.4(e)–(f).

**Why it matters:** This is the single largest exposure in the current paper. The prior draft (August 21, 2026) contained two express exclusions in § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities) that are absent from the executed APA:

- Prior Draft § 2.4(e): *"any Liability arising out of any solar photovoltaic system that Seller installed, or any service Seller performed, before the Closing, including all workmanship, roof-penetration and other warranty obligations for those systems"*
- Prior Draft § 2.4(f): *"any Liability arising out of any action, suit, claim or proceeding that was pending or threatened against Seller as of the Closing, including each matter set forth on Schedule 4.9"*

The current APA § 2.4 omits both. In their place, current § 2.3(c) provides that Buyer assumes "Liabilities set forth on Schedule 2.3(c)," and the Disclosure Schedule affirmatively lists:

- **§ 4.1:** All workmanship and roof-penetration warranty obligations for approximately **4,100 residential systems** (each carrying a 10-year warranty per Schedule 4.10, Forms TS-W1 and TS-W2), plus small-commercial systems.
- **§ 4.2:** All liabilities from *Halvorsen, et al. v. Tredeil Sunworks, Inc.*, Case No. CV2025-017346 — a putative class action by Arizona homeowners alleging roof leaks, property damage, breach of warranty, negligence, and Arizona Consumer Fraud Act violations. Class certification is pending.

Because these are **Assumed Liabilities**, Buyer is directly and primarily liable to the customers and to the Halvorsen plaintiffs. Seller's indemnification obligation under § 8.2 does not extend to Assumed Liabilities (see § 8.5(b), which carves Assumed Liabilities out of the cap only for *Buyer's* indemnification obligation to Seller under § 8.3(c)). There is no backstop. The 18-month survival period in § 8.1 is irrelevant — these are not representation breaches; they are operational liabilities Buyer has contractually taken on.

The Halvorsen case alone could expose Buyer to eight-figure liability (class-wide roof damage, Consumer Fraud Act treble damages, attorneys' fees). The warranty book represents a decade of potential repair claims on 4,100+ roofs.

**Recommended fix:**

This requires a judgment call. If the client is willing to accept these liabilities (perhaps as the price of the deal), we need:

1. **A specific indemnity from Seller** for the Halvorsen matter and for pre-Closing warranty claims, outside the cap and basket. Proposed language:

> **Section 8.2(d) (new):** Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in this Article VIII, Seller shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless the Buyer Indemnitees from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to (i) any claim, demand, action or proceeding by any customer or third party alleging breach of any workmanship, roof-penetration or other warranty provided by Seller for a system installed before the Closing Date, or (ii) the matter described in Schedule 4.9, Section 8.1 (*Halvorsen, et al. v. Tredeil Sunworks, Inc.*), in each case whether or not such Losses would otherwise constitute an Assumed Liability. This indemnity shall not be subject to the Cap, the Deductible, or the survival period in Section 8.1.

2. **A purchase price reduction or escrow** to fund the assumed warranty and litigation exposure. Given 4,100 systems with 10-year warranties and a pending class action, I would recommend the client request a dedicated escrow (e.g., $3–5 million) to be released only upon final resolution of Halvorsen and a defined warranty reserve.

3. **If the client will not accept these liabilities**, we need to reopen § 2.3(c) and Schedule 2.3(c) to restore the prior draft's exclusions. This would likely require a purchase price adjustment.

**Flag for partner:** This appears to be a negotiated change from the prior draft. I need to confirm with the client whether this was a conscious business decision or whether the client understood the full scope. If it was not intentional, we have leverage to reopen before Closing (October 1, 2026).

## ISSUE 2: Key Employee Agreement Assignment Is Void Without Okafor-Lindqvist's Consent

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.2; APA § 2.1(c)(iii); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6 § 6.1.

**Why it matters:** APA § 2.1(c)(iii) includes the Key Employee Agreement as an "Assigned Contract" to be transferred to Buyer at Closing. However, Key Employee Agreement § 9.2 provides:

> "The Company may not assign this Agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under this Agreement, without the prior written consent of Employee. This restriction applies to any assignment by operation of law or otherwise, **including an assignment in connection with a merger, consolidation, or sale of all or substantially all of the assets of the Company**. Any purported assignment without that consent is **void**."

This is an unambiguous anti-assignment clause that specifically contemplates and prohibits the very transaction we are effecting. Without Okafor-Lindqvist's prior written consent, the assignment of the Key Employee Agreement to Buyer is void ab initio.

Compounding the problem, Disclosure Schedule § 6.1 (Required Consents – Assigned Contracts) states: **"None."** The Key Employee Agreement's consent requirement is not disclosed, and therefore the closing condition in APA § 7.2(a) (all consents on Schedule 4.6 obtained) is effectively meaningless with respect to this contract. Seller's representation in § 4.6 (that Schedule 4.6 sets forth *each* consent required) is inaccurate.

If the assignment is void, Buyer does not acquire:
- The employment relationship (title, duties, reporting structure);
- The qualifying party obligation (§ 1.3);
- The non-competition covenant (§ 6.1);
- The non-solicitation covenants (§§ 6.2–6.3);
- The confidentiality obligations (§ 7.1);
- The work product assignment (§ 7.3);
- The injunctive relief remedy (§ 8.1).

**Recommended fix:**

1. **Obtain Okafor-Lindqvist's written consent** to the assignment before Closing. This should be a standalone consent letter or a tripartite amendment to the Key Employee Agreement. Proposed consent language:

> I, Dana Okafor-Lindqvist, hereby consent to the assignment of the Key Employee Agreement dated March 1, 2024 between me and Tredeil Sunworks, Inc. to Roumoux Solar Holdings, LLC, effective as of the Closing under the Asset Purchase Agreement dated September 14, 2026. Upon such assignment, Roumoux Solar Holdings, LLC shall succeed to all rights and obligations of Tredeil Sunworks, Inc. under the Agreement, and I agree that the reference to "Company" in the Agreement shall thereafter mean Roumoux Solar Holdings, LLC. I further agree that the assignment does not constitute a material diminution in title, authority or duties for purposes of Section 4.4 of the Agreement.

2. **Amend Schedule 4.6** to list the Key Employee Agreement consent as a Required Consent, so that § 7.2(a) operates as a genuine closing condition.

3. **Add a specific representation** in the APA that Seller has obtained all consents necessary for the assignment of each Assigned Contract, including the Key Employee Agreement.

**Flag for partner / client:** We need to confirm whether Okafor-Lindqvist has been approached about this consent. If she is unwilling to consent, the entire structure of the deal (which appears to depend on her as qualifying party and operations leader) is at risk. This is a deal-critical item.

## ISSUE 3: Contractor License Gap – All Licenses Tied to Okafor-Lindqvist

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8; APA § 6.4; Key Employee Agreement § 1.3.

**Why it matters:** All four contractor licenses (CR-11 Residential, C-11 Commercial, CR-77 Solar Residential, C-77 Solar Commercial) list Dana Okafor-Lindqvist as the sole qualifying party. These licenses expire June 30, 2027 — less than nine months after the anticipated Closing.

APA § 6.4 requires Seller to cooperate for 180 days post-Closing in Buyer's application for contractor licenses, "including by making available the qualifying party listed on Schedule 4.8." However:

- If the Key Employee Agreement assignment is void (Issue 2), Okafor-Lindqvist has no contractual obligation to Buyer. Her § 1.3 duties run to "the Company" (Seller).
- Her § 1.3(c) prohibits her from serving as qualifying party for any other Person **without the Company's prior written consent**. If "the Company" remains Seller, she needs *Seller's* consent to serve as qualifying party for *Buyer*.
- The 180-day cooperation period in § 6.4 is a covenant of Seller, but Seller's ability to compel Okafor-Lindqvist to "be made available" to Buyer depends on her continuing employment with Seller and her contractual obligations to Seller.
- If Okafor-Lindqvist accepts an offer from Buyer (per APA § 6.5, Seller must terminate her effective at Closing), she is no longer employed by Seller, and her § 1.3 obligations to Seller end. She would then need to voluntarily agree to serve as qualifying party for Buyer — which is only enforceable if the Key Employee Agreement has been successfully assigned to Buyer.

This creates a circular dependency: Buyer needs Okafor-Lindqvist as qualifying party → which requires the Key Employee Agreement to be assigned → which requires her consent → which we don't have.

**Recommended fix:**

1. **Make the contractor license transition a closing condition.** Add to § 7.2:

> (f) Buyer shall have received written confirmation from the Arizona Registrar of Contractors that Buyer's applications for the licenses listed on Schedule 4.8 have been filed and are pending, or that the licenses will be transferred to Buyer upon the Closing.

2. **Extend the cooperation period** in § 6.4 from 180 days to at least 365 days, and add a specific obligation:

> Seller shall use its best efforts to obtain Okafor-Lindqvist's written consent to serve as qualifying party for Buyer's license applications, and shall not take any action that would impair her willingness or ability to do so.

3. **Require Okafor-Lindqvist to execute a direct agreement with Buyer** (separate from or in addition to the assigned Key Employee Agreement) confirming her willingness to serve as qualifying party for Buyer for a specified period (e.g., 24 months), with appropriate compensation.

4. **Identify a backup qualifying party.** The client should begin searching for an alternative qualifying party immediately, in case Okafor-Lindqvist is unavailable.

**Flag for client:** The client needs to confirm whether it has identified or can identify an alternative qualifying party who meets the Arizona Registrar of Contractors' experience and examination requirements. This is a business question, not a legal one, but it drives the legal structure.

## ISSUE 4: No Non-Compete Protection from Okafor-Lindqvist

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 6.1; APA § 2.1(c)(iii).

**Why it matters:** The 24-month non-compete in Key Employee Agreement § 6.1 runs in favor of "the Company." If the assignment fails (Issue 2), "the Company" remains Seller. Buyer acquires no non-compete, non-solicitation, or confidentiality protection from the single most important person in the Business — the VP of Installation Operations who knows every crew, every site, every customer relationship, and every installation method.

Okafor-Lindqvist could, upon the failure of the assignment:
- Walk away from the transaction entirely (she is not a party to the APA);
- Join a competitor in Arizona within 24 months of her separation from Seller (the non-compete binds her only to Seller's benefit);
- Take customer relationships and proprietary installation knowledge with her.

Even if the assignment succeeds, the non-compete only runs for 24 months post-termination. Given that the APA's non-compete against Seller runs for 3 years (§ 6.7), there is a one-year gap during which Okafor-Lindqvist could compete while Seller is still restricted.

**Recommended fix:**

1. **If the assignment succeeds:** Negotiate an extension of the non-compete to 36 months to align with Seller's non-compete, or add a separate non-compete from Okafor-Lindqvist in favor of Buyer with a 36-month term.

2. **If the assignment fails:** Require Okafor-Lindqvist to enter a direct employment or consulting agreement with Buyer that includes non-compete, non-solicitation, and confidentiality covenants in favor of Buyer. Make this a condition to Closing.

3. **In all cases:** Ensure the Key Employee Agreement (or replacement) includes a provision that the restrictive covenants are for the benefit of the Company and its successors and assigns, to the extent permitted by Arizona law.

**Flag for partner:** Arizona law (A.R.S. § 23-152) imposes limitations on non-compete enforceability in the employment context. The 24-month duration and statewide geographic scope in § 6.1 may be at the outer limit of what Arizona courts will enforce. If we extend to 36 months, we increase the risk of unenforceability. The blue-pencil provision in § 6.4(c) helps, but a court could still strike the covenant entirely. I recommend we get a separate Arizona employment law opinion on the enforceability of the proposed covenants before finalizing.

## ISSUE 5: Expanded Earnout Information Rights Combined with Seller's Unrestricted Assignment Right

**Where:** APA § 2.6(d), § 10.6. Compare Prior Draft § 2.6(d), § 10.6.

**Why it matters:** Two changes work together to create a significant information-security risk:

**Change 1 – Information Rights (§ 2.6(d)):**

| Feature | Prior Draft | Current APA |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Recipient | Seller only | "Seller **or its permitted assignee**" |
| Quarterly reports | Installation volumes and PTO dates | Same |
| Access to records | "Books and records relating to Installed Megawatts," "only to the extent reasonably necessary" | "**Customer pipeline reports, pricing and proposal data**, and books and records" |
| Confidentiality | "Seller shall treat them as confidential information of Buyer under Section 6.6" | *Deleted* |
| Express exclusion | "Buyer is **not required to disclose** customer pipeline, pricing or proposal data" | *Deleted* |

**Change 2 – Assignment Right (§ 10.6):**

- Prior Draft: Seller could not assign without Buyer's consent. Only Buyer had a limited assignment right (to Affiliates or lenders).
- Current APA: "**Seller may assign any of its rights under this Agreement, including the right to receive Earnout Payments and to make claims under Article VIII, to any Person without the consent of Buyer.**"

**Combined effect:** Seller can sell or assign its earnout rights (and its indemnification claim rights) to any third party — a competitor, a hedge fund, a data broker — and that third party becomes a "permitted assignee" with the right to demand Buyer's customer pipeline, pricing, and proposal data every quarter for two years, with no confidentiality obligation (the § 6.6 reference was deleted) and no "reasonably necessary" limitation.

For a solar company, pricing and proposal data is highly sensitive. A competitor gaining access to Roumoux's pipeline and pricing would have a significant competitive advantage.

**Recommended fix:**

1. **Restore the prior draft's limitations on § 2.6(d).** Proposed language:

> (d) *Information Rights.* During each Earnout Period and until the final determination of the Earnout Payment for the Second Earnout Period, Buyer shall provide Seller, within twenty (20) days after the end of each calendar quarter, reports of installation volumes by system and permission-to-operate date. Following delivery of an Earnout Statement, Buyer shall afford Seller (or the independent accounting firm referred to in Section 2.6(c), or Seller's independent accountants subject to a customary confidentiality undertaking) reasonable access during normal business hours to Buyer's books and records relating to Installed Megawatts, in each case solely to the extent reasonably necessary to verify the Earnout Statement. Buyer is not required to disclose customer pipeline reports, pricing or proposal data. All information provided under this Section 2.6(d) shall be treated as confidential information of Buyer, and Seller shall comply with Section 6.6 with respect thereto.

2. **Restrict Seller's assignment right in § 10.6.** Proposed language:

> Seller may assign its right to receive Earnout Payments to one or more of its Affiliates or to a financial institution as collateral security, in each case without Buyer's consent. Seller may not assign any other right under this Agreement, including information rights under Section 2.6(d) or indemnification rights under Article VIII, without Buyer's prior written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld. Any permitted assignee of Earnout Payment rights shall be bound by the confidentiality and use limitations of Section 2.6(d) and Section 6.6.

3. **Restore the confidentiality reference in § 6.6.** Proposed language:

> 6.6 Confidentiality. From and after the Closing, Seller shall hold in confidence all confidential information concerning the Business and the Purchased Assets, **and all information Seller or its permitted assignees receive under Section 2.6(d)**, except to the extent such information becomes publicly available other than through Seller's breach of this Section 6.6.

**Flag for partner:** The Seller's assignment right to "any Person" is unusually broad and appears designed to allow Seller to monetize the earnout (e.g., by selling it at a discount to a factoring company). If the client is willing to accept that, we should at minimum require the assignee to be bound by confidentiality. If not, we should restrict the assignment as above.

## ISSUE 6: Missing Required Consents for Backlog and Supplier Contracts

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6 § 6.1 ("Assigned Contracts. None."); APA § 4.6, § 4.7.

**Why it matters:** Schedule 4.6 lists no consents required for any Assigned Contract. The Assigned Contracts include:

- The assignment may be ineffective, meaning Buyer does not actually acquire the contract;
- Seller's representation in § 4.7 (that each Assigned Contract is valid and in full force) may be technically true, but the representation in § 4.3(c) (that no consent is required except as set forth on Schedule 4.6) would be breached;

1. **Require Seller to re-run the consent analysis** for all Assigned Contracts and populate Schedule 4.6 accurately before Closing.

> To Seller's knowledge, no Assigned Contract contains a provision that prohibits or restricts assignment to Buyer without the counterparty's consent, except as set forth on Schedule 4.6.

3. **Make § 7.2(a) a meaningful condition** by ensuring Schedule 4.6 is complete. If consents are required and not obtained, Buyer should have the right to close without the affected contracts (treating them as Excluded Assets) or to terminate.

**Flag for client:** I need the client to confirm whether its deal team reviewed the anti-assignment provisions in the four supplier agreements and a sample of the Backlog Contracts during due diligence. ## ISSUE 7: Severance Exposure for Okafor-Lindqvist – Unclear Allocation

**Where:** APA § 6.5; Key Employee Agreement § 5.2; APA § 2.4(b).

**Why it matters:** APA § 6.5 provides that "Seller shall terminate the employment of each employee who accepts an offer from Buyer effective as of the Closing." If Okafor-Lindqvist accepts an offer from Buyer (which the deal structure strongly implies), Seller terminates her. Under Key Employee Agreement § 5.2, a termination without Cause entitles her to:

- 12 months of continued base salary ($185,000);
- 12 months of COBRA premium reimbursement;
- Plus the qualifying party stipend ($1,500/month) for the period she continues to serve under § 4.5.

Total approximate exposure: **~$203,000+** in severance, plus COBRA.

The question is: **who pays?**

- APA § 2.4(b) excludes from Assumed Liabilities "any Liability relating to the employment or termination of employment of any employee of Seller arising before the Closing." The termination occurs *at* the Closing, not before it. This is a close call.
- If the Key Employee Agreement is successfully assigned to Buyer, Buyer is "the Company" and owes the severance directly.
- If the assignment fails, Seller is "the Company" and owes the severance. But Seller is the one effecting the termination at Buyer's request (per § 6.5), creating an argument that Buyer should bear the cost.

**Recommended fix:**

1. **Clarify in the APA** that the severance obligations under the Key Employee Agreement (if assigned) are Assumed Liabilities of Buyer, or (if not assigned) are the sole responsibility of Seller. Proposed addition to § 2.3:

> (d) If the Key Employee Agreement is assigned to Buyer at Closing, all obligations of the Company thereunder arising on or after the Closing Date, including any severance payments under Section 5.2 thereof, shall be Assumed Liabilities.

2. **If the assignment fails:** Add to § 2.4:

> (f) Any Liability of Seller for severance, continuation of benefits, or other payments due to Dana Okafor-Lindqvist arising from the termination of her employment in connection with the transactions contemplated by this Agreement shall be an Excluded Liability.

3. **In all cases:** Confirm with the client whether Okafor-Lindqvist's offer letter from Buyer accounts for the severance (i.e., whether she will actually trigger § 5.2 or whether her new employment terms will supersede the old agreement).

**Flag for client:** Please confirm whether the client has made a formal offer to Okafor-Lindqvist and what the terms are. If her new role with Buyer is materially different (title, duties, compensation), the "Good Reason" definition in Key Employee Agreement § 4.4 could be triggered even if the assignment succeeds, giving her the right to resign and collect severance from Buyer.

## ISSUE 8: Bill of Sale Governing Law and Jurisdiction Split

**Where:** Bill of Sale §§ 6.1–6.2; APA §§ 10.10–10.11, 10.13.

**Why it matters:** The APA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery. The Bill of Sale is governed by Arizona law with exclusive jurisdiction in Maricopa County, Arizona. APA § 10.13 provides that the Bill of Sale "controls on matters concerning the transfer of title to the Purchased Assets."

This creates a bifurcated dispute-resolution framework:
- Title disputes → Arizona law, Arizona courts;
- All other APA disputes → Delaware law, Delaware courts;
- The Bill of Sale's power of attorney (§ 5.2), further assurances (§ 5.1), and post-Closing receipts provision (§ 5.3) are governed by Arizona law but are not clearly "matters concerning the transfer of title."

In practice, this could lead to forum-shopping, inconsistent rulings, and the need to litigate related claims in two jurisdictions.

**Recommended fix:**

This is lower priority and may be acceptable as-is (Arizona law for the Bill of Sale makes sense given the assets are in Arizona). However, I recommend:

1. **Add a clarifying sentence to Bill of Sale § 6.1:**

> For the avoidance of doubt, disputes arising under Section 5.1 (Further Assurances) or Section 5.2 (Power of Attorney) of this Bill of Sale shall be governed by Arizona law and subject to the jurisdiction set forth in Section 6.2.

2. **Alternatively, conform the Bill of Sale to Delaware law/jurisdiction** if the client prefers a single forum. This would require Seller's agreement.

**Flag for partner:** This is a lower-priority item. I would not raise it with the counterparty unless a dispute is anticipated. It is primarily a structural observation.

## ISSUE 9: No Representation on Employee Classification or Benefit Plan Liabilities

**Where:** APA Article IV (Seller's Reps); Disclosure Schedule (no corresponding schedule).

**Why it matters:** The APA contains no representation that Seller's employees (including Okafor-Lindqvist) are properly classified as employees versus independent contractors, or that Seller has complied with applicable wage-and-hour, payroll tax, or employee benefit laws. Given that:

- The Business has installation crews who may be classified as subcontractors (the non-solicit in Key Employee Agreement § 6.3 references "installation subcontractors");
- Buyer is assuming the Business and may inherit these workers;
- Arizona has specific requirements for contractor licensing that tie to the employment relationship;

there is potential exposure for misclassification penalties, unpaid wages, or tax liabilities that are not clearly allocated.

APA § 2.4(b) excludes "any Liability relating to the employment or termination of employment of any employee of Seller arising before the Closing" — but this only covers *employees*, not *subcontractors* who may have been misclassified.

**Recommended fix:**

1. **Add a representation in Article IV:**

> **4.13 Employment Matters.** To Seller's knowledge, all individuals who have performed services for the Business in the two (2) years preceding the Effective Date have been properly classified as employees or independent contractors in accordance with applicable law. Seller is not liable for any unpaid wages, overtime, payroll taxes, or employee benefit obligations with respect to any such individual, except as set forth on Schedule 4.13.

2. **Add a corresponding schedule** (even if "None") to force Seller to make the representation.

**Flag for client:** This is a moderate-priority item. I would include it in our next round of comments but would not make it a deal-breaker.
