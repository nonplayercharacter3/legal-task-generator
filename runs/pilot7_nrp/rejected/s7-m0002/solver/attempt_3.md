## Summary

The current paper creates several material exposures for Roumoux, the most serious of which are (1) the assignment of the Key Employee Agreement is void as a matter of its own terms, undermining the contractor-license transition, the non-compete protection, and the qualifying-party arrangement; and (2) Roumoux has assumed the Seller's pre-closing warranty obligations for approximately 4,100 installed systems and the *Halvorsen* putative class action—liabilities that the prior draft expressly excluded. Additional concerns include significantly expanded earnout information rights, Seller's unrestricted right to assign its deal rights to any person, and a confidentiality gap for the information Roumoux must share during the earnout period.

Issues are ordered by significance to the client.

## Issue 1 – Assignment of the Key Employee Agreement Is Void

### Where it appears

- **APA § 2.1(c)(iii)** (current): includes the Key Employee Agreement among the Assigned Contracts.
- **KEA § 9.2**: "The Company may not assign this Agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under this Agreement, without the prior written consent of Employee. This restriction applies to any assignment by operation of law or otherwise, including an assignment in connection with a merger, consolidation, or sale of all or substantially all of the assets of the Company. **Any purported assignment without that consent is void.**"
- **Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6, item 6.1**: lists "None" as Required Consents for Assigned Contracts.
- **APA § 4.6**: represents that Schedule 4.6 sets forth each consent required "including the assignment to Buyer of each Assigned Contract."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

The KEA is the contractual vehicle for three things Roumoux needs:

1. **Qualifying party for contractor licenses.** All four Arizona ROC licenses (Schedule 4.8) name Dana Okafor-Lindqvist as qualifying party. KEA § 1.3 obligates her to serve in that capacity and, critically, § 1.3(c) prohibits her from serving as qualifying party for any other person without the Company's consent. If the KEA is not validly assigned to Roumoux, she has no obligation to Roumoux and is contractually barred from serving as Roumoux's qualifying party without Tredeil's consent.

2. **Non-compete and non-solicit protection.** KEA §§ 6.1–6.3 impose 24-month post-termination restrictions on Okafor-Lindqvist. If the assignment is void, Roumoux holds no benefit of these covenants. She could immediately compete or solicit Roumoux's customers and employees.

3. **APA § 6.4 (Contractor License Transition)** requires Seller to cooperate for 180 days post-Closing in Roumoux's license application, "including by making available the qualifying party." But KEA § 4.5 only requires her to cooperate in designating a successor "through her final day of employment." If her employment terminates at Closing (per APA § 6.5), her KEA-based cooperation obligation ends on the Closing Date—leaving a gap for the remainder of the 180-day transition window.

The voidness is not curable by indemnification. Even if Roumoux recovered damages for Seller's breach of § 4.6 (the "None" disclosure), the cap is $2,760,000 (APA § 8.5(a)), and the loss of the non-compete, the qualifying-party arrangement, and the license transition is not readily quantifiable within that cap.

### Recommended fix

**Before Closing, obtain Okafor-Lindqvist's written consent to the assignment of the KEA to Roumoux.** This should be structured as a tripartite amendment and assignment agreement among Tredeil, Roumoux, and Okafor-Lindqvist, in which:

- Okafor-Lindqvist consents to the assignment of the KEA from Tredeil to Roumoux;
- Roumoux agrees to assume Tredeil's obligations under the KEA (compensation, benefits, etc.);
- Okafor-Lindqvist confirms she will serve as qualifying party for Roumoux's contractor license applications during the 180-day transition period under APA § 6.4; and
- The restrictive covenants in KEA § 6 are confirmed to run in favor of Roumoux as assignee.

**Proposed language for the consent (to be included in a side letter or tripartite agreement):**

> **Consent to Assignment.** Employee hereby consents to the assignment of this Agreement by the Company to Roumoux Solar Holdings, LLC ("Assignee") in connection with the Asset Purchase Agreement dated September 14, 2026 between the Company and Assignee. Effective as of the Closing Date under the Asset Purchase Agreement, Assignee shall succeed to all of the Company's rights and obligations under this Agreement, and Employee shall look to Assignee for performance thereof. Employee further agrees to serve as the qualifying party for Assignee's applications for contractor licenses from the Arizona Registrar of Contractors during the transition period described in Section 6.4 of the Asset Purchase Agreement, and to cooperate in good faith in the designation and qualification of a successor qualifying party thereafter.

**Additionally**, correct Schedule 4.6 to reflect that Okafor-Lindqvist's consent is a Required Consent, and make delivery of that consent a condition to Closing under APA § 7.2(a).

**Flag for partner / client:** We need to confirm whether the client intends to hire Okafor-Lindqvist. If so, we should negotiate a new employment agreement (or confirm the KEA terms will be honored) as a condition to Closing. If the client does not intend to hire her, the entire license-transition strategy needs to be rethought, and we should consider whether the deal is viable without a qualifying party in place.

## Issue 2 – Roumoux Has Assumed Pre-Closing Warranty Obligations and the *Halvorsen* Class Action

### Where it appears

- **APA § 2.3(c)** (current): includes as Assumed Liabilities those "set forth on Schedule 2.3(c)."
- **Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.3(c), items 4.1 and 4.2**:
  - 4.1: "All workmanship and roof-penetration warranty obligations of Seller for solar photovoltaic systems installed by Seller before the Closing. These obligations cover approximately 4,100 residential systems…each covered by Seller's standard 10-year workmanship warranty and 10-year roof-penetration warranty…together with the small-commercial systems recorded in the Warranty Book."
  - 4.2: "All Liabilities arising out of or relating to the matter described in Section 7.1 of Schedule 4.9 (*Halvorsen, et al. v. Tredeil Sunworks, Inc.*), a putative class action by homeowners alleging roof leaks and related property damage from roof penetrations made during installations by Seller."
- **Prior Draft APA § 2.4(e)** (deleted in current): "any Liability arising out of any solar photovoltaic system that Seller installed, or any service Seller performed, before the Closing, including all workmanship, roof-penetration and other warranty obligations for those systems."
- **Prior Draft APA § 2.4(f)** (deleted in current): "any Liability arising out of any action, suit, claim or proceeding that was pending or threatened against Seller as of the Closing, including each matter set forth on Schedule 4.9."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

The prior draft made these liabilities **Excluded**—Seller retained them and would have indemnified Roumoux under § 8.2(c) (uncapped under § 8.5(b)). The current draft flips this: they are now **Assumed** by Roumoux. The practical consequences:

1. **No indemnification from Seller.** Because these are Assumed Liabilities, they are not Excluded Liabilities. Seller's indemnification under § 8.2(c) does not cover them. If the *Halvorsen* class is certified and a judgment enters against Roumoux, Roumoux bears the full loss with no recourse against Seller.

2. **Unquantified exposure.** The *Halvorsen* action covers ~4,100 residential installations from January 2019 forward, alleging breach of warranty, negligence, and Arizona Consumer Fraud Act violations. Class certification is pending. A class judgment at even modest per-plaintiff damages could far exceed the $2,760,000 indemnification cap. The warranty obligations themselves (10-year workmanship and roof-penetration) represent a multi-year tail of potential service calls and repairs.

3. **The § 4.9 litigation representation is qualified by the disclosure.** Because *Halvorsen* is listed on Schedule 4.9, Seller's representation in § 4.9 is not breached by the existence of the lawsuit. Roumoux cannot claim indemnification for the litigation on the theory that Seller misrepresented its litigation status.

4. **Economic value of the deal is impaired.** The Base Purchase Price is $18,400,000. If Roumoux is assuming a class action that could result in seven- or eight-figure damages, plus ongoing warranty costs for thousands of systems, the effective purchase price is materially higher than stated.

### Recommended fix

**Option A (Preferred): Restore the prior draft's exclusion.** Move the warranty obligations and the *Halvorsen* litigation back to Excluded Liabilities. This requires:

- Deleting items 4.1 and 4.2 from Schedule 2.3(c);
- Adding to APA § 2.4 (Excluded Liabilities) the language from prior draft §§ 2.4(e) and (f):

> (e) any Liability arising out of any solar photovoltaic system that Seller installed, or any service Seller performed, before the Closing, including all workmanship, roof-penetration and other warranty obligations for those systems; and
> (f) any Liability arising out of any action, suit, claim or proceeding that was pending or threatened against Seller as of the Closing, including each matter set forth on Schedule 4.9.

This would make these liabilities subject to Seller's uncapped indemnification under § 8.2(c) / § 8.5(b).

**Option B (Fallback): If Seller insists Roumoux assume these liabilities, require a specific indemnity and/or price adjustment.**

- Add a new section (e.g., § 8.2(d)) providing that Seller shall indemnify Roumoux for all Losses arising from the *Halvorsen* action and pre-closing warranty obligations, **outside the Cap and Deductible** (i.e., add to § 8.5(b): "Losses arising out of the matters described in Schedule 2.3(c) shall not be subject to the Cap or the deductible").
- Require Seller to establish an escrow or holdback from the Base Purchase Price sufficient to cover the estimated *Halvorsen* exposure (we would need the client's and outside litigation counsel's assessment of that exposure).
- Adjust the Base Purchase Price downward to reflect the assumed liabilities.

**Flag for partner / client:** This appears to be a significant economic concession from the prior draft. We need to understand why this change was made—whether it was a negotiating trade for something else (e.g., the earnout terms, the non-compete duration) or whether it was an error. If it was a deliberate trade, the client needs to confirm it was made with full understanding of the *Halvorsen* exposure. I recommend we get a litigation risk assessment from the client's inside counsel or outside litigation counsel before we respond to the other side.

## Issue 3 – Earnout Information Rights Require Disclosure of Competitive Data

### Where it appears

- **APA § 2.6(d)** (current): "Buyer shall provide Seller **or its permitted assignee**, within twenty (20) days after the end of each calendar quarter, reports of installation volumes by system and permission-to-operate date, and shall afford Seller or its permitted assignee reasonable access during normal business hours to **Buyer's customer pipeline reports, pricing and proposal data**, and books and records relating to Installed Megawatts, in each case to permit verification of Installed Megawatts."
- **Prior Draft § 2.6(d)**: limited reports to "installation volumes by system and permission-to-operate date"; gave access only to "the accounting firm referred to in Section 2.6(c), or Seller's independent accountants subject to a customary confidentiality undertaking"; and expressly stated: "**Buyer is not required to disclose customer pipeline, pricing or proposal data.**"
- **APA § 10.6** (current): "Seller may assign any of its rights under this Agreement, **including the right to receive Earnout Payments**, to any Person without the consent of Buyer."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

Roumoux is required to give Seller (or any person to whom Seller assigns the earnout) quarterly access to:
- Customer pipeline reports (i.e., prospective deals not yet closed);
- Pricing data (i.e., what Roumoux charges customers); and
- Proposal data (i.e., the specifics of bids Roumoux has submitted).

This is for **two full years** (the First and Second Earnout Periods). A solar competitor who acquires the earnout would receive a continuous feed of Roumoux's go-to-market strategy, pricing architecture, and sales pipeline. This is competitively sensitive information that goes far beyond what is needed to verify Installed Megawatts (which requires only data on systems actually installed and granted PTO).

### Recommended fix

Restore the prior draft's structure with the following modifications to § 2.6(d):

> **(d) *Information Rights.*** Within twenty (20) days after the end of each calendar quarter during each Earnout Period, Buyer shall provide Seller with a report of installation volumes by system and permission-to-operate date for systems installed during such quarter. Seller may use such reports solely to verify Installed Megawatts and shall treat them as confidential information of Buyer. Following delivery of an Earnout Statement, Buyer shall give the independent accounting firm referred to in Section 2.6(c), or Seller's independent accountants subject to a confidentiality undertaking reasonably acceptable to Buyer, reasonable access during normal business hours to Buyer's books and records relating to Installed Megawatts, solely to the extent reasonably necessary to verify the Earnout Statement. **Buyer is not required to disclose customer pipeline reports, pricing data, or proposal data.**

Additionally, add to § 6.6 (Confidentiality):

> From and after the Closing, Seller shall hold in confidence all confidential information concerning the Business and the Purchased Assets, **and all information Seller receives under Section 2.6(d)**, except to the extent such information becomes publicly available other than through Seller's breach of this Section 6.6.

**Flag for partner:** The "or its permitted assignee" language combined with § 10.6's unrestricted assignment right means a third party (potentially a competitor) could receive this data. If the client is unwilling to accept any third-party access, we should also require that earnout information rights be non-assignable or that any assignee be bound by a confidentiality agreement in a form acceptable to Roumoux.

## Issue 4 – Seller May Assign All Deal Rights (Including Indemnification Claims) to Any Person

### Where it appears

- **APA § 10.6** (current): "…Seller may assign any of its rights under this Agreement, **including the right to receive Earnout Payments and to make claims under Article VIII**, to any Person without the consent of Buyer."
- **Prior Draft § 10.6**: contained no such Seller assignment right. The prior draft stated: "Neither Party may assign its rights or obligations under this Agreement without the prior written consent of the other Party, and **any purported assignment without that consent is void**." (Only Buyer had a limited assignment right to Affiliates and lenders.)

### Why it matters for Roumoux

1. **Indemnification claims can be sold to a third party.** Seller could assign its right to receive indemnification payments (under § 8.3, for Assumed Liabilities) or its general deal rights to a fund or a competitor. More concerning, Seller could assign the benefit of the non-compete in § 6.7 to a third party, potentially creating an awkward enforcement dynamic.

2. **Combined with Issue 3**, Seller could assign the earnout (and the associated information rights) to a direct competitor of Roumoux in the Arizona solar market. That competitor would then have both (a) a financial stake in Roumoux's performance and (b) quarterly access to Roumoux's pipeline and pricing data.

3. **Removal of "void" language.** The prior draft made unauthorized assignments void. The current draft does not. While this is less of an issue for Seller (who has broad assignment rights), it weakens the overall anti-assignment framework.

### Recommended fix

Modify § 10.6 as follows:

> **10.6 Successors and Assigns.** This Agreement is binding upon and inures to the benefit of the Parties and their respective successors and permitted assigns. Neither Party may assign its rights or obligations under this Agreement without the prior written consent of the other Party, except that (a) Buyer may assign its rights under this Agreement to one or more of its Affiliates or to any lender as collateral security, and (b) Seller may assign its right to receive Earnout Payments to one or more of its Affiliates or to a financial institution as collateral security for its obligations to an Affiliate. **Any assignment of Seller's rights under this Agreement to a Person that is a competitor of Buyer in the solar photovoltaic installation business in the State of Arizona requires Buyer's prior written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld.** No assignment relieves the assigning Party of its obligations hereunder.

Alternatively, if the client wants a simpler fix: limit Seller's assignment right to "its Affiliates or to a financial institution" and delete the reference to "to make claims under Article VIII."

**Flag for partner:** This is a judgment call. The other side may have inserted this for legitimate reasons (e.g., Seller wants to be able to monetize the earnout by selling it to a fund). We should ask the client whether it would be acceptable for Seller to assign the earnout to a financial institution (as opposed to a competitor) and whether the client has any concern about Seller assigning indemnification claims.

## Issue 5 – No Contractual Mechanism to Secure Okafor-Lindqvist for Roumoux Post-Closing

### Where it appears

- **APA § 6.5**: "Buyer **may, but is not obligated to**, offer employment to any employee of Seller."
- **No employment agreement or side letter between Roumoux and Okafor-Lindqvist** is referenced in any of the transaction documents.
- **KEA § 9.2**: assignment requires her consent (see Issue 1).
- **APA § 6.4**: Seller must cooperate for 180 days post-Closing in license transition, "including by making available the qualifying party."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

The deal structure implicitly depends on Okafor-Lindqvist being available to Roumoux post-Closing (as qualifying party, as operational leader, and as a non-compete obligor). But:

- If the KEA assignment is void (Issue 1), she has no contractual relationship with Roumoux.
- APA § 6.5 makes it optional for Roumoux to offer her employment—there is no obligation, no condition to Closing, and no agreed-upon terms.
- APA § 6.4 obligates *Seller* to cooperate, but Seller cannot compel Okafor-Lindqvist to act after her employment with Seller ends.
- If Roumoux does not hire her, or if she refuses to cooperate with the license transition, Roumoux may be unable to obtain the contractor licenses necessary to operate the Business.

### Recommended fix

1. **Make Okafor-Lindqvist's consent to the KEA assignment (Issue 1) a condition to Closing** under § 7.2.
2. **Negotiate a direct employment agreement between Roumoux and Okafor-Lindqvist** as a condition to Closing, or at minimum a letter of intent confirming:
   - She will accept employment with Roumoux at terms no less favorable than the KEA (base salary ≥ $185,000; target bonus ≥ 30%; qualifying party stipend ≥ $1,500/month);
   - She will serve as qualifying party for Roumoux's contractor license applications; and
   - The restrictive covenants in KEA § 6 will run in favor of Roumoux.
3. **Add to § 7.2 (Conditions to Buyer's Obligations):** "Okafor-Lindqvist shall have executed and delivered a consent to assignment of the Key Employee Agreement and an employment agreement (or letter of intent) with Buyer, in each case in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer."

**Flag for client:** We need to confirm the client's intent regarding Okafor-Lindqvist's employment. If the client plans to hire her, we should start negotiating terms now. If the client is open to not hiring her, we need to develop an alternative qualifying-party strategy (e.g., identifying another qualified individual within the organization) and assess whether the 180-day transition window in § 6.4 is sufficient.

## Issue 6 – Confidentiality Obligation Does Not Clearly Cover Earnout Information

### Where it appears

- **APA § 6.6** (current): "From and after the Closing, Seller shall hold in confidence all confidential information **concerning the Business and the Purchased Assets**, except to the extent such information becomes publicly available other than through Seller's breach of this Section 6.6."
- **Recital A**: defines "the Business" as Seller's business of designing, selling, installing and servicing solar systems in Arizona.
- **Prior Draft § 6.6**: expressly included "and all information Seller receives under Section 2.6(d)."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

The information Roumoux must share under § 2.6(d)—pipeline reports, pricing, proposal data—relates to **Roumoux's** forward-looking business, not to "the Business" (which is defined as Seller's historical business) or the "Purchased Assets." A narrow reading of § 6.6 would not cover this information. If Seller (or its assignee) discloses Roumoux's pricing or pipeline to a third party, Roumoux may have no contractual remedy under § 6.6.

### Recommended fix

Amend § 6.6 to:

> **6.6 Confidentiality.** From and after the Closing, Seller shall hold in confidence all confidential information concerning the Business and the Purchased Assets, **and all information Seller or its assignees receive under Section 2.6(d)**, except to the extent such information becomes publicly available other than through Seller's breach of this Section 6.6. This obligation survives for so long as the information remains non-public.

## Issue 7 – Bill of Sale Governing Law and Jurisdiction Differ from APA

### Where it appears

- **APA §§ 10.10–10.11**: Delaware governing law; exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware Court of Chancery (or Superior Court).
- **Bill of Sale §§ 6.1–6.2**: Arizona governing law; exclusive jurisdiction in Maricopa County, Arizona state courts.
- **APA § 10.13**: "the Bill of Sale controls on matters concerning the transfer of title to the Purchased Assets."

### Why it matters for Roumoux

If a title dispute arises (e.g., a lien is not properly released, or a third party claims an interest in the Fleet and Equipment), the dispute would be governed by Arizona law in Arizona courts, while all other disputes under the APA would be in Delaware. This creates:
- Potential for inconsistent rulings if related claims are split across jurisdictions;
- Increased litigation cost and complexity; and
- A possible argument that a title dispute is really an indemnification dispute (which would be in Delaware).

This is a common structure in asset purchases and is not necessarily wrong, but it is worth flagging.

### Recommended fix

This is low-priority. If the client wants uniformity, we could conform the Bill of Sale to Delaware law and jurisdiction, with the proviso that matters of real property or UCC perfection would be governed by the law of the relevant jurisdiction. However, given that the assets are in Arizona and the Seller is an Arizona corporation, Arizona law for the Bill of Sale is defensible. I would not push on this unless the client raises it.

## Issues 8–10 – Lower-Priority Observations

### Issue 8 – Cap and Deductible Are a Tipping Basket, Not a Deductible

**Where:** APA § 8.5(a).

The current language states: "An Indemnifying Party shall not be liable…until the aggregate amount of Losses thereunder exceeds $138,000, **after which it shall be liable only for Losses in excess of such amount**."

This is a true deductible (not a tipping basket/threshold). Once Losses exceed $138,000, the Indemnifying Party pays only the excess. The prior draft used the same structure but labeled it a "Deductible" and used the word "excess" more clearly. The current draft's use of "Cap and Basket" as the heading is misleading—it is a cap and a deductible, not a basket. This is a drafting hygiene issue, not a substantive one, but we should correct the heading to "Cap and Deductible" to avoid ambiguity.

### Issue 9 – No Specific Indemnity for Breach of Non-Compete (§ 6.7)

**Where:** APA § 6.7; § 8.2(b).

Seller's non-compete is a covenant, so a breach would be indemnifiable under § 8.2(b) (breach of covenant). However, § 8.2(b) claims are subject to the $2,760,000 Cap and $138,000 Deductible (unlike Fundamental Reps or fraud). If Seller breaches the non-compete and the harm to Roumoux exceeds the cap, Roumoux's recovery is limited. Given that the non-compete is a key protection (especially if the KEA non-compete is lost per Issue 1), we should consider whether § 6.7 breaches should be carved out of the Cap.

**Recommended fix:** Add to § 8.5(b): "Losses arising out of any breach of Section 6.7 (Non-Competition) shall not be subject to the Cap or the deductible in Section 8.5(a)."

### Issue 10 – No Rep That Okafor-Lindqvist Will Remain Available

**Where:** APA Article IV (Seller's Reps and Warranties).

There is no representation by Seller that Okafor-Lindqvist will remain employed through Closing, that she will consent to the KEA assignment, or that she will cooperate with the license transition. The only related protection is the § 7.2 closing conditions (which do not include any Okafor-Lindqvist-specific condition). If she resigns or becomes unavailable between now and Closing, Roumoux has no walk-right specifically tied to her status.

**Recommended fix:** Add to § 7.2: "(f) Okafor-Lindqvist shall remain employed by Seller and shall have executed the consent and cooperation described in [the tripartite agreement referenced in Issue 1]."
