## Issue 1 – Price Objection Was Delivered Late; 14% Increase Likely Deemed Accepted

**Document / Section:** Zeisein Letter dated September 14, 2026; Supply Agreement § 5.2–5.3

**Why it matters:** Bruben's Price Adjustment Notice was dated and delivered July 28, 2026, proposing a 14% increase effective October 1, 2026. Section 5.3 requires Customer to deliver a written objection "within 30 days after receipt." Thirty days after July 28 is **August 27, 2026**. Zeisein's letter is dated **September 14, 2026**—18 days past the deadline. The operative consequence of § 5.3 is explicit: "If Customer does not deliver a notice of objection within that period, the adjusted prices are deemed accepted by Customer and take effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice." On the face of the contract, the 14% increase became effective October 1, 2026, and Zeisein's September 14 letter is a nullity as a § 5.3 objection.

**Recommended fix:**

- Immediately assess whether Zeisein has any factual or equitable argument that the notice was not "received" on July 28 (e.g., courier misdelivery, wrong addressee). The letter states it was "delivered to our King of Prussia offices by courier the same day," which undercuts this.
- If no such argument exists, treat the 14% increase as contractually in effect. Negotiate a commercial resolution: ask Bruben to agree, by written amendment under § 18.3, to revert to the original prices or to a lower increase, in exchange for Zeisein's continued performance and perhaps a volume commitment.
- Going forward, implement an internal calendar with a 20-day alert (i.e., 10 days before the contractual deadline) for any Price Adjustment Notice. Assign ownership to the Director of Modernization or General Counsel.
- Consider whether to raise the lateness as a negotiating point (i.e., "we objected late, but we believe the increase is unjustified and we are prepared to negotiate in good faith") or to simply negotiate the economics without referencing the procedural default. The latter is likely more effective commercially.

## Issue 2 – Order of Precedence Causes the Site Indemnity to Override the Product-Defect Indemnity

**Document / Section:** Order Form § 6.1; Supply Agreement §§ 11.1(a), 18.2, 12.2(b)

**Why it matters:** Order Form § 6.1 requires Zeisein to indemnify Bruben for all claims "arising from site conditions at any Program Site, or from the installation, adjustment, testing, start-up or **operation** of Products at any Program Site," including claims by Bruben's own employees and subcontractors. Supply Agreement § 11.1(a) requires Bruben to indemnify Zeisein for claims arising from "any defect in a Product."

Section 18.2 establishes the order of precedence: **Service Level Schedule → Order Form → Supply Agreement body**. Because the Order Form ranks above the Supply Agreement, in any conflict the Order Form controls. A product-defect injury at a Program Site is simultaneously (a) a claim "arising from a defect in a Product" under § 11.1(a) and (b) a claim arising from "operation of Products at any Program Site" under Order Form § 6.1. Under the precedence rule, § 6.1 prevails, meaning **Zeisein indemnifies Bruben for product-defect injuries at its own sites**.

Compounding this, § 12.2(b) carves "a Party's obligations under any Order Form" out of the liability cap in § 12.1. Zeisein's site indemnity is an obligation under the Order Form, so it is **uncapped**. Zeisein therefore bears unlimited, first-dollar indemnification exposure for product-defect claims at Program Sites.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate an amendment to Order Form § 6.1 that carves out claims "to the extent arising from a defect in design, materials or workmanship of a Product" (tracking the language of § 9.1(b)). This restores the intended allocation: Bruben covers product defects; Zeisein covers site conditions and its own installation errors.
- Alternatively, amend § 18.2 to provide that, as between the Order Form and the Supply Agreement, the indemnification provisions of § 11 control over any conflicting indemnity in an Order Form.
- Until the amendment is in place, Zeisein should ensure its own CGL and umbrella policies include adequate products-liability coverage for the Bruben components it installs, and should confirm with its broker that the policies respond to third-party bodily-injury claims where the alleged cause is a component defect rather than a site condition.

## Issue 3 – Exclusive Supply Clause Prohibits In-House Refurbishment and Is Overly Broad

**Document / Section:** Supply Agreement § 3.1

**Why it matters:** Section 3.1 prohibits Customer and its Affiliates, "directly or indirectly," from (a) purchasing elevator components from any person other than Bruben, (b) manufacturing elevator components, or (c) "refurbish[ing], rebuild[ing] or remanufactur[ing] Elevator Components for resale or for installation at any customer site," in each case "other than Products supplied by Supplier." The restriction applies "throughout North America" and covers "all Elevator Components" as defined in § 1.2 (an extremely broad definition that includes "parts, assemblies and components of every kind").

For an elevator maintenance and modernization contractor, in-house refurbishment and remanufacturing is a core revenue stream and a standard industry practice. This clause effectively outsources that function to Bruben. It also creates a serious single-source risk: if Bruben experiences a supply disruption beyond the 10-Business-Day force-majeure threshold in § 16.2, Zeisein may be in breach by purchasing substitutes. The clause has no exception for Bruben's failure to supply, no phase-out, and no geographic limitation narrower than all of North America.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a carve-out permitting Zeisein to refurbish, rebuild, or remanufacture components **for use at its own customer sites** (as opposed to for resale), provided the underlying component was originally supplied by Bruben. This preserves the exclusivity for new-component purchases while allowing the maintenance function.
- Add a supply-failure exception: if Bruben is unable to fill an order (other than due to Zeisein's fault), Zeisein may purchase the affected component from an alternate source without breaching § 3.1, at Bruben's price or the alternate source's price, whichever is lower.
- Consider limiting the exclusivity to the Program Territory (PA, NJ, DE) rather than all of North America, or to the specific product categories in the Order Form rather than "all Elevator Components of every kind."

## Issue 4 – Warranty Period Uses "Whichever Ends First," Shortening Coverage for Staged Parts

**Document / Section:** Supply Agreement § 9.1

**Why it matters:** The product warranty runs "for eighteen (18) months after delivery or twelve (12) months after installation, **whichever ends first**." In a modernization program, Zeisein may receive components and hold them in inventory for weeks or months before the corresponding elevator is scheduled for modernization. If a hydraulic power unit is delivered in January and installed in August (7 months later), the warranty expires in July of the following year (12 months after installation), not in July two years later (18 months after delivery). Zeisein loses 6 months of warranty coverage on that unit.

The "whichever ends first" formulation is unusual. Most supply agreements use "whichever ends **later**" to protect the buyer. Here, the shorter period always governs, which disadvantages Zeisein as the party that controls the installation schedule.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend § 9.1 to read "whichever ends **later**." This is the standard buyer-favorable formulation and ensures that a part installed late still gets a full 12 months of post-installation warranty.
- If Bruben resists, propose a compromise: "whichever ends later, provided that in no event shall the warranty period exceed twenty-four (24) months after delivery." This gives Bruben a backstop while protecting Zeisein from the current trap.

## Issue 5 – Emergency-Order Service Credit for Crew Standby Is Inadequate

**Document / Section:** Service Level Schedule § 5.1

**Why it matters:** If Bruben fails to dispatch a Critical Part within the required 4 hours, Zeisein receives (a) a 25% or 50% credit on the part's price and (b) a flat **$750** "to offset Customer's crew standby costs." In an elevator entrapment scenario, Zeisein dispatches a technician (or two) who arrives on site and waits. A single technician's cost for a 4-hour standby (wages, benefits, vehicle, overtime premium if after-hours) easily exceeds $500–$800. If the delay extends to 8 or 12 hours, the cost multiplies. The $750 credit is a fraction of actual standby cost, and the 25–50% part-price credit is modest relative to the operational disruption (a trapped passenger, a building owner's complaint, potential regulatory exposure).

Additionally, the 20% Monthly Fees cap in § 5.3 means that in a bad month with multiple emergency failures, Zeisein's total recovery is limited to 20% of that month's invoices.

**Recommended fix:**

- Replace the flat $750 with a formula tied to actual standby cost, such as "the lesser of (i) Customer's documented standby costs for the affected crew and (ii) $[X] per hour per technician, where X reflects Zeisein's fully loaded hourly rate." Alternatively, increase the flat amount to a more realistic figure (e.g., $2,500–$5,000 per Emergency Order) based on Zeisein's actual cost data.
- Raise the credit cap in § 5.3 from 20% to 35% or 40% of Monthly Fees, or provide that the cap does not apply to credits arising from Emergency Order failures involving a passenger entrapment.
- Add a termination trigger: if Bruben misses the Emergency Dispatch time more than [X] times in any rolling 3-month period, Zeisein may terminate the affected Order Form without cure period (this would supplement the existing § 15.3 trigger, which requires three months of failure of the *same* service level).

## Issue 6 – Parent Guaranty Recovery Is Constrained by the Liability Cap

**Document / Section:** Parent Guaranty § 2.3; Supply Agreement §§ 12.1–12.2

**Why it matters:** Guaranty § 2.3 states: "The Guarantor's liability under this Guaranty is equal to, and **no greater than**, Supplier's liability under the Agreement." Because § 12.1 caps Supplier's general liability at "the fees paid or payable in the 12 months preceding the claim," the parent guarantor's exposure for most breaches (e.g., failure to deliver, failure to meet service levels, breach of the supply commitment in § 3.2) is limited to that same 12-month fee amount.

Early in the relationship, when cumulative fees are low, this cap is small. For example, if Zeisein has purchased $400,000 of products in the 12 months preceding a claim, the maximum recovery from Bruben Industrial Holdings for a supply failure is $400,000—even if Zeisein's actual losses (cost of substitute parts, project delays, liquidated damages to building owners) far exceed that amount.

The guaranty is more valuable for the uncapped categories in § 12.2 (indemnification, Order Form obligations, confidentiality, gross negligence/fraud, payment), but the core supply-and-performance obligations are capped.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a higher cap or a separate, higher cap for supply failures. For example: "Notwithstanding Section 12.1, Supplier's liability for failure to deliver Products or to meet the service levels in the Service Level Schedule shall not be less than the fees paid or payable in the 24 months preceding the claim."
- Alternatively, add a specific minimum to the guaranty: "The Guarantor's liability under this Guaranty shall in no event be less than $[X] million, regardless of the fees paid or payable in any 12-month period."
- Ensure that the "obligations under any Order Form" exclusion in § 12.2(b) is interpreted to include Bruben's obligation to supply the Products listed in the Order Form (not just Zeisein's obligations under the Order Form). The current drafting is ambiguous as to whether it covers both parties' Order Form obligations or only the indemnifying party's.

## Issue 7 – No Express Right to Audit Bruben's Inventory or Production Capacity

**Document / Section:** Supply Agreement § 3.2; Service Level Schedule §§ 6.1(d), 6.3, 7.1

**Why it matters:** Section 3.2 requires Bruben to "maintain production capacity and inventory sufficient to meet Customer's requirements." The Service Level Schedule requires Bruben to report monthly inventory levels (§ 6.1(d)) and to keep minimum stock at each location (§ 7.1). However, Zeisein's right to verify Bruben's compliance is limited to reviewing records "on 10 Business Days' notice, during normal business hours, no more than twice in any 12-month period" (§ 6.3). There is no right to physically inspect stocking locations, observe production, or conduct a surprise audit.

Given the exclusive-supply obligation (Issue 3) and the emergency-dispatch requirements, Zeisein is operationally dependent on Bruben's inventory being where Bruben says it is. Two reviews per year is insufficient for a business that may face multiple emergency orders per month.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend § 6.3 to permit Zeisein to conduct up to four (not two) record reviews per 12-month period, and to add one (1) unannounced physical inventory verification per year at any stocking location, on 48 hours' notice.
- Add a requirement that Bruben provide Zeisein with real-time or near-real-time (e.g., daily) inventory visibility for the Critical Parts in Annex 1, through a shared portal or API feed. This is increasingly standard in supply-chain agreements and would allow Zeisein to plan modernization schedules around actual part availability.

## Issue 8 – Force Majeure Exception in Service Level Schedule Could Excuse Emergency Dispatch Failures

**Document / Section:** Service Level Schedule § 2.3(a); Supply Agreement § 16

**Why it matters:** Service Level Schedule § 2.3(a) provides that a service level failure "does not occur to the extent it is caused by a force majeure event under Section 16 of the Agreement." Section 16.1 defines force majeure broadly to include "epidemics, government orders and labor actions not involving the affected Party's own workforce." A regional power outage, a port strike, a severe weather event, or a pandemic-related shutdown could excuse Bruben from meeting the 4-hour emergency dispatch requirement entirely, with no service credit owed.

For an elevator maintenance company, the inability to obtain a Critical Part during a force majeure event can mean passengers remain trapped for days. While § 16.2 allows Zeisein to purchase substitutes after 10 Business Days, the first 10 days are unprotected, and the force majeure event could last longer.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a provision to the Service Level Schedule stating that, notwithstanding § 2.3(a), if a force majeure event prevents Bruben from meeting the Emergency Dispatch time, Bruben shall (i) notify Zeisein within 1 hour, (ii) use commercially reasonable efforts to source the Critical Part from any available channel (including non-Bruben sources, at Bruben's cost), and (iii) if the part cannot be sourced within 24 hours, Bruben shall reimburse Zeisein for the reasonable cost of procuring the part from an alternate source.
- Shorten the § 16.2 substitute-purchase threshold from 10 Business Days to 5 Business Days for Critical Parts specifically.

## Issue 9 – NDA Term Expires Before the Supply Agreement Term

**Document / Section:** Mutual NDA § 7.1; Supply Agreement § 2.1, Order Form § 2.1

**Why it matters:** The NDA was effective January 12, 2026, with a 3-year term, expiring **January 12, 2029**. The Supply Agreement and Order Form have a 36-month initial term from March 2, 2026, expiring **March 2, 2029**, with automatic 12-month renewals. The NDA therefore expires approximately 2 months before the initial term ends, and well before any renewal terms.

While Supply Agreement § 13.4 states that § 13 "applies in addition to any separate nondisclosure agreement," and § 13 has no stated expiration (it survives under § 15.4), the NDA contains protections not found in § 13: the broader definition of Confidential Information (including "bid information," "customer, building owner and site information, including the identity and location of elevators"), the no-reverse-engineering clause (§ 2.5), and the injunctive-relief acknowledgment (§ 8.1). Once the NDA expires (and its 3-year survival period ends in January 2032), these additional protections fall away, leaving only the narrower § 13.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend NDA § 7.1 to tie the term to the Supply Agreement: "This NDA continues for so long as the Supply Agreement (including any Order Form) remains in effect, and for three (3) years thereafter."
- Alternatively, execute a short amendment extending the NDA term to January 12, 2032 (to cover the initial term plus one renewal plus the survival period).

## Issue 10 – Insurance Certificate: No Evidence of Renewal Tracking

**Document / Section:** Certificate of Insurance (dated February 27, 2026); Supply Agreement § 14.2

**Why it matters:** The certificate evidences policies with a period of **March 1, 2026 to March 1, 2027**. Section 14.2 requires Bruben to deliver a certificate "upon each renewal." There is no certificate in the file for the renewal period beginning March 1, 2027. While the current policy period is still in effect (we are in September 2026), Zeisein has no mechanism in the file to confirm that Bruben will deliver the renewal certificate on time or that coverage will continue at the required limits.

Additionally, the certificate shows the Workers' Compensation policy covers Delaware, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey—matching the Program Territory. If the Program expands or if Bruben uses carriers that operate in other states, the WC coverage may not follow.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add the renewal certificate deadline (February 2027, i.e., 30 days before the March 1, 2027 expiration) to the internal compliance calendar.
- Consider amending § 14.2 to require Bruben to deliver the renewal certificate at least **45 days** (not just "upon each renewal") before the current policy expires, to give Zeisein time to verify coverage and raise any deficiencies.
- Require that the WC policy cover all states in which Zeisein operates or in which Program Sites are located, not just the current three-state territory.

## Issue 11 – Deemed-Acceptance of Purchase Orders Creates Unplanned Commitments

**Document / Section:** Supply Agreement § 4.1

**Why it matters:** Section 4.1 provides that if Bruben does not reject a Purchase Order within two (2) Business Days, "it is deemed accepted." This is buyer-favorable in that it prevents Bruben from sitting on orders. However, it also means that if Zeisein's ordering process has a glitch (duplicate PO, wrong quantity, wrong part number), Bruben has only 2 Business Days to catch it. After that, the erroneous order is binding. Given that the Products are expensive (hydraulic power units at $14,850–$19,975 each), a single erroneous PO could create a six-figure commitment.

**Recommended fix:**

- Implement an internal two-person verification step for all Purchase Orders before transmission, with a log confirming the part number, quantity, and price against the Order Form.
- Consider negotiating an extension of the deemed-acceptance period from 2 to 5 Business Days to give Bruben (and Zeisein) more time to identify errors. Alternatively, add a provision that a PO is not deemed accepted if it contains an obvious clerical error (e.g., a quantity that is an order of magnitude different from the forecast).

## Issue 12 – Volume Rebate Threshold May Be Difficult to Reach Given Price Increase

**Document / Section:** Order Form § 3.3; Zeisein Letter (re: 14% price increase)

**Why it matters:** The rebate triggers when aggregate purchases in a Contract Year exceed **$1,500,000**. If the 14% price increase is in effect (see Issue 1), the same physical volume of purchases generates 14% more revenue, making the threshold easier to reach in dollar terms. However, the rebate is only 3% of the *excess* above $1.5 million. If Zeisein purchases $2 million in a year, the rebate is 3% × $500,000 = $15,000. This is a very modest incentive relative to the total spend.

More importantly, if Zeisein's building-owner contracts are fixed-price (as stated in the Zeisein Letter, ¶ 2(c)), a 14% component cost increase directly erodes margin. The 3% rebate on the excess does not offset this.

**Recommended fix:**

- As part of the commercial negotiation on the price increase (Issue 1), request that the rebate threshold be reduced (e.g., to $1,000,000) or the rebate percentage increased (e.g., to 5%) for the duration of the initial term.
- Alternatively, request a most-favored-customer clause: if Bruben offers a lower price or a higher rebate to any other customer of similar volume in the Program Territory, the same terms apply to Zeisein.
