## 1. Price Adjustment Notice Issued During Fixed-Price Period (CRITICAL)

**Where:** Order Form No. 2026-01, § 3.2; Supply Agreement, §§ 5.2–5.3; Zeisein letter dated September 14, 2026, ¶ 1.

**Issue:** Order Form § 3.2 states: "The prices in Section 1.3 are fixed for the first twelve (12) months after the Effective Date." The Effective Date is March 2, 2026, so prices are contractually fixed through March 2, 2027. Bruben's Price Adjustment Notice is dated July 28, 2026—four months *inside* the fixed-price window. Under the order of precedence in Supply Agreement § 18.2, the Order Form controls over the Supply Agreement body, meaning the fixed-price lock overrides Supplier's general right to propose adjustments under § 5.2.

**Why it matters:** The July Notice has no contractual basis. A Price Adjustment Notice issued in violation of the Order Form's fixed-price provision is not a valid notice under § 5.2, which requires compliance with the Agreement as a whole (including the Order Form). This is Zeisein's strongest position in the current price dispute.

**Recommendation:** Advise the client that the July Notice is invalid as a matter of contract. Zeisein should send a follow-up letter (i) stating that the Notice was issued during the fixed-price period and is therefore of no effect, (ii) confirming that the § 1.3 prices remain in force through March 2, 2027, and (iii) reserving all rights. Do not concede that the 30-day objection window under § 5.3 was triggered, because a defective notice does not start the clock. If Bruben disputes this, the matter should be referred to the § 5.3/§ 19.2 dispute process.

## 2. Zeisein's Objection Letter Is 18 Days Late (CRITICAL – but likely moot given Issue 1)

**Where:** Supply Agreement, § 5.3; Zeisein letter dated September 14, 2026, ¶ 1.

**Issue:** Section 5.3 requires Customer to deliver a notice of objection "within 30 days after receipt of a Price Adjustment Notice." The July Notice was delivered by courier on July 28, 2026 (per the letter itself). Thirty days later is August 27, 2026. The objection letter is dated September 14, 2026—18 days after the deadline. Section 5.3 provides that if Customer does not object within the window, "the adjusted prices are deemed accepted by Customer and take effect on the date stated in the Price Adjustment Notice."

**Why it matters:** If the July Notice were valid, Zeisein has already lost its contractual right to object, and the 14% increase takes effect October 1, 2026. The letter as drafted concedes the framework of § 5.3 ("Under Section 5.3 of the Agreement, Customer hereby objects…"), which could be read as an admission that the notice was valid and that Zeisein is attempting (belatedly) to use the objection mechanism.

**Recommendation:** Because Issue 1 likely renders the notice invalid, this problem may be moot. However, the client should be warned that Bruben will argue the objection was late. The follow-up letter recommended in Issue 1 should reframe the correspondence: Zeisein is not "objecting" to a valid notice but rather *disputing the validity* of the notice itself. Avoid language that concedes the § 5.3 process was properly triggered.

## 3. "Critical Part" Definition Conflict – Narrow SLS Definition Controls (HIGH)

**Where:** Service Level Schedule, § 1.2 and Annex 1; Supply Agreement, § 1.4; Supply Agreement, § 18.2.

**Issue:** The Supply Agreement defines "Critical Part" broadly in § 1.4: "any Elevator Component that is needed to release passengers from an entrapment or to return any elevator to service." The Service Level Schedule defines it narrowly in § 1.2: "an Elevator Component listed in Annex 1." Annex 1 lists only six part numbers. Under § 18.2, the SLS prevails over the Supply Agreement body. Therefore, only those six parts qualify as Critical Parts for the emergency dispatch regime (SLS § 3), the 24/7 order desk (Supply Agreement § 4.3), and the associated service credits (SLS § 5.1).

**Why it matters:** In a real entrapment, the part needed to free passengers may not be one of the six listed items (e.g., a door operator motor, a hydraulic hose, a limit switch). If it is not in Annex 1, Bruben has no contractual obligation to dispatch it under the emergency timeline, and Zeisein has no service credit if delivery is late. Zeisein's crews could be stranded at a site with trapped passengers, with no contractual leverage to compel fast delivery.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate an amendment to Annex 1 to add all parts that could realistically be needed for entrapment release or return-to-service. Alternatively, amend SLS § 1.2 to incorporate the broader Supply Agreement § 1.4 definition, with Annex 1 serving as a *minimum* list rather than an exclusive one. Until the list is expanded, Zeisein should maintain its own emergency inventory of the most common entrapment-release parts as a business mitigation.

## 4. Exclusive Supply Combined with Narrow Force Majeure Carve-Out (HIGH)

**Where:** Supply Agreement, §§ 3.1, 16.2, 15.1–15.2.

**Issue:** Section 3.1 makes Bruben the "exclusive source of all Elevator Components purchased or used by Customer and its Affiliates throughout North America." It prohibits Zeisein from purchasing from any other supplier, manufacturing parts, or even refurbishing components for its own customers. The only express carve-out from exclusivity is § 16.2, which permits substitute purchasing if a force majeure event prevents supply for more than 10 Business Days—and only for the duration of that event.

If Bruben simply fails to perform (production shortfall, quality issues, financial distress short of bankruptcy), Zeisein has no right to buy from another source. It must: (a) prove material breach, (b) give 30 days to cure (§ 15.1), and (c) terminate—during which time it remains locked into exclusivity. The insolvency trigger (§ 15.2) requires a bankruptcy filing that is not dismissed within 60 days. A supplier that is struggling but not yet in bankruptcy leaves Zeisein with no exit.

**Why it matters:** For an elevator maintenance company, supply continuity is existential. A multi-week gap in critical component supply could mean missed maintenance windows, entrapments, and liability to building owners. The exclusivity clause removes Zeisein's ability to self-help.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate a "supply failure" provision that allows Zeisein to purchase substitutes (with cost recovery from Bruben) if Bruben fails to deliver against an accepted Purchase Order or Emergency Order for more than a specified period (e.g., 5 Business Days for Critical Parts, 15 Business Days for standard items), without requiring termination first. Also consider narrowing § 3.1 to exclude components that Bruben does not actually manufacture (e.g., door hardware from a third-party OEM), so the exclusivity obligation is limited to Bruben's product lines.

## 5. Site Indemnity Is Overly Broad (HIGH)

**Where:** Order Form No. 2026-01, § 6.1.

**Issue:** Zeisein indemnifies Bruben for "all claims…arising from site conditions at any Program Site, or from the installation, adjustment, testing, start-up or operation of Products at any Program Site." This covers: (a) pre-existing site conditions (reasonable), (b) installation and commissioning (reasonable, since Zeisein performs those), and (c) *operation* of the Products (problematic). "Operation" is not defined and could be read to cover the entire operational life of the elevator—years after installation. It also expressly includes claims by "Supplier's own employees, agents and subcontractors," meaning if Bruben's delivery driver is injured at a site due to a wet floor, Zeisein indemnifies Bruben.

Because the Order Form prevails over the Supply Agreement (§ 18.2), and because § 12.2(b) excludes Order Form obligations from the liability cap, this indemnity is uncapped.

**Why it matters:** Zeisein could face open-ended indemnification exposure for elevator malfunctions that occur years after installation, for injuries to Bruben's own personnel at Zeisein's customer sites, and for site conditions that Zeisein did not create or control. This is inconsistent with the mutual indemnity structure in Supply Agreement § 11, where each party indemnifies only for its own negligence or willful misconduct.

**Recommendation:** Amend Order Form § 6.1 to: (a) limit the "operation" language to the initial start-up and commissioning period (e.g., 30 days after installation), or delete "operation" entirely; (b) exclude claims by Supplier's own employees from the indemnity (those should be covered by Bruben's workers' compensation); (c) make the indemnity subject to the same fault standard as Supply Agreement § 11.2 (i.e., arising from Customer's negligence or willful misconduct); and (d) confirm the indemnity is subject to the § 12 liability cap (or at least a negotiated sub-cap).

## 6. Warranty Period Is Shorter Than Expected (MEDIUM)

**Where:** Supply Agreement, § 9.1.

**Issue:** The product warranty runs for "eighteen (18) months after delivery or twelve (12) months after installation, whichever ends first." Because elevator components are typically installed shortly after delivery, the 12-months-after-installation period will almost always expire before the 18-months-after-delivery period. The practical warranty is 12 months from installation.

**Why it matters:** Hydraulic power units, valve assemblies, and controller boards are subject to significant cyclic stress in elevator service. A 12-month warranty is short for capital equipment with a useful life of 10–20 years. If a controller board fails at month 13, Zeisein bears the cost of the part plus the labor to replace it (which § 9.4 would have covered had the warranty still been in effect).

**Recommendation:** Negotiate to change "whichever ends first" to "whichever ends later," or extend the installation-based period to 18 or 24 months. At minimum, for the higher-value items (hydraulic power units at $14,850–$19,975), seek a longer warranty period.

## 7. Volume Rebate Threshold Likely Unattainable (MEDIUM)

**Where:** Order Form No. 2026-01, §§ 3.3, 4.1.

**Issue:** The rebate triggers only if aggregate purchases in a Contract Year exceed $1,500,000. The program estimate is approximately 90 elevators over 36 months (§ 4.1), or roughly 30 per year. Based on the § 1.3 pricing, a typical elevator modernization package (one HPU, one main valve, one controller, one door operator interface, solenoid coils, I/O board) runs approximately $25,000–$35,000. At 30 elevators per year, annual purchases would be roughly $750,000–$1,050,000—well below the $1.5M threshold.

**Why it matters:** The rebate appears to be a negotiating placeholder that Zeisein will never actually earn. It creates an expectation of savings that will not materialize, and the 3% rate on the excess is modest even if triggered.

**Recommendation:** Either (a) reduce the threshold to a level the program can realistically reach (e.g., $750,000), or (b) restructure as a tiered rebate (e.g., 1% above $750K, 3% above $1M, 5% above $1.5M), or (c) delete the provision and use the negotiating capital to secure a lower base price.

## 8. Service Credit Cap May Be Inadequate (MEDIUM)

**Where:** Service Level Schedule, §§ 5.1, 5.3.

**Issue:** Total service credits in any Measurement Period are capped at 20% of Monthly Fees (§ 5.3). The per-event credits for missed Emergency Dispatches are modest: 25% or 50% of the part price plus $750 (§ 5.1). For a $168 solenoid coil, the maximum per-event credit is $84 + $750 = $834.

**Why it matters:** If Bruben repeatedly misses emergency dispatches in a month—say, 10 missed dispatches of low-cost parts—the total credit would be roughly $8,340. If Monthly Fees for that month are $100,000, the 20% cap ($20,000) is not hit, but the credits still far undercompensate Zeisein for crew standby time, building owner complaints, and reputational harm. More importantly, if the missed parts are higher-value (e.g., a $3,410 controller board), the per-event credit is larger but the number of events that can be credited before hitting the cap is smaller. The cap means that in a bad month, Zeisein absorbs the excess losses with no further remedy short of the § 15.3 termination trigger (same service level missed in 3 of 12 months).

**Recommendation:** Raise the cap to 30% or 40% of Monthly Fees, or add a "super-cap" exception for months with multiple missed Emergency Dispatches (where passenger safety is implicated). Also consider adding a per-event minimum credit (e.g., not less than $2,000 per missed Emergency Order) to ensure the credit is meaningful even for low-cost parts.

## 9. NDA Term Expires Before Supply Agreement Term (LOW)

**Where:** Mutual NDA, § 7.1; Order Form No. 2026-01, § 2.1.

**Issue:** The NDA was effective January 12, 2026 and runs for 3 years, expiring January 12, 2029. The Supply Agreement's Initial Term is 36 months from March 2, 2026, ending March 2, 2029. There is a roughly two-month gap (January 12 – March 2, 2029) where the NDA has expired but the Supply Agreement is still in effect.

**Why it matters:** In practice, this is unlikely to cause a problem because Supply Agreement § 13 provides its own confidentiality obligations that survive termination (§ 15.4), and NDA § 7.2 provides 3 years of post-expiration survival for information disclosed during the NDA term. However, if the NDA is terminated early (either party can give 30 days' notice under § 7.1), the survival period could end well before the Supply Agreement term, leaving a gap for information that was disclosed under the NDA but not under the Supply Agreement.

**Recommendation:** Low priority. If the parties amend the NDA for any reason, align its term with the Supply Agreement term (or make it co-terminus with the Supply Agreement plus the survival period). Otherwise, monitor that neither party inadvertently terminates the NDA early.

## 10. Supply Commitment Uses a Relative Standard That Is Difficult to Enforce (LOW)

**Where:** Supply Agreement, § 3.2.

**Issue:** Section 3.2 requires Supplier to "maintain production capacity and inventory sufficient to meet Customer's requirements" and provides that Supplier "shall not allocate available supply in a way that disfavors Customer as compared with Supplier's other customers." The second sentence is a relative standard—Zeisein would need to show that Bruben treated its other customers better. In a supply-constrained scenario, Bruben could argue it allocated based on order priority, contract value, or geographic proximity, and that no customer was "disfavored" as compared to Zeisein.

**Why it matters:** In a genuine supply shortage (e.g., a chip shortage affecting controller boards), Zeisein may find itself deprioritized behind larger or more profitable Bruben customers, with no clear contractual basis to complain. The "sufficient capacity" language in the first sentence is also unquantified.

**Recommendation:** Add a most-favored-customer clause or a priority provision: in any supply allocation, Customer's orders (particularly Emergency Orders for Critical Parts) shall be filled before or concurrently with orders from Supplier's other customers of comparable size. Alternatively, require Bruben to maintain a minimum inventory level (beyond the Annex 1 minimums) calibrated to Zeisein's forecast.

## 11. Certificate of Insurance – Confirm Endorsements Are Attached (LOW)

**Where:** Bruben Certificate of Insurance (Feb. 27, 2026), §§ 5.1–5.3; Supply Agreement, § 14.2.

**Issue:** The certificate references specific endorsements for additional insured status (CG 20 15 04 13, CG 20 37 04 13, CA 20 48 10 13) and waiver of subrogation (CG 24 04 05 09, CA 04 44 10 13, WC 00 03 13). The certificate itself "confers no rights" (preamble to § 6). The actual protection depends on whether those endorsements are properly attached to the underlying policies with the correct additional insured language.

**Why it matters:** If an endorsement is missing or contains a narrower additional insured grant than the certificate implies, Zeisein could discover the gap only when it needs to file a claim. The products-completed operations additional insured status (CG 20 37) is particularly important given that Zeisein installs Bruben's products in elevators that operate for years.

**Recommendation:** Request copies of the actual policies and endorsements (not just the certificate) from Bruben's broker, Delmarva Risk Partners. Verify that (a) the additional insured endorsements are primary and non-contributory, (b) the products-completed operations grant is not limited to "operations performed for" the additional insured (which could exclude Zeisein's own installation work), and (c) the waiver of subrogation is mutual and covers all additional insureds. Set a calendar reminder to request updated certificates at each annual renewal (policy period ends March 1, 2027).

## 12. Termination Trigger Requires the *Same* Service Level to Be Missed Three Times (LOW)

**Where:** Supply Agreement, § 15.3.

**Issue:** Customer may terminate for "repeated service failures" only if Supplier "fails to meet the same service level under the Service Level Schedule in any three (3) months within a rolling twelve (12) month period." If Bruben misses the emergency dispatch time in January, the on-time delivery percentage in March, and order accuracy in May, no single service level has been missed three times, and the termination trigger never fires.

**Why it matters:** A pattern of chronic underperformance across *different* service levels—each individually below the termination threshold—could persist indefinitely without giving Zeisein a termination right. Zeisein would be limited to accumulating service credits (subject to the 20% cap in SLS § 5.3) and the general material breach provision (§ 15.1), which requires a higher showing.

**Recommendation:** Amend § 15.3 to allow termination if Supplier fails to meet *any* service level (not just the same one) in three or more months within a rolling 12-month period. Alternatively, add a "cumulative failure" trigger: if the total service credits accrued in any rolling 12-month period exceed a specified threshold (e.g., 50% of total Monthly Fees for that period), Customer may terminate.
