## ISSUE 1 – NIMBARA CHANGE-OF-CONTROL CONSENT OMITTED FROM REQUIRED CONSENTS

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Required Consents"); SPA § 6.4(c) (closing condition); SPA § 5.5 (covenant to obtain consents)
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.4, Item 1 (acknowledges Nimbara consent is required); Schedule 4.10(a), Item 1 (describes the Nimbara MSA); Schedule 6.4 (lists only the Ostrander Park landlord consent)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The Nimbara Master Supply Agreement is the Company's largest contract, representing 38% of FY2025 revenue. Section 14.2 of that agreement requires Nimbara's prior written consent to a change of control; absent consent, Nimbara may terminate and claim service credits. The Disclosure Schedule correctly identifies this consent as required (Schedule 4.4, Item 1), yet Schedule 6.4—the schedule that feeds the SPA's definition of "Required Consents"—lists only the Ostrander Park landlord consent. As a result:

- The closing condition in § 6.4(c) ("all Required Consents shall have been obtained") does **not** protect Buyer against Nimbara's refusal to consent.
- The covenant in § 5.5 to use "reasonable best efforts" to obtain Required Consents does **not** obligate Seller to pursue Nimbara's consent.
- If Nimbara consents conditionally or not at all, Buyer closes without a walk right and without a specific indemnity trigger tied to the consent failure (the matter is disclosed, so the § 4.4 rep is qualified).

This is the single largest revenue-concentration risk in the deal and the SPA currently provides no structural protection against it.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add the Nimbara consent to Schedule 6.4 so it becomes a "Required Consent" under the SPA. This activates both the § 5.5 covenant and the § 6.4(c) closing condition.
- Alternatively (if Seller resists making it a condition to Buyer's obligation to close), add a specific closing condition in § 6.4 that the Nimbara MSA remains in full force and effect and that Nimbara has delivered written consent to the change of control.
- In either case, add a specific indemnity in § 9.2 for losses arising from Nimbara's termination or exercise of service-credit rights if consent is not obtained pre-closing, carved out from the Cap.
- **Flag: resolve before signing.** Without this fix, Starev is paying $184M for a company whose largest customer can walk the day after closing with no contractual remedy beyond a general (and capped) indemnity for a rep that is already disclosed away.

## ISSUE 2 – SIDE LETTER NARROWS CAP EXCLUSIONS TO FRAUD ONLY

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.5(a) (Cap = 10% of Purchase Price = $18.4M, applying to § 9.2(a) losses); § 9.5(b) (exclusions from Cap: Fraud, Fundamental Reps, and § 9.2(d) Scheduled Refrigerant Matter)
- Side Letter § 3 ("the exclusions from the Cap under Section 9.5(b) of the Agreement are limited solely to Fraud")
- SPA § 11.11 (order of precedence: Side Letter controls over SPA)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
Under the SPA as originally drafted, three categories of loss were uncapped: (i) Fraud, (ii) breach of Fundamental Representations (§§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.5, 4.17), and (iii) the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity (§ 9.2(d)). The Side Letter, which has contractual priority, reduces the exclusions to Fraud alone. The practical effects:

- **Fundamental Reps are now capped at $18.4M.** If Seller's title to the Shares is defective, or the capitalization rep is wrong (e.g., undisclosed options or liens), Buyer's recovery for those breaches is limited to $18.4M—against a $184M purchase price.
- **Scheduled Refrigerant Matter is now capped at $18.4M** (if the Cap is read to apply to § 9.2(d) losses; see ambiguity note below). The current estimated remaining cost is $3.9M (Disclosure Schedule 4.15), which fits within the Cap. But HFC remediation costs are notoriously volatile; if the DNREC work plan expands or groundwater contamination is found beyond the current monitoring area, costs could exceed $18.4M. Buyer would be out of pocket for the excess with no recourse against Seller.
- **The deductible in § 9.5(c) ($1.38M) does not apply to § 9.2(d) losses** (it applies only to § 9.2(a)), so the Refrigerant Matter indemnity is at least not subject to the deductible. But the Cap exposure remains.

**Ambiguity to resolve:** Section 9.5(a) defines the Cap as applying to "Losses for which Seller is liable under Section 9.2(a)." Section 9.2(d) is a separate subsection. Read literally, the Cap never applied to § 9.2(d) losses, making the original § 9.5(b) reference to the Refrigerant Matter redundant and the Side Letter's narrowing of that reference a nullity. However, the drafter's intent in listing § 9.2(d) in § 9.5(b) suggests the Cap was meant to apply to all of § 9.2. This ambiguity must be resolved in Buyer's favor.

**Recommended fix:**
- Strike Side Letter § 3 in its entirety, restoring the SPA's original three-category exclusion from the Cap (Fraud, Fundamental Reps, and § 9.2(d)).
- If Seller insists on narrowing, accept Fraud and Fundamental Reps as uncapped but **carve out § 9.2(d) (Scheduled Refrigerant Matter) from the Cap**, given the known and quantified environmental liability.
- Clarify in § 9.5(a) that the Cap applies to § 9.2(a) losses only, so that § 9.2(b) (covenant breaches), § 9.2(c) (pre-closing taxes), and § 9.2(d) (Refrigerant Matter) are not subject to the Cap.
- **Flag: resolve before signing.** This is a direct reduction in Buyer's post-closing protection on a known, quantified $3.9M+ liability and on fundamental deal reps.

## ISSUE 3 – KELVARA PRICE-LOCK OPTION DEADLINE HAS ALREADY PASSED

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule 4.11, Item 3 (Kelvara Fluids Supply Agreement: Initial Term expires December 29, 2026; exercise notice required "not less than ninety (90) days before the expiration of the Initial Term")
- Side Letter § 2.1 (covenants Seller to cause the Company to exercise the option "Before the Closing"); § 2.2 (evidence of exercise due 5 Business Days before Closing); § 2.3 (covenant is for purposes of §§ 5.1 and 6.4(b))
- SPA § 3.1 (Closing Date: November 20, 2026)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
Ninety days before December 29, 2026 is **September 30, 2026**. The SPA is dated October 6, 2026. The exercise deadline passed **before the parties signed the agreement**. The Side Letter's covenant to exercise the option "before the Closing" (November 20, 2026) is therefore impossible to perform as written.

If the option is not exercised:
- After December 29, 2026 (approximately six weeks post-closing), the Kelvara agreement converts to month-to-month at "Kelvara's then-current list pricing," terminable by either party on 30 days' notice.
- The Company's supply of single-phase dielectric immersion fluid—critical to its core product—becomes unpriced and terminable at will.
- There is no closing condition protecting Buyer against this outcome because the Side Letter covenant, while technically a condition under § 6.4(b) (performance of covenants in all material respects), is impossible to satisfy.

**Facts needed from client:**
- Was the extension option already exercised before September 30, 2026? If so, obtain a copy of the notice and Kelvara's acknowledgment.
- If not, has Kelvara been informally approached about extending the price-lock?
- What is Kelvara's current list pricing relative to the Locked Price (Exhibit B to the Kelvara agreement)?

**Recommended fix:**
- If the option was already exercised: require Seller to deliver evidence of exercise as a closing deliverable (add to § 3.2) and confirm the extension is in effect.
- If the option was **not** exercised (and the deadline has passed):
  - Replace Side Letter § 2.1 with a covenant requiring Seller to negotiate and deliver, before the Closing, a new written agreement with Kelvara extending the price-lock for 24 months at the Locked Price (or at a price no greater than the Locked Price plus a specified percentage).
  - Add a closing condition in § 6.4 that the extended Kelvara agreement has been executed and delivered.
  - If Seller cannot deliver the extension, add a specific indemnity in § 9.2 for the excess of actual fluid costs over the Locked Price during the 24-month period, uncapped.
- **Flag: resolve before signing.** Starev should not close a $184M acquisition of an immersion-cooling company without locked pricing on its primary input.

## ISSUE 4 – "MATERIAL ADVERSE EFFECT" IS NOT DEFINED

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (Definitions) – "Material Adverse Effect" does not appear
- SPA § 4.7 (Absence of Certain Changes – rep that no MAE has occurred since December 31, 2025)
- SPA § 6.4(e) (closing condition – no MAE shall have occurred)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
"Material Adverse Effect" is used in both a representation and a closing condition but is never defined. This creates several problems:

- **Scope uncertainty.** Without a definition, it is unclear whether the term includes (or excludes) general economic downturns, industry-wide conditions, the pending Refrigerant Matter costs, the loss of the Kelvara price-lock, or the potential Nimbara termination. Delaware courts construe undefined MAE clauses narrowly, which generally favors the party against whom the condition is enforced (here, Seller), making it harder for Buyer to walk.
- **Closing condition weakness.** § 6.4(e) is one of Buyer's primary walk rights. An undefined MAE gives Seller an argument that only existential, enterprise-wide deterioration qualifies—far higher a bar than a defined MAE with standard carve-outs would set.
- **Representation overlap.** § 4.7 uses the same undefined term. If an MAE occurs between signing and closing, Buyer's ability to claim a rep breach (and trigger indemnification) depends on the same ambiguous standard.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add a definition of "Material Adverse Effect" in § 1.1. A market-standard definition for a buyer-favorable SPA would:
  - Define MAE as a change, event, or effect that materially and adversely affects the business, assets, financial condition, or results of operations of the Company **taken as a whole**;
  - Carve out (i) general economic or market conditions, (ii) changes in law or GAAP, (iii) the pendency or announcement of the transaction, and (iv) actions taken in contemplation of the transaction;
  - **Not** carve out the Refrigerant Matter, the Kelvara supply risk, or the Nimbara consent risk (these are specific, quantifiable, and known).
- Alternatively, if Seller resists a full definition, at minimum add: "For purposes of this Agreement, 'Material Adverse Effect' does not include the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter, any failure to obtain the Nimbara consent, or any adverse change in the Company's supply agreements."
- **Flag: resolve before signing.** This is a foundational term that affects both the walk right and the indemnification analysis.

## ISSUE 5 – "CLOSING WORKING CAPITAL" IS NOT DEFINED

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 2.3(a)–(c) (Working Capital Adjustment mechanism)
- SPA § 1.1 (Definitions) – "Closing Working Capital" and "Working Capital" do not appear

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The purchase price is adjusted based on the difference between Estimated Closing Working Capital (delivered by Seller pre-closing) and final Closing Working Capital (calculated by Buyer post-closing). But neither "Working Capital" nor "Closing Working Capital" is defined. Key questions unanswered:

- Which balance sheet items are included? (Typically: accounts receivable + inventory + cash − accounts payable − accrued expenses. But is cash included or excluded? Are restricted cash or the DNREC remediation accruals included?)
- Is the $3.9M estimated Refrigerant Matter remediation liability an "accrued expense" that reduces working capital? If so, Seller's estimate will be lower, and Buyer pays less at closing—but then the post-closing true-up could go either way.
- What is the treatment of the Kelvara month-to-month transition (if the price-lock lapses)? If fluid inventory must be built up at higher list prices, working capital could be artificially inflated.

Without a definition, the 90-day post-closing calculation is ripe for dispute, and the "independent accounting firm" mechanism in § 2.3(b) will be the tiebreaker—a costly and time-consuming process.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add a definition in § 1.1 or a new § 2.3(d): "Closing Working Capital" means, as of the Closing Date, the sum of (i) trade accounts receivable (net of reasonable reserves), (ii) inventory (at lower of cost or market), and (iii) cash and cash equivalents, minus (iv) trade accounts payable, (v) accrued expenses (excluding accrued income taxes and the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter remediation liability), and (vi) other current liabilities that are not recurring operating payables.
- Specify that the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter costs are excluded from Working Capital (they are addressed through the § 9.2(d) indemnity).
- **Flag: resolve before signing** to avoid a post-closing dispute that could delay the true-up by months.

## ISSUE 6 – BILL OF SALE GOVERNING LAW AND JURISDICTION MISMATCH

**Documents and Sections:**
- Bill of Sale § 6.1 (New York governing law); § 6.2 (exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts)
- SPA § 11.8 (Delaware governing law; exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware Court of Chancery)
- SPA § 11.11 (order of precedence: "the Bill of Sale shall control with respect to any matter relating to the conveyance of the Contributed Assets")

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The Contributed Assets (six patents/patent applications and all design files for the CIT product lines) are the core IP of the Company's immersion-tank business. Yet disputes over their conveyance are governed by New York law and must be litigated in New York, while all other transaction disputes are in Delaware. This creates:

- **Forum fragmentation.** A single dispute (e.g., whether IP Holdings properly conveyed all design files) could implicate both the Bill of Sale (NY) and the SPA's indemnification provisions (DE), requiring parallel or sequential proceedings in two jurisdictions.
- **Substantive law risk.** New York and Delaware differ on assignment enforceability, implied covenants, and the scope of "further assurances." The Bill of Sale's power-of-attorney provision (§ 4.4) and no-retained-rights representation (§ 5.2) may be construed differently under NY law.
- **Enforcement complexity.** A Delaware judgment on the SPA indemnity and a New York judgment on the Bill of Sale could be inconsistent.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend Bill of Sale §§ 6.1 and 6.2 to conform to the SPA: Delaware governing law and exclusive jurisdiction in the Court of Chancery (or Superior Court).
- If Seller resists (perhaps because IP Holdings has other NY-based operations), at minimum add a provision that the Bill of Sale is subject to the SPA's dispute-resolution mechanism for any claim that also implicates the SPA's indemnification articles.
- This is a lower-priority fix but should be raised in the next draft.

## ISSUE 7 – IP HOLDINGS IS NOT A PARTY TO THE SPA; BUYER LACKS DIRECT PRIVITY

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA Recital B (IP Holdings is an "Affiliate of Seller"); § 4.2 (Seller represents IP Holdings has authority); § 5.4 (Seller shall "cause" IP Holdings to act); § 3.2(b) (Bill of Sale delivered at Closing)
- Bill of Sale (between IP Holdings and the Company only; no Buyer signature block)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
IP Holdings is the entity that actually owns the Contributed Assets and must execute the Bill of Sale. But IP Holdings is not a party to the SPA and has no direct obligations to Buyer. If IP Holdings:
- Fails to execute the Bill of Sale at Closing,
- Conveys defective title (e.g., a patent is subject to a security interest not disclosed), or
- Becomes uncooperative post-closing on further-assurance requests (§ 5.4, Bill of Sale § 4.1),

Buyer's only remedy is an indemnification claim against Seller under § 9.2(a) or (b)—subject to the Cap, the deductible (for rep breaches), and the 12-month survival period. Seller's ability to "cause" IP Holdings to act post-closing is unenforceable by Buyer directly.

**Recommended fix:**
- Require IP Holdings to execute a joinder or third-party agreement acknowledging its obligations under the Bill of Sale and agreeing to be bound by the SPA's indemnification provisions with respect to the Contributed Assets.
- Alternatively, add IP Holdings as a guarantor of Seller's obligations under § 5.4 and the Bill of Sale.
- At minimum, add a specific closing condition in § 6.4 that IP Holdings has delivered a certificate of good standing and an officer's certificate confirming authority and absence of encumbrances on the Contributed Assets.

## ISSUE 8 – ESCROW AMOUNT MAY BE INSUFFICIENT FOR MAXIMUM EXPOSURE

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 2.2(a) (Escrow Amount: $9.2M = 5% of Purchase Price)
- SPA § 9.5(a) (Cap: $18.4M = 10% of Purchase Price)
- SPA § 9.6 (Buyer must first seek recovery from Escrow before going after Seller directly)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The Escrow is the primary source of post-closing recovery. The Cap is $18.4M, but the Escrow holds only $9.2M. If Buyer's aggregate rep/warranty losses (net of the $1.38M deductible) exceed $9.2M, Buyer must pursue Seller directly for the remainder. Risks:

- Seller is an LLC. Post-closing, Seller may distribute the $174.8M balance of the Purchase Price to its members, reducing its ability to satisfy a direct indemnity claim.
- The 12-month survival period means Buyer has limited time to identify and assert claims. If a significant issue surfaces in month 10, there may be insufficient time to litigate before the escrow is released.
- The Scheduled Refrigerant Matter (estimated $3.9M) will likely be the first claim against the escrow, leaving $5.3M for all other rep/warranty breaches.

**Recommended fix:**
- Increase the Escrow Amount to $18.4M (matching the Cap) or, at minimum, to $12M.
- Alternatively, stagger the escrow release: hold $9.2M for 12 months (general reps) and an additional $4.7M for 24 months (environmental and IP-specific reps).
- Add a provision that the escrow agent shall not release any portion of the Escrow to Seller while a claim is pending under § 9.4(d).

## ISSUE 9 – NEWARK LEASE CHANGE-OF-CONTROL CONSENT: TIMELINE AND FINANCIAL STRENGTH TEST

**Documents and Sections:**
- Newark Lease § 10.2 (change of control = assignment requiring consent); § 10.3 (request must be made ≥ 30 days before effective date; Landlord has 15 days to respond); § 10.4(a) (Landlord may withhold consent if surviving tenant "lacks financial strength reasonably sufficient to perform Tenant's obligations")
- SPA § 3.1 (Closing: November 20, 2026); Disclosure Schedule 6.4, Item 1 (landlord consent is a Required Consent)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The consent is properly listed as a Required Consent, so § 6.4(c) protects Buyer. However:

- **Timeline.** The request must be made at least 30 days before November 20, 2026—i.e., by **October 21, 2026**. The SPA is dated October 6, 2026, leaving only 15 days to prepare and submit the request (including financial information about the "proposed transferee" per § 10.3). This is tight.
- **Financial strength test.** § 10.4(a) allows Landlord to withhold consent if the "surviving tenant" lacks financial strength. Because § 10.6 provides that a change of control "does not change the identity of Tenant," the surviving tenant is still the Company. But the Company's balance sheet post-closing will reflect the new ownership. If the Company has significant debt or the working capital is low at closing, Landlord could argue insufficient financial strength.
- **Lease term.** The lease runs to February 28, 2031, with a 5-year extension option exercisable between 12 and 18 months before expiration (i.e., between August 2029 and February 2030). This is not an immediate issue but is relevant to long-term facility planning.

**Recommended fix:**
- Confirm that Seller has already submitted (or will submit by October 21, 2026) the consent request to Ostrander Park, including Buyer's financial information.
- Add to the closing deliverables in § 3.2(c) the requirement that the landlord consent be "unconditional" or, if conditioned, that conditions are limited to standard ones (e.g., a guaranty from Buyer or an increased security deposit).
- If the landlord has not responded by 10 Business Days before the Closing Date, add a mechanism for Buyer to deliver a guaranty or letter of credit to satisfy the financial-strength concern.

## ISSUE 10 – BILL OF SALE: VAGUE STANDARD FOR RETAINED BACKUP COPIES

**Documents and Sections:**
- Bill of Sale § 2.2 (Assignor shall not retain design files "except for copies that Assignor holds in automatic electronic backups in the ordinary course"; shall not "access or use any retained backup copy for any purpose")

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The Contributed Assets include all CAD models, design files, and engineering documentation for the CIT-42, CIT-52, and CIT-60 product lines (Disclosure Schedule 4.12(b), Part 2). These are the technical foundation of the Company's product. The backup exception is problematic because:

- "Automatic electronic backups in the ordinary course" is undefined. Cloud-based backup systems (e.g., AWS, Azure, Veeam) can retain versions for 30, 60, 90, or 365 days. IP Holdings could argue that any backup within its retention window is "in the ordinary course."
- "Shall not access or use" is a negative covenant that is extremely difficult to monitor or verify. There is no audit right, no certification requirement, and no specific remedy for breach beyond the general SPA indemnity.
- If IP Holdings is wound down post-closing (as is common after a sale), its backup systems may be migrated or archived in a way that makes the "no access" covenant meaningless.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend § 2.2 to specify: (i) the maximum retention period for backups (e.g., 30 days); (ii) a requirement that IP Holdings certify in writing, within 30 days after the Effective Date, that all backup copies have been purged or isolated; and (iii) a specific remedy for breach (e.g., liquidated damages or an uncapped indemnity for IP misappropriation).
- Add a post-closing covenant (in the SPA or a side agreement) requiring IP Holdings to confirm in writing that it has ceased all operations related to the Contributed Assets and has destroyed or sequestered all non-backup copies.

## ISSUE 11 – NIMBARA MOST-FAVORED-CUSTOMER PRICING PROVISION

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule 4.10(a), Item 1 (notes that § 7.4 of the Nimbara MSA contains an MFC pricing provision for immersion-tank units)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The MFC provision means that if Starev (post-closing) offers a lower price for immersion-tank units to any other customer, Nimbara can claim the same pricing. This constrains Starev's ability to:

- Discount for volume or strategic customers,
- Enter new market segments at introductory pricing, or
- Match competitor pricing in a bid.

The provision is disclosed, so it is not a rep breach. But its commercial impact on post-closing pricing strategy should be understood before Starev commits $184M.

**Recommended fix:**
- Request the full text of § 7.4 of the Nimbara MSA from the data room and confirm the scope (does it apply to all immersion-tank units or only specific models? Is there a de minimis threshold? Does it expire with the MSA term?).
- If the MFC provision is broad, consider negotiating a specific indemnity or price-adjustment mechanism in the SPA that accounts for the revenue impact if Starev is forced to extend lower pricing to Nimbara.
- This is a commercial rather than legal fix, but should be raised with the client's deal team.

## ISSUE 12 – ENVIRONMENTAL LIABILITY: INTERACTION BETWEEN CONSENT ORDER, LEASE, AND INDEMNITY

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule 4.15, Item 1 (DNREC Consent Order: $3.9M estimated remaining cost; remediation and monitoring through June 30, 2029)
- Newark Lease § 6.2 (Tenant must perform all remediation required by Environmental Laws at its own cost); § 6.3 (Tenant indemnifies Landlord for Hazardous Materials Tenant releases); § 6.6 (Section 6 survives lease termination)
- SPA § 9.2(d) (Seller indemnifies for Scheduled Refrigerant Matter); § 9.4(c) (post-closing, Company controls the matter)
- Newark Lease § 9.1(c) (Tenant must maintain pollution legal liability insurance ≥ $2M per claim)

**Why it matters to Starev:**
The Company (and thus Starev post-closing) is exposed on three fronts:

1. **DNREC Consent Order:** $3.9M in estimated remaining costs through 2029. Seller indemnifies under § 9.2(d), but see Issue 2 (Cap may now apply).
2. **Lease indemnity to Landlord:** If the remediation is incomplete or a further release occurs, Landlord can claim under § 6.3. The lease indemnity is unlimited and survives termination.
3. **Insurance gap:** The required pollution legal liability insurance is $2M per claim. If a single remediation event or further release costs more than $2M, the excess is the Company's (Starev's) problem, recoverable from Seller only to the extent of the § 9.2(d) indemnity (and subject to the Cap if the Side Letter stands).

Additionally, the Consent Order runs through June 2029—nearly three years post-closing. The 12-month survival period for general reps (§ 9.1(a)) will expire in November 2027, well before the Consent Order obligations end. The § 9.2(d) indemnity is a covenant, not a rep, so it survives "until fully performed" (§ 9.1(c))—but only if it is not subject to the Cap (see Issue 2).

**Recommended fix:**
- Confirm that the § 9.2(d) indemnity is not subject to the Cap (see Issue 2 fix).
- Require the Company to maintain pollution legal liability insurance of not less than $5M per claim (above the lease minimum) through the duration of the Consent Order, at Seller's expense for the first 12 months post-closing.
- Add a specific covenant in the SPA requiring Seller to cooperate with and fund (through the § 9.2(d) indemnity) all DNREC interactions through the completion of the Consent Order, including any modifications or extensions DNREC may require.
- Confirm with the client's environmental counsel whether the $3.9M estimate is reasonable or whether there is upside risk (e.g., if groundwater monitoring reveals plume migration beyond the current remediation area).
