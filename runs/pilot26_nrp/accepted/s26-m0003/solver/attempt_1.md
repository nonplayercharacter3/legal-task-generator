## CRITICAL ISSUES – RESOLVE BEFORE SIGNING

### Issue 1: Kelvara Price-Lock Extension Option Notice Deadline Has Already Passed

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.11, Item 3; Side Letter, § 2.1; SPA, § 3.1

**Description.** The Kelvara Fluids Supply Agreement (December 30, 2023) provides that the Company may extend the agreement for 24 months at the Locked Price by delivering written notice to Kelvara "not less than ninety (90) days before the expiration of the Initial Term." The Initial Term expires **December 29, 2026**. Ninety days before that date is **October 1, 2026**. The SPA is dated **October 6, 2026**—five days after the notice deadline.

The Side Letter (§ 2.1) covenants that "Before the Closing, Seller shall cause the Company to exercise the price-lock extension option." If the notice was not given by October 1, 2026, the option may be irrevocably lost. After the Initial Term expires, the agreement continues month-to-month at Kelvara's "then-current list pricing" terminable on 30 days' notice (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.11, Item 3). Given that the Closing is scheduled for November 20, 2026, the Company would enter month-to-month status just six weeks post-Closing, with no price protection for a critical input (single-phase dielectric immersion fluid for the CIT product lines).

**Why it matters.** Dielectric immersion fluid is a core operating input. Loss of the Locked Price exposes the Company to potentially significant cost increases at the most vulnerable moment (immediately post-Closing, when Starev is integrating the business). The month-to-month structure also means Kelvara could terminate on 30 days' notice, creating supply continuity risk.

**Recommendation.**
- **Before signing**, confirm with the client whether the Company already delivered the extension notice to Kelvara on or before October 1, 2026. If it did, request a copy and have Seller confirm in writing.
- If the notice was **not** given, require Seller to (a) obtain Kelvara's written waiver of the 90-day deadline or (b) agree to a specific indemnity for any increase in fluid costs attributable to the lost option, in addition to the Side Letter covenant.
- Add the following as a new closing condition in Section 6.4: *"The Company shall have exercised the price-lock extension option under the Kelvara Fluids Supply Agreement, or Seller shall have delivered to Buyer a written agreement from Kelvara Fluids Corp. confirming that the option remains available for exercise."*
- If the option is truly lost, consider a purchase price adjustment reflecting the present value of the pricing differential over the 24-month extension period.

### Issue 2: Nimbara Cloud Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Schedule 6.4 (Required Consents)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.4, Item 1; Schedule 4.10(a), Item 1; Schedule 6.4; SPA, §§ 6.4(c), 5.5

**Description.** The Nimbara Cloud Master Supply Agreement is the Company's **largest contract**, accounting for **38% of fiscal year 2025 revenue** (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.10(a), Item 1). Section 14.2 of that agreement requires Nimbara's prior written consent to any change of control; absent consent, Nimbara may terminate and claim service credits. This is properly disclosed in Schedule 4.4, Item 1.

However, **Schedule 6.4 (Required Consents) lists only the Ostrander Park (Newark Lease) consent**. The Nimbara consent is absent. Because Section 6.4(c) makes "all Required Consents" a condition to Buyer's closing obligation, and "Required Consents" is defined as "the consents, approvals and notices listed in Schedule 6.4" (SPA, § 1.1), the Nimbara consent is **not a closing condition**. The deal can close without it.

**Why it matters.** If the Closing occurs without Nimbara's consent, Nimbara can terminate the Company's largest revenue contract immediately post-Closing. At 38% of revenue, this would be devastating to the business Starev is acquiring for $184 million. The ordinary course covenant (SPA § 5.1) and the conduct-of-business covenants do not prevent this outcome because Nimbara's right arises from the change of control itself, not from any pre-Closing act by the Company.

**Recommendation.**
- **Add the Nimbara consent to Schedule 6.4** as item 2: *"Consent of Nimbara Cloud Services, Inc. to the change of control of the Company resulting from the sale of the Shares to Buyer, as required by Section 14.2 of the Master Supply Agreement dated March 2, 2023, as amended."*
- This makes it a closing condition under § 6.4(c) and triggers the "reasonable best efforts" obligation in § 5.5.
- In the alternative, if Seller resists adding it as a condition (e.g., because Nimbara has indicated it will not consent), require a specific indemnity from Seller for any termination or service-credit claim by Nimbara arising from the change of control, **uncapped** and surviving for the full term of the Nimbara agreement (through March 1, 2029, plus any renewal terms).
- **Before signing**, request that the client confirm whether any preliminary discussions with Nimbara have taken place and what Nimbara's posture is expected to be.

### Issue 3: Side Letter § 3 Strips Fundamental Representation and Scheduled Refrigerant Matter Exclusions from the Indemnification Cap

**Documents:** Side Letter, § 3; SPA, §§ 9.5(a), 9.5(b), 9.2(a), 9.2(d), 11.11

**Description.** SPA § 9.5(b) provides that the Cap (10% of the Purchase Price = $18.4 million, per § 9.5(a)) does **not** apply to: (i) Fraud, (ii) breach of Fundamental Representations, and (iii) the Section 9.2(d) Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity.

Side Letter § 3 states: *"Seller and Buyer agree that the exclusions from the Cap under Section 9.5(b) of the Agreement are limited solely to Fraud."*

Under the order of precedence (SPA § 11.11), the Side Letter controls. The practical effect is:

| Claim Type | Under SPA § 9.5(b) | Under Side Letter § 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Uncapped | Uncapped |
| Fundamental Rep breach (organization, authority, capitalization, title, brokers) | Uncapped | **Subject to $18.4M Cap** |
| Scheduled Refrigerant Matter (§ 9.2(d)) | Uncapped (expressly excluded) | **Ambiguous – see below** |
| Other rep/warranty breach (§ 9.2(a)) | Subject to $18.4M Cap | Subject to $18.4M Cap (no change) |

**Regarding the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter:** The Cap in § 9.5(a) by its terms applies only to "Losses for which Seller is liable under Section 9.2(a)." The Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity is in § 9.2(d), not § 9.2(a). One could therefore argue that removing the § 9.2(d) reference from § 9.5(b) has no practical effect because the Cap never applied to § 9.2(d) claims in the first place. However, the Side Letter's language is broad enough ("the exclusions from the Cap under Section 9.5(b) … are limited solely to Fraud") to create a colorable argument that the parties intended to subject **all** non-Fraud indemnification to the Cap, including § 9.2(d). This ambiguity is dangerous for the buyer.

**Why it matters.**
- **Fundamental Reps:** If Seller's title to the Shares is defective, or if the capitalization is wrong, Starev's recovery would be capped at $18.4 million (shared with all other non-Fraud rep breaches). In a worst-case scenario where the Shares are not validly issued, the entire $184 million purchase price could be at risk, but recovery would be limited to $18.4 million.
- **Scheduled Refrigerant Matter:** If the ambiguity is resolved against Starev, the $3.9 million in estimated remaining Consent Order costs (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.15, Item 1) would count against the same $18.4 million Cap as all other rep breaches. Any additional environmental liability (e.g., a second release, DNREC-imposed cost overruns) would also be capped.

**Recommendation.**
- **Delete Side Letter § 3 in its entirety.** The SPA's existing structure in § 9.5(b) is appropriate: Fraud, Fundamental Reps, and the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter should all be excluded from the Cap.
- If Seller insists on some modification, the absolute minimum acceptable position is: (a) Fundamental Representations remain excluded from the Cap, and (b) the § 9.2(d) Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity is expressly stated to be **not subject to the Cap in § 9.5(a) or the overall limit in § 9.5(d)**, and to survive for the full duration of the Consent Order obligations (through June 30, 2029, plus any extension).
- If the Side Letter language must remain in some form, add a clarifying sentence: *"For the avoidance of doubt, the indemnification obligation in Section 9.2(d) of the Agreement is not subject to the Cap in Section 9.5(a) or the overall limit in Section 9.5(d), and shall survive until the later of (i) the date on which all obligations under the DNREC Consent Order effective September 8, 2025 have been fully discharged, and (ii) sixty (60) days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations."*

### Issue 4: Scheduled Refrigerant Matter Indemnity Survival Period Is Inadequate

**Documents:** SPA, §§ 9.1(a), 9.1(b), 9.2(d), 4.15; Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.15, Item 1

**Description.** The DNREC Consent Order (effective September 8, 2025) requires the Company to complete all remediation and monitoring through **June 30, 2029** (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.15, Item 1). The expected Closing Date is November 20, 2026 (SPA § 3.1).

Under SPA § 9.1(a), non-Fundamental representations survive for **12 months** after the Closing Date (i.e., until approximately November 20, 2027). Section 9.1(b) provides that Seller's covenants in §§ 9.2(b) through 9.2(d) survive "until the expiration of the survival period of the representation and warranty to which such covenant relates." The § 9.2(d) Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity relates to the § 4.15 (Environmental Matters) representation, which is not a Fundamental Representation and therefore survives only 12 months.

**Result:** The Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity expires approximately **November 20, 2027**, but the Consent Order obligations continue through **June 30, 2029**—a gap of roughly **19 months** during which the Company must perform remediation and monitoring with no indemnification backstop from Seller.

**Why it matters.** The estimated remaining compliance cost is $3.9 million, "most of [which] relates to remediation completion and monitoring to be performed in 2027 through 2029" (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.15, Item 1). If soil remediation encounters unexpected contamination, if DNREC requires additional work, or if monitoring reveals further migration, costs could exceed the estimate. Starev would bear those costs with no recourse to Seller after November 2027.

**Recommendation.**
- Add the following to SPA § 9.1 (or as a new § 9.1(e)): *"Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in this Section 9.1, the indemnification obligation of Seller under Section 9.2(d) (Scheduled Refrigerant Matter) shall survive the Closing until the later of (i) the date on which all obligations of the Company under the DNREC Consent Order effective September 8, 2025 (including all remediation, monitoring, reporting and related obligations) have been fully performed and discharged, and (ii) sixty (60) days after the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations for any claim arising from the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter."*
- Alternatively, add the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter to the list of representations in § 9.1(a) that survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations.

## HIGH-PRIORITY ISSUES

### Issue 5: "Material Adverse Effect" Is Undefined

**Documents:** SPA, §§ 1.1, 4.7, 6.4(e)

**Description.** The term "Material Adverse Effect" is used in § 4.7 (Absence of Certain Changes) and as a closing condition in § 6.4(e). However, it is **not defined** in Section 1.1 or anywhere else in the Agreement.

**Why it matters.** Without a definition, the MAE closing condition is essentially unadministrable. At Closing, Seller could argue that any adverse development is not "material" because the term has no agreed-upon threshold or carve-outs. Conversely, the absence of a definition could be argued to make the condition more protective (any effect that is material in fact), but this invites dispute. In a $184 million transaction, an undefined MAE condition is a significant gap.

**Recommendation.** Add a definition of "Material Adverse Effect" to Section 1.1, using market-standard language. At minimum, the definition should:
- Require the effect to be material to the business, assets, liabilities, or results of operations of the Company taken as a whole;
- Exclude effects arising from changes in general economic or market conditions, changes in law, or the pendency or announcement of the transaction itself;
- Exclude effects that do not exceed a specified dollar threshold (e.g., 2% of the Purchase Price or $3.68 million) in the aggregate;
- Provide that the Scheduled Refrigerant Matter, to the extent disclosed in Schedule 4.15, shall not constitute a Material Adverse Effect (to avoid circularity with the specific indemnity).

### Issue 6: IP Holdings Is Not a Party to the SPA; No Direct Privity with Buyer

**Documents:** SPA, Recital B, §§ 3.2(b), 5.4; Bill of Sale, passim

**Description.** Drasiv IP Holdings, LLC ("IP Holdings") holds the Contributed Assets (three U.S. patents, one U.S. patent application, one European patent validated in four countries, one PCT application, and extensive design files). IP Holdings is not a party to the SPA. Its only obligation is to execute the Bill of Sale, which is a bilateral agreement between IP Holdings and the Company.

If IP Holdings fails to deliver the Contributed Assets, delivers them with defective title, or breaches the Bill of Sale covenants (e.g., retains design files, fails to deliver prosecution files), Buyer's direct contractual recourse is against **Seller** under the SPA indemnification (§ 9.2(a) for breach of § 4.12(b), or § 9.2(b) for breach of § 5.4). But:
- IP Holdings is not bound by the SPA's jurisdiction clause (Delaware, § 11.8);
- The Bill of Sale is governed by **New York law** with exclusive New York jurisdiction (Bill of Sale, §§ 6.1–6.2);
- If Seller is dissolved or undercapitalized post-Closing, enforcement of the indemnity may be difficult.

**Why it matters.** The Contributed Assets are the core intellectual property of the Company's immersion-tank business (the CIT-42, CIT-52, and CIT-60 product lines). If title is defective or if IP Holdings' covenants are breached, Starev's primary remedy is an indemnity claim against Seller, subject to the Cap and (potentially) the Side Letter's narrowing of Cap exclusions.

**Recommendation.**
- Require IP Holdings to execute a **joinder or direct covenant** in favor of Buyer, agreeing to be bound by the SPA's indemnification provisions with respect to the Contributed Assets, and submitting to Delaware jurisdiction.
- In the alternative, add IP Holdings as a guarantor of Seller's indemnification obligations under § 9.2(a) to the extent they arise from the Contributed Assets.
- At minimum, require delivery of an **IP Holdings officer's certificate** at Closing, confirming (a) authority to execute the Bill of Sale, (b) that no licenses or encumbrances have been granted on the Contributed Assets, and (c) that all maintenance fees are current.

### Issue 7: Bill of Sale Governed by New York Law; Conflicting Jurisdiction with SPA

**Documents:** Bill of Sale, §§ 6.1, 6.2; SPA, §§ 11.8, 11.11

**Description.** The Bill of Sale specifies New York governing law and exclusive New York jurisdiction (Bill of Sale, §§ 6.1–6.2). The SPA specifies Delaware governing law and exclusive Delaware jurisdiction (SPA, § 11.8). SPA § 11.11 provides that "the Bill of Sale shall control with respect to any matter relating to the conveyance of the Contributed Assets."

**Why it matters.** A dispute over the Contributed Assets (e.g., whether all design files were delivered, whether IP Holdings retained a prohibited copy, whether a patent assignment was properly recorded) would be litigated in New York under New York law, while a related dispute about the SPA (e.g., whether Seller breached § 5.4) would be in Delaware. This creates the risk of inconsistent rulings, increased litigation costs, and forum complications. New York and Delaware law can differ on issues of contract interpretation, the scope of "further assurances," and the enforceability of powers of attorney coupled with an interest.

**Recommendation.**
- Amend Bill of Sale § 6.1 to provide that the Bill of Sale is governed by Delaware law, consistent with the SPA.
- Amend Bill of Sale § 6.2 to provide for exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware courts identified in SPA § 11.8.
- If Seller resists (perhaps because IP Holdings has a New York nexus), at minimum add a provision to the Bill of Sale stating that, to the extent of any conflict between the Bill of Sale and the SPA, the SPA's dispute-resolution provisions control, and that the parties consent to the Delaware courts having jurisdiction over any claim that arises under both documents.

### Issue 8: Drasiv Health and Welfare Plan – Post-Closing Entanglement

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.14, Item 2; SPA, § 4.14

**Description.** Schedule 4.14, Item 2 identifies the "Drasiv Industrial Group, LLC Health and Welfare Plan, in which Company employees participate." This is a plan maintained by **Seller's parent/affiliate**, not by the Company. Post-Closing, the Company's employees will continue to participate in a plan owned and controlled by Seller.

The SPA contains no covenant requiring Seller to maintain the plan post-Closing, no provision addressing COBRA or plan amendment risk, and no requirement for the Company to establish a replacement plan.

**Why it matters.**
- Seller could amend or terminate the plan post-Closing, disrupting employee benefits and potentially triggering retention issues.
- The Company (now owned by Starev) may have ongoing administrative or funding obligations under the plan that are not clearly allocated.
- Employee relations risk: if benefits are disrupted during the integration period, key engineering personnel may depart.

**Recommendation.**
- Add a covenant in Article V requiring Seller to maintain the Drasiv Health and Welfare Plan on terms no less favorable to Company employees for a specified period post-Closing (e.g., 18 months), or to cooperate in good faith with the Company's establishment of a replacement plan.
- Alternatively, require Seller to assign its obligations under the plan to the Company at Closing, with the Company assuming the plan going forward.
- Request from the client: How many Company employees participate in this plan? What are the plan's terms regarding employer contributions? Is there a provision allowing the plan sponsor to terminate or amend?

## MODERATE-PRIORITY ISSUES

### Issue 9: No Defined Target Working Capital

**Documents:** SPA, §§ 2.2(b), 2.3

**Description.** Section 2.3 provides for a working capital adjustment between the Estimated Closing Working Capital (delivered pre-Closing) and the final Closing Working Capital (determined post-Closing). However, the Agreement never defines "Working Capital" (e.g., current assets minus current liabilities, with or without specific exclusions) and does not specify a **Target Working Capital** level.

**Why it matters.** Without a defined target, the Purchase Price effectively adjusts to whatever Working Capital the Company happens to have at Closing. Seller could argue that a low Working Capital level is acceptable if it matches the estimate. The ordinary course covenant (§ 5.1) provides some protection, but it is a soft standard. In practice, the absence of a target makes the adjustment mechanism less protective of Buyer.

**Recommendation.**
- Add a definition of "Working Capital" to Section 1.1 (e.g., "current assets minus current liabilities, excluding [specify exclusions such as deferred tax assets, non-recurring items, etc.]").
- Specify a **Target Working Capital** dollar amount in Section 2.3, against which the adjustment is measured. The target should be based on the Company's trailing 12-month average or a negotiated figure.
- If Seller resists a fixed target, at minimum define Working Capital with agreed exclusions so that the estimate-to-actual comparison is meaningful.

As of the date of this Agreement, each such person is employed by the Company, and to Seller's Knowledge, no such person has given notice of resignation or is likely to resign before the Closing Date."*

### Issue 12: Outside Date (January 15, 2027) May Be Inadequate Given Open Items

**Documents:** SPA, § 10.1(b)

**Description.** The outside date is January 15, 2027—approximately two months after the scheduled Closing Date of November 20, 2026. **Why it matters.** If the outside date passes, either party can terminate (unless the non-breaching party caused the delay). In a market where the Company's value may fluctuate, the ability of Seller to walk at January 15, 2027 creates leverage asymmetry.

Alternatively, provide that the outside date is automatically extended if the delay is caused by a third party's failure to respond to a consent request made in a timely manner.

### Issue 14: Bill of Sale § 2.2 – Design File Retention Covenant Is Difficult to Enforce

**Documents:** Bill of Sale, § 2.2

**Description.** Bill of Sale § 2.2 requires IP Holdings to deliver all design files at Closing and prohibits it from retaining copies (other than automatic backups) or accessing/using any retained backup "for any purpose."

- Add a post-Closing audit right: For 24 months after the Effective Date, the Company may, on reasonable notice, require IP Holdings to certify in writing that it has not accessed or used any retained backup copy.
- Consider requiring IP Holdings to **delete** all non-backup copies at Closing and to deliver a certificate of deletion.

### Issue 19: Newark Lease – Hazardous Materials Indemnity and Insurance Interaction

**Documents:** Newark Lease, §§ 6.2, 6.3, 9.1(c)

**Description.** The Newark Lease requires Tenant to maintain pollution legal liability insurance of not less than $2,000,000 per claim (§ 9.1(c)) and provides that Tenant must indemnify Landlord for all Hazardous Materials brought onto or released at the Project (§ 6.3). The DNREC Consent Order remediation is occurring at the Test Facility.

**Why it matters.** If the ongoing soil remediation or any future release triggers a claim by Landlord (e.g., for damage to common areas or for Landlord's defense costs), the Tenant indemnity in § 6.3 is broad and not subject to a negligence standard. The $2 million pollution liability policy may be insufficient if remediation costs escalate. Additionally, the SPA's § 9.5(e) provides that Losses are calculated "net of any insurance proceeds actually received"—meaning if the pollution policy pays, the indemnity from Seller is reduced by that amount.

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the client's insurance broker that the Company's current pollution legal liability policy has adequate limits for the ongoing Consent Order remediation and that the policy will remain in effect post-Closing.
- If the policy limit is $2 million and the estimated remaining remediation cost is $3.9 million, consider requiring Seller to maintain (or the Company to obtain) additional environmental impairment liability coverage as a condition to Closing.
- Clarify in the SPA that the § 9.5(e) insurance offset does not apply to the § 9.2(d) Scheduled Refrigerant Matter indemnity (i.e., Starev should be able to recover from Seller the full amount of Losses without netting insurance proceeds, or at minimum, the insurance proceeds should be credited to Starev, not reduce the indemnity).

### Issue 20: Section 5.5 – "Reasonable Best Efforts" Standard for Required Consents

**Documents:** SPA, § 5.5

**Description.** Section 5.5 requires Seller to use "reasonable best efforts" to obtain Required Consents. Under Delaware law, "reasonable best efforts" is a high standard (higher than "commercially reasonable efforts"), but it is not an absolute obligation. Seller could argue that it used reasonable best efforts even if a consent is not obtained, so long as it took meaningful steps.

**Why it matters.** If the Nimbara consent is added to Schedule 6.4 (per Issue 2), Seller's obligation would be to use "reasonable best efforts" to obtain it. If Nimbara unreasonably withholds consent, Seller might not be in breach, yet the closing condition in § 6.4(c) would not be satisfied. This could create a standoff.

**Recommendation.**
- Add to § 5.5: *"If any Required Consent is withheld or conditioned by the relevant third party, Seller shall use reasonable best efforts to negotiate in good faith to obtain the consent or to secure a waiver or modification that is reasonably satisfactory to Buyer. If Seller is unable to obtain a Required Consent despite such efforts, Seller shall promptly notify Buyer and the parties shall meet to discuss in good faith the appropriate resolution, which may include a purchase price adjustment, a specific indemnity, or termination of this Agreement."*
- Alternatively, for the Nimbara consent specifically, consider making it a condition that can be **waived by Buyer only** (not by Seller), with a requirement that Seller provide a detailed status report if the consent has not been obtained 15 days before the scheduled Closing.

### Issue 22: Section 4.12(c) – IP Non-Infringement Representation Is Knowledge-Qualified

**Documents:** SPA, § 4.12(c)

**Description.** Section 4.12(c) provides: *"To Seller's Knowledge, the conduct of the Company's business does not infringe, misappropriate or otherwise violate the intellectual property rights of any Person."* This is qualified by "To Seller's Knowledge," and "Knowledge" is not defined in the Agreement.

**Why it matters.** In an IP-intensive business (immersion cooling patents, design files, engineering documentation), the risk of third-party IP claims is real. A knowledge qualifier means Seller is not liable if it genuinely did not know of an infringement, even if a reasonable investigation would have revealed it. The undefined "Knowledge" term adds further ambiguity (is it actual knowledge of a named individual? Knowledge after reasonable inquiry?).

**Recommendation.**
- Define "Knowledge" in Section 1.1: *"'Knowledge' means, with respect to Seller, the actual knowledge of the officers of the Company listed on Schedule [___], after reasonable inquiry of the Company's engineering and legal personnel."*
- Consider upgrading § 4.12(c) to an unqualified representation for the specific Contributed Assets: *"The Contributed Assets do not infringe, misappropriate or otherwise violate the intellectual property rights of any Person."* (This is a narrower, more defensible rep than a blanket statement about all business conduct.)

### Issue 23: No Tax Representation Regarding Transfer Tax Allocation

**Documents:** SPA, § 8.2

**Description.** Section 8.2 provides that transfer, documentary, sales, use and similar taxes are split 50/50 between Seller and Buyer. For a stock purchase, transfer taxes are generally minimal (Delaware does not impose a stock transfer tax on domestic corporations), but if any states impose such taxes, the 50/50 split could be unfavorable if the tax base is the full Purchase Price.

**Why it matters.** This is a lower-risk item, but worth confirming. In most stock purchases, transfer taxes are de minimis. However, if the transaction structure is modified (e.g., an asset purchase for some entities), the tax implications could change.

**Recommendation.** Confirm with the tax team that the 50/50 split is appropriate for a stock purchase of a Delaware corporation. If there are any state transfer taxes that could apply, consider shifting them entirely to Seller (as is market for stock purchases).

### Issue 24: Section 11.6 (No Third-Party Beneficiaries) – Company Is Not a Third-Party Beneficiary

**Documents:** SPA, § 11.6

**Description.** Section 11.6 provides: *"Except for the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX, this Agreement is for the sole benefit of the parties and their permitted assigns."* The "Buyer Indemnitees" are defined in § 9.2 as "Buyer, the Company and their respective Affiliates and representatives."

**Why it matters.** The Company (Coldline) is a Buyer Indemnitee and can benefit from the indemnification provisions. However, the Company is **not** a third-party beneficiary of the covenants in Article V (e.g., the conduct-of-business covenants, the access rights, the Required Consents covenant). If Seller breaches § 5.1 (conduct of business), only Buyer can enforce the remedy. The Company itself cannot sue Seller for pre-Closing breaches of covenant.

This is generally acceptable in a stock purchase (the Company is Seller's subsidiary pre-Closing and Buyer's subsidiary post-Closing, and Buyer controls the Company). However, it means that if Buyer fails to act on a covenant breach, the Company has no independent right to do so.

**Recommendation.** This is a lower-priority item. If the client wants additional protection, add the Company as a third-party beneficiary of Article V covenants, with the right to enforce them directly. However, this is not market-standard in a stock purchase and Seller may resist.
