## Issue 1 – Non-Renewal Notice Is Likely Untimely, Locking Praidein Into a 24-Month Renewal

**Where:** Agreement § 12.2; Side Letter Recital C and §§ 4.1–4.3; Order Form § 1.2.

**Analysis:** The Initial Subscription Term is 36 months from 1 February 2024, ending **31 January 2027**. Section 12.2 requires non-renewal notice "at least one hundred twenty (120) days before the end of the then-current Subscription Term." Counting back 120 days from 31 January 2027 gives a deadline of approximately **2–3 October 2026**. The Side Letter confirms the notice was delivered on **14 October 2026** (Recital C; § 4.1)—eleven to twelve days late. Licensor has expressly stated its position that the notice is "untimely and ineffective" (Side Letter § 4.2). Both parties reserved rights (§ 4.3).

If the notice is ineffective, the Subscription Term auto-renews for **24 months** (to approximately 31 January 2029) under § 12.2. This is the single most consequential issue in the file: it determines whether Praidein is free at the end of January 2027 or locked in for two more years.

**Why it matters:** A 24-month renewal at current fees ($175,200 base + $58,200 Cleveland = $233,400/year) represents approximately $466,800 in committed spend. More importantly, it interacts with the Change of Control issue (Issue 2) and limits Praidein's negotiating leverage with Ostrander.

**Recommended fix:**
- Treat the timeliness question as a live dispute. The 11-day gap is not trivial, but arguments exist: (a) whether the 120-day period runs from the last day of the term or the day before; (b) whether delivery under § 16.1 (effective "on receipt") means the date Licensor actually received the notice rather than the date it was sent; (c) whether Licensor's conduct—executing a Side Letter that adds a new site and acknowledges the notice—constitutes a waiver or estoppel, particularly given that § 16.4 requires waivers to be in writing.
- **Urgently** seek a written resolution with Licensor. If the parties cannot agree, consider whether the Side Letter's execution (which both parties signed on 21 October 2026, *after* the disputed notice) can be characterized as an implied acceptance of the non-renewal position. This is a weak argument given the express reservation of rights in § 4.3, but it is not without force.
- If the notice is held ineffective, negotiate a buyout or early-termination right as part of the Ostrander merger (see Issue 2).

**Information needed from client:** The exact date and method of delivery of the 14 October 2026 notice (courier tracking, email timestamp, etc.), and whether any communication with Zousen before or after that date referenced the renewal deadline.

## Issue 2 – Change of Control (Ostrander Merger) Requires Licensor Consent; Failure Triggers Fee Acceleration

**Where:** Agreement § 15.3; Side Letter Recital B.

**Analysis:** Section 15.3 provides that any Change of Control of Licensee "shall be deemed an assignment of this Agreement by Licensee requiring Licensor's prior written consent under Section 15.1." The Side Letter confirms that on 11 September 2026, Praidein entered into a Merger Agreement under which Ostrander will acquire 100% of Praidein's equity. There is **no evidence in the file** that Licensor has consented.

If the merger closes without consent, Licensor may terminate on 30 days' notice, and **all Fees for the remainder of the Subscription Term accelerate and become immediately due** (§ 15.3, final sentence).

**Why it matters:** This gives Licensor enormous leverage over the Ostrander transaction. If the non-renewal notice is ineffective (Issue 1), the "remainder of the Subscription Term" is 24 months, meaning approximately $466,800 in accelerated fees. Even if the non-renewal is effective, there is a question of whether the acceleration clause applies to the remaining portion of the Initial Term (approximately 10 weeks of fees from a late-2026 closing to 31 January 2027). In either scenario, this is a material financial exposure that Ostrander will want to understand before closing.

Additionally, the deemed-assignment structure means Licensor can consent or withhold consent at its discretion (subject only to the "not unreasonably withheld" standard in § 15.1), giving it a de facto veto over the merger.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Before the Ostrander merger closes**, obtain Licensor's written consent to the Change of Control. This should be a condition precedent in the Merger Agreement (if not already one—please confirm).
- In the consent negotiation, seek: (a) confirmation that no fee acceleration will apply; (b) if the non-renewal dispute remains unresolved, a agreed resolution (e.g., Licensor accepts the non-renewal in exchange for a modest break fee or a shortened transition period); (c) clarification that the post-closing entity (Ostrander or its subsidiary) will be the recognized Licensee.
- If Licensor withholds consent unreasonably, argue that the "not unreasonably withheld" standard in § 15.1 is breached, particularly if the non-renewal notice is ultimately held effective (in which case the agreement is ending anyway and consent is moot).

**Information needed from client:** (a) Whether the Merger Agreement contains a condition precedent regarding Zousen consent; (b) the expected closing date; (c) whether any discussions have taken place with Zousen regarding the merger; (d) the identity of the post-closing entity that will hold the Praidein equity.

## Issue 3 – "Authorized User" Definition Conflict Excludes Agency Workers from the License

**Where:** Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Authorized User"); Order Form § 2.1 (definition of "Authorized User"); Agreement § 1.3 (Order of Precedence); Order Form § 2.4 (Overage); Agreement Recital B.

**Analysis:** The Agreement defines "Authorized User" as "employees, **contractors and agency workers** of Licensee and its Affiliates who are permitted by Licensee to access and use the Software" (§ 1.1). The Order Form narrows this to "a **named employee** of Licensee who works at an Authorized Site, who has been issued a unique user credential… and whom Licensee has registered in the Software's user administration console" (§ 2.1). Under the Order of Precedence (§ 1.3), the Order Form controls.

Recital B of the Agreement expressly acknowledges that "Licensee staffs its flight kitchens **largely with agency galley crews**." If those agency crews use the Software (e.g., for tray-assembly or meal-load functions), they are **not** Authorized Users under the Order Form. Each such user triggers the overage charge of **$145 per individual per month** (Order Form § 2.4).

**Why it matters:** This is a structural mismatch between the license and Praidein's operating model. If, for example, 60 agency workers across the three (soon four) sites access the Software, the overage exposure is $60 × $145 × 12 = **$104,400 per year**—nearly 60% of the base subscription fee. This is an uncontrolled, potentially large cost that Praidein may not have budgeted for. It also creates compliance risk: if Zousen audits (permitted quarterly under Order Form § 2.4) and identifies agency workers in the system, it can invoice retroactively.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate a correction to Order Form § 2.1 to align with the Agreement's broader definition, or at minimum to include "agency workers" and "contractors" who are registered in the user administration console. The practical fix is to allow registration of agency workers as Authorized Users within the 240-seat Subscription Quantity, rather than treating each as an overage.
- In the interim, implement internal controls: ensure only named employees with registered credentials can access the Software, and that agency workers do not log in. This is operationally difficult given that agency crews "work alongside Licensee's employees" (Recital B) and likely need system access for tray assembly.
- If the 240-seat cap is insufficient once agency workers are properly included, negotiate an increase in Subscription Quantity at a fixed per-seat rate rather than the $145/month overage.

**Information needed from client:** (a) How many agency workers currently access or need access to the Software at each site; (b) whether any overage invoices have been received to date; (c) whether the user administration console currently shows any unregistered users.

## Issue 4 – One-Sided Assignment Right in Favor of Licensor

**Where:** Order Form § 7; Agreement § 15.1; Agreement § 1.3 (Order of Precedence).

**Analysis:** Agreement § 15.1 provides that "neither party may assign" without the other's consent. Order Form § 7 overrides this for Licensor: "Licensor may assign or transfer this Order Form and the Agreement, in whole or in part… **without Licensee's consent**, to any Affiliate or to any acquirer of all or part of its business or assets." Under the Order of Precedence (§ 1.3), the Order Form prevails.

**Why it matters:** Zousen can sell or transfer the contract to a third party—potentially a competitor of Praidein or an entity with weaker data-protection practices—without Praidein's approval. While § 15.2 provides that the Agreement "binds and benefits… permitted successors and assigns" (so confidentiality and data-protection obligations should carry over), this is a weaker protection than a consent right. For a business whose Licensee Data includes "airline-contract pricing, meal-load plans, production volumes" (Agreement § 1.1), the identity of the counterparty matters.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation (whether driven by the non-renewal dispute, the CoC consent, or a fee adjustment), seek to delete or narrow Order Form § 7. At minimum, require that any assignee be a non-competitor and that Praidein receive 30 days' prior notice (rather than the current 30 days *after* the assignment takes effect).
- Confirm that the assignee executes a joinder or assumption agreement binding it to all data-protection and confidentiality obligations.

## Issue 5 – No Service Credit or Financial Remedy for Availability Failures

**Where:** Agreement § 3.2 (Availability); § 7.2 (Software warranty); § 10.1 (Exclusion of consequential damages).

**Analysis:** Section 3.2 commits Licensor to 99.5% monthly availability. However, the Agreement contains **no service credit, fee reduction, or other financial remedy** for failure to meet this SLA. The only potential remedy is the warranty in § 7.2 (Software must "perform materially in accordance with the Documentation"), which requires a 30-day cure period before Licensee can terminate and obtain a refund. Section 10.1 excludes all consequential damages, which would encompass lost revenue from missed flight loads.

**Why it matters:** For an airline caterer, a software outage during a loading window can mean missed flight loads, contractual penalties from airline customers, and wasted labor. The 99.5% SLA permits up to ~3.6 hours of downtime per month, and there is no financial consequence if Zousen fails to meet even that threshold. Praidein bears all the operational risk of downtime with no recourse short of termination (which requires a material breach and a 30-day cure period).

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate a service credit schedule (e.g., 5% of monthly fees for each 0.1% below 99.5%, capped at 30% of monthly fees).
- Clarify that repeated or prolonged outages (e.g., >4 hours in a single event) constitute a material breach triggering termination rights under § 13.1 without the full 30-day cure period.
- At minimum, document the airline contractual penalties Praidein faces for missed loads so that the business case for a service credit is concrete.

**Information needed from client:** (a) Any history of downtime or SLA failures to date; (b) the magnitude of penalties Praidein faces under its airline customer contracts for missed or late loads.

## Issue 6 – Support Terms Depend on an External "Standard Support Policy"

**Where:** Agreement § 3.3.

**Analysis:** Section 3.3 provides 24/7 support for Severity 1 issues but only "business hours" support for all other issues "in accordance with its standard support policy." That policy is not attached to or incorporated in the Agreement. Licensor can presumably update it unilaterally.

**Why it matters:** The scope, response times, and escalation paths for non-critical issues are undefined. If Zousen changes its support policy to, say, business hours only Monday–Friday 09:00–17:00 Eastern, Praidein has no contractual recourse. For a 24/7 airline catering operation, this creates uncertainty about when configuration issues, data errors, or integration problems will be addressed.

**Recommended fix:**
- Request a copy of Zousen's current standard support policy and have it attached as an exhibit to the Order Form or a Side Letter, with a provision that material changes require Praidein's consent.
- Define "business hours" in the Agreement (e.g., 07:00–19:00 local time at the affected Authorized Site, Monday–Saturday).

**Information needed from client:** A copy of Zousen's current standard support policy, if one has been provided.

## Issue 7 – Data Return Window (60 Days) May Be Insufficient for Transition

**Where:** Agreement § 5.4.

**Analysis:** On termination or expiry, Licensor must make Licensee Data available for export for **60 days**, then delete it "subject to its standard backup retention cycle." The data includes menu specifications, recipes, airline-contract pricing, meal-load plans, production volumes, and galley configurations (§ 1.1).

**Why it matters:** If the agreement ends (whether by effective non-renewal, CoC termination, or for-cause termination), Praidein has only 60 days to export and migrate all operational data. For a business being acquired by Ostrander, this window may need to align with the merger integration timeline. The "standard backup retention cycle" carve-out is vague and could mean Zousen retains (and potentially uses) the data longer than 60 days.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate an extension of the export window to 90 or 120 days, particularly if a CoC termination is a realistic scenario.
- Delete or clarify the "standard backup retention cycle" language: specify that backups will be purged within a defined period (e.g., 30 days after the 60-day export window closes) and that no data will be used for any purpose after deletion.
- Confirm the export format is truly usable (e.g., CSV, JSON, or a format compatible with Praidein's or Ostrander's replacement system).

## Issue 8 – Cleveland Site Added to Zousen but Not to Lorvex Refrigeration Coverage

**Where:** Side Letter § 2.1 (Cleveland site added effective 1 November 2026); Lorvex Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Service Locations"); Lorvex Schedule A.

**Analysis:** The Side Letter adds the Cleveland (CLE) flight kitchen as an Authorized Site under the Zousen agreement effective 1 November 2026. The Lorvex Fleet Refrigeration Maintenance Agreement defines "Service Locations" as "Customer's flight kitchens at Pittsburgh (PIT), Columbus (CMH) and Buffalo (BUF), and any other location the parties agree in writing" (§ 1.1). Schedule A lists vehicles based at PIT, CMH, and BUF only. There is no Cleveland coverage.

**Why it matters:** If the Cleveland kitchen is operational (and the Side Letter contemplates configuration, data import, and training within 30 days of 1 November 2026), it will need refrigerated transport for chilled meals and galley equipment. Without Lorvex coverage, Praidein either has no maintenance contract for Cleveland-based vehicles or is relying on an informal arrangement. This is an operational gap that could create food-safety and regulatory exposure.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend the Lorvex agreement (or add a schedule) to include Cleveland as a Service Location and to add any Cleveland-based vehicles to Schedule A.
- Confirm with the client whether Cleveland vehicles already exist or are being acquired as part of the site launch, and whether any interim maintenance arrangement is in place.

**Information needed from client:** (a) Whether the Cleveland kitchen is operational as of the date of this memo; (b) whether refrigerated vehicles are based at or will be based at Cleveland; (c) whether any maintenance arrangement exists for those vehicles.

## Issue 9 – Lorvex Agreement Term Expires Before Potential Zousen Renewal Period

**Where:** Lorvex Agreement § 10.1 (Initial Term: 24 months from 3 March 2025 = ends 2 March 2027); § 10.2 (Renewal by mutual written agreement only); Agreement § 12.2 (Zousen auto-renewal for 24 months if non-renewal notice ineffective).

**Analysis:** If the Zousen non-renewal notice is held ineffective (Issue 1), the Zousen Subscription Term renews to approximately 31 January 2029. The Lorvex agreement, however, ends on 2 March 2027 and does **not** auto-renew—it requires mutual written agreement (§ 10.2). This creates a potential gap: Praidein could be committed to Zousen software for two more years while having no refrigeration maintenance contract after March 2027.

**Why it matters:** While this is not a Zousen contract issue per se, it affects Praidein's overall operational planning and negotiating position. If Lorvex leverage is reduced by the absence of a competing maintenance provider, Praidein may face higher renewal costs. Conversely, if Praidein is locked into Zousen through 2029 but loses Lorvex, it may need to source alternative refrigeration maintenance on short notice.

**Recommended fix:**
- Begin Lorvex renewal negotiations well before 2 March 2027. The Lorvex agreement is more favorable to Praidein in several respects (termination for convenience on 60 days' notice under § 10.3; assignment to successors without consent under § 11.2), so there is less urgency, but the Cleveland gap (Issue 8) should be addressed in the same negotiation.
- Consider whether the Ostrander merger affects Lorvex's willingness to renew (Lorvex § 11.2 permits assignment to successors without consent, so the merger should not disrupt the Lorvex relationship).

## Issue 10 – Liability Cap Is Low Relative to Operational Risk

**Where:** Agreement § 10.2 (Cap: Fees paid/payable in preceding 12 months); § 10.1 (Exclusion of indirect/consequential damages).

**Analysis:** The aggregate liability cap is the Fees paid or payable in the 12 months preceding the claim. At current run-rate, that is approximately $175,200 (base) or $233,400 (including Cleveland). Combined with the exclusion of consequential damages (§ 10.1), Praidein's maximum recovery for a serious software failure, data breach, or security incident is limited to roughly $233,400—before the exceptions in § 10.3 (which cover indemnification, confidentiality breach, data security breach under § 5.3, payment obligations, and gross negligence/willful misconduct/fraud).

**Why it matters:** A data breach involving airline-contract pricing or a prolonged software outage during peak season could cause damages far exceeding the cap. The cap is standard in SaaS agreements but is worth noting in the context of the Ostrander merger, where the acquirer will be assessing Praidein's vendor risk profile.

**Recommended fix:**
- The § 10.3 exceptions already carve out data security breaches (§ 5.3) and confidentiality breaches (§ 9) from the cap, which is helpful. Confirm that a failure to meet the 72-hour breach notification obligation in § 5.3 would qualify as a breach of § 5.3 (and thus fall outside the cap).
- For any renegotiation, seek to raise the cap to 2× or 3× annual fees, or to add a super-cap for data security incidents.
- Ensure Praidein's own cyber insurance (if any) covers the gap between the Zousen cap and actual potential losses.

**Information needed from client:** Whether Praidein carries cyber liability insurance and what the coverage limits are.

## Issue 11 – Side Letter Execution Could Undermine Non-Renewal Position

**Where:** Side Letter §§ 2, 3, 4.3; Agreement § 12.2.

**Analysis:** The Side Letter (dated 21 October 2026) adds the Cleveland site, provides for implementation services, and sets a fee of $58,200/year—while simultaneously recording that the non-renewal notice was delivered 7 days earlier (14 October 2026) and that Licensor disputes its timeliness. By executing the Side Letter and agreeing to pay for a new site, Praidein is taking actions consistent with continuing the relationship. Licensor could argue (though § 4.3 expressly disclaims any waiver) that Praidein's conduct is inconsistent with a bona fide intent not to renew, potentially supporting an estoppel or course-of-dealing argument.

**Why it matters:** This does not change the black-letter law (the notice was late), but it weakens Praidein's equitable position if the dispute is litigated or arbitrated. It also signals to Ostrander (and to the market) that Praidein is expanding its Zousen footprint rather than exiting.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any settlement of the non-renewal dispute, make clear that the Cleveland site addition was a business decision independent of the renewal question (e.g., the Cleveland kitchen needed the software regardless of the contract term).
- If the dispute proceeds to litigation, be prepared for Licensor to raise the Side Letter as evidence of Praidein's intent to continue. The express reservation of rights in § 4.3 is the primary defense.

## Issue 12 – No Insurance Requirement for Licensor to Name Licensee as Additional Insured

**Where:** Agreement § 11; compare Lorvex Agreement § 9.

**Analysis:** Section 11 requires Licensor to maintain $2M CGL and $5M tech E&O/cyber insurance, and to provide certificates on request. It does **not** require Licensor to name Licensee as an additional insured. By contrast, the Lorvex agreement (§ 9) requires the provider to name Customer as an additional insured on CGL and auto liability policies.

**Why it matters:** If a Zousen data breach or software failure causes a third-party claim (e.g., an airline customer sues Praidein for spoiled meals due to a software error in meal-load forecasting), Praidein would need to rely on Zousen's indemnity (§ 8.1) rather than being directly covered under Zousen's insurance. The indemnity is subject to the liability cap (though § 10.3 carves out indemnification obligations from the cap), and enforcement may be slower and more costly than a direct insurance claim.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation, add a requirement that Licensor name Licensee (and its Affiliates) as additional insureds on the CGL and tech E&O policies, or at minimum provide a direct payment clause in the insurance policy for claims arising out of Licensor's breach.
