## Executive Summary

Review of all nine documents in the matter folder identifies **four critical issues**, **four high-priority issues**, and **five moderate-to-low issues** that should be addressed before Brukaisk signs. The most significant problems are (1) a 90-day survival period for the Installed Systems representation that is grossly misaligned with the 10- to 25-year customer warranties Buyer is assuming, (2) a Side Letter provision that channels the DOL installer-classification exposure through the capped and basketed representation-breach remedy instead of the uncapped Excluded Liability remedy, (3) a Transition Services Agreement in which Buyer indemnifies Seller for Seller's own ordinary negligence, and (4) a Key Employee Agreement that defines "Business" far more narrowly than the APA, gutting Renfeld's non-compete.

## CRITICAL ISSUES

### 1. Specified Warranty Period of 90 Days vs. 10–25 Year Customer Warranties

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 9.1(b): Section 4.9 representations survive "until the expiration of the Specified Warranty Period."
- APA § 1.1: "Specified Warranty Period" has "the meaning given in Schedule 4.9."
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Part A: defines "Specified Warranty Period" as "the period beginning on the Closing Date and ending **ninety (90) days** after the Closing Date."
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8, Part A: residential workmanship warranties run **10 years** (Form RSP-2019) or **25 years** (Forms RSP-2022, RSS-2022); roof-penetration warranties run **10 years**.
- APA § 2.3(b): Buyer assumes post-closing liabilities under Assigned Contracts (i.e., the customer warranties).
- APA § 4.9(c): Seller represents no Installed System has "any roof-penetration, flashing, leak or water-intrusion defect, or any structural or load-bearing defect, attributable to its design or installation."

**Why It Matters:**
Buyer is stepping into 6,842 residential and 87 commercial customer contracts carrying 10- to 25-year workmanship and roof-penetration warranties. If a latent installation defect (e.g., a roof leak) manifests in year 2 or year 5, Buyer must honor the warranty to the customer but has **no indemnification recourse against Seller** because the Section 4.9 representation expired 90 days after closing. The 90-day period was evidently intended as a transition window for Seller to administer *already-submitted* claims (per the Schedule 4.9 Part A language), but it has been repurposed as the survival period for the entire Section 4.9 representation package, including the forward-looking warranty that no defects exist.

The Disclosure Schedule itself reveals the risk is not hypothetical: 112 roof-leak reports were received from 2023–2026, 71 were attributable to the system (all repaired), 38 claims remain open, and 23 systems lack final inspection sign-off.

**Recommended Fix:**
Decouple the survival period from the transition-administration period. Amend APA § 9.1(b) to provide that Section 4.9(a)–(c) survive for a period matching the longest customer warranty (25 years), or at minimum 10 years, consistent with the roof-penetration and workmanship warranty terms. Alternatively, add a specific indemnity (outside Article IX) for pre-closing installation defects that is not subject to the general survival period. The 90-day "Specified Warranty Period" can remain as the window during which Seller administers pre-closing-submitted claims, but it should not cap the survival of the underlying representation.

### 2. Side Letter Channels DOL Exposure Through Capped/Basketed Remedy

**Documents and Sections:**
- Side Letter § 2: "any Losses incurred by any Buyer Indemnitee arising out of or relating to the Installer Classification Matter shall be recoverable by Buyer under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement **solely** as Losses arising from a breach of the representations and warranties of Seller in Section 4.13(c)."
- APA § 9.5(a): cap of 15% of Purchase Price = **$5,775,000**.
- APA § 9.5(b): basket of **$385,000** (first-dollar once exceeded).
- APA § 9.5(c): cap and basket do **not** apply to claims under § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities).
- APA § 2.4(d): Excluded Liabilities expressly include "any classification of any individual as an independent contractor rather than an employee."
- APA § 1.1: "Fundamental Representations" include § 4.14 (Taxes) but **not** § 4.13.
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.13(c), item 1: DOL audit opened May 2026; 137 individuals engaged as independent contractors since January 2023; no findings issued.

**Why It Matters:**
The Installer Classification Matter is, by the APA's own terms, an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(d). Absent the Side Letter, Buyer could recover under § 9.2(c) (breach relating to an Excluded Liability), which is **exempt from both the cap and the basket** per § 9.5(c). The Side Letter's "solely" language strips that route and forces all recovery through § 9.2(a) (representation breach), subjecting it to the $5.775M cap and the $385K basket.

The exposure is potentially severe: 137 workers × 3+ years of misclassification could generate back wages, overtime, payroll taxes (FICA, FUTA, state), penalties, and liquidated damages well in excess of $5.775M. Moreover, because § 4.13 is not a Fundamental Representation, the survival period is only 18 months (APA § 9.1). The DOL audit was opened in May 2026 with no findings yet; if the assessment is not issued and noticed within 18 months of closing, the claim may be time-barred.

Additionally, had the matter been routed through § 4.14 (Taxes—a Fundamental Representation), it would carry unlimited survival (60 days past the statute of limitations) and no cap or basket. The Side Letter forecloses even this argument.

**Recommended Fix:**
Amend Side Letter § 2 to delete "solely" and add: "Without limiting the foregoing, Losses arising from the Installer Classification Matter that constitute Excluded Liabilities under Section 2.4(d) of the Purchase Agreement shall also be recoverable under Section 9.2(c) of the Purchase Agreement, and the limitations in Section 9.5(a) and (b) shall not apply to such Losses." Alternatively, negotiate a specific indemnity for the DOL matter outside Article IX with its own (longer) survival period tied to the resolution of the audit.

### 3. Transition Services Agreement: Buyer Indemnifies Seller for Seller's Own Negligence

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 7.1: "Recipient shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless Provider... from and against any and all Losses arising out of or relating to the Services... **including Losses arising from Provider's negligence in performing the Services**, except to the extent such Losses result from Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct."
- TSA § 7.2: "Provider shall indemnify... Recipient... from and against any and all Losses **to the extent arising out of Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct** in performing the Services."
- TSA § 8.3: the liability cap does not apply to "Recipient's obligations under Section 7.1."

**Why It Matters:**
This is an inverted indemnity structure. Buyer (Recipient) is indemnifying Seller (Provider) for Seller's own ordinary negligence in performing the transition services, while Seller's indemnity to Buyer is limited to gross negligence or willful misconduct. The cap exception in § 8.3 means Buyer's indemnity obligation to Seller is **uncapped**.

The services at risk include Monitoring Platform hosting ($42,000/month), permitting support ($18,500/month), and the customer call center ($27,000/month). A negligent error in platform operation (e.g., data loss, failed alerts leading to system damage) or in permit filings (e.g., missed deadlines, incorrect submissions) would trigger Buyer's indemnity to Seller, not the other way around.

**Recommended Fix:**
Rewrite TSA § 7.1 to delete "including Losses arising from Provider's negligence in performing the Services." The standard structure should be: each party indemnifies the other for its own negligence (or, at minimum, Provider indemnifies Recipient for Provider's negligence in performing the Services). If Seller insists on a mutual indemnity, it should be symmetric: Recipient indemnifies Provider for Recipient's negligence; Provider indemnifies Recipient for Provider's negligence.

### 4. Key Employee Agreement: "Business" Defined Far Too Narrowly

**Documents and Sections:**
- KEA § 1.1: "'**Business**' means the **installation of residential solar photovoltaic systems**."
- APA § 1.1: "'**Business**' means the **design, sale, installation, maintenance and monitoring of residential and commercial solar energy and battery storage systems**, as conducted by Seller as of the date of this Agreement."
- KEA § 7.1(a): non-compete on "the Business" (i.e., only residential solar PV installation).
- KEA § 7.1(b): non-solicit of Customers "in the Business."
- KEA § 7.1(c): non-solicit of employees "in the Business."
- KEA § 7.1(d): non-solicit of suppliers "in the Business."
- APA § 6.6: Seller entity non-compete covers the full APA "Business" definition for 5 years.

**Why It Matters:**
Because the KEA uses its own narrow definition, Renfeld is restricted only from *installing residential solar PV*. He is free to:
- Found or join a **commercial** solar company;
- Sell, design, or monitor residential solar systems (just not install them);
- Operate a **battery storage** business (residential or commercial);
- Solicit Buyer's customers, employees, or suppliers in any of those adjacent areas.

Given that Renfeld is the founder, CEO, and majority shareholder whose knowledge spans the entire business, and that the APA recitals identify his covenants as a "material inducement" to Buyer (KEA Recital D; APA Recital C), this narrow scope substantially undermines the protection Buyer is paying for. The APA § 6.6 entity non-compete is of limited practical value if the Seller entity becomes a post-closing shell with no operations.

**Recommended Fix:**
Amend KEA § 1.1 to conform the "Business" definition to the APA: "the design, sale, installation, maintenance and monitoring of residential and commercial solar energy and battery storage systems, as conducted by Seller as of the date of the Purchase Agreement." Alternatively, add a separate defined term (e.g., "Restricted Business") for use in Article VII that tracks the APA definition.

## HIGH-PRIORITY ISSUES

### 5. TSA: Provider May Assign Without Buyer's Consent

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 12.3: "Provider may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, or delegate the performance of any of the Services, to **any Person without the consent of Recipient**. Recipient may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations under it without the prior written consent of Provider."

**Why It Matters:**
The TSA grants Provider (Seller) access to Customer Data, the Monitoring Platform, and billing systems for six months post-closing. Seller could assign the TSA to a third party—potentially a competitor or an entity with weaker data-security practices—without Buyer's approval. The asymmetry (Seller can assign freely; Buyer cannot) is particularly concerning given TSA § 5.3's data-security obligation, which would bind the assignee.

**Recommended Fix:**
Amend TSA § 12.3 to require Recipient's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment or delegation by Provider, or at minimum prohibit assignment to any Person that is a competitor of Recipient or that does not agree in writing to be bound by Sections 5 and 6 (Customer Data and Confidentiality).

### 6. Missing Closing Deliverables for Lien Payoffs

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.3, item 2: fleet-lease liens on 41 vehicles "will be released at the Closing against payoff of the amount stated in the payoff letter **to be delivered under Section 3.2** of the Agreement."
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.3, item 3: UCC-1 on all assets "will be released at the Closing against payoff... Seller will deliver a payoff letter and UCC-3 termination statement authorization at the Closing."
- APA § 3.2 (Seller Closing Deliverables): lists nine items (a)–(i). **No payoff letter, UCC-3, or lien-release document appears.**

**Why It Matters:**
The 41 vehicles are Purchased Assets (APA § 2.1(e)), and the UCC-1 covers "all assets of Seller." Without payoff and release at closing, Buyer takes subject to liens it did not agree to assume. The Disclosure Schedule cross-references § 3.2 for the payoff letter delivery, but § 3.2 does not include it—creating a gap that could leave the liens unreleased and give Buyer no contractual hook to demand them as a closing condition.

**Recommended Fix:**
Add to APA § 3.2: "(j) payoff letters for the fleet lease program and the revolving credit facility, together with UCC-3 termination statements or authorizations therefor, and evidence of release of the Permitted Liens listed in items 2 and 3 of Schedule 4.3." Make satisfaction of this item a condition to Buyer's closing obligation under § 7.2.

### 7. DOL Audit Timing vs. 18-Month Survival

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 9.1: general survival = 18 months post-closing.
- APA § 9.4: "No claim may be made under Section 9.2(a)... unless notice of such claim is delivered before the expiration of the applicable survival period."
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.13(c), item 1: DOL audit opened May 2026; document request received; response delivered July 2026; "No findings or assessment have been issued."
- Side Letter § 2: routes DOL matter through § 4.13(c) (18-month survival).

**Why It Matters:**
If the DOL audit is still pending 18 months after closing (a realistic scenario given federal audit timelines), Buyer may lack the factual basis to deliver a compliant Claim Notice (which must describe the claim "in reasonable detail, including the amount of Losses if then known"). While § 9.4 permits a "good-faith estimate" under the Escrow Agreement (§ 4.1), the APA itself requires the amount "if then known." If no assessment has been issued, the amount is not known, and Seller could argue the notice was insufficient.

**Recommended Fix:**
Add a provision (in the Side Letter or as an amendment to APA § 9.4) stating that for the Installer Classification Matter, a Claim Notice may be delivered upon the issuance of any DOL finding, assessment, or proposed resolution, even if the amount is not yet final, and that the survival period for purposes of such notice is extended until 90 days after the DOL issues its final determination or the matter is otherwise resolved, whichever is later.

### 8. TSA Liability Cap Is Inadequate

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 8.2: cap = "total Service Fees paid by Recipient to Provider."
- TSA Schedule A: monthly fees total approximately $109,000 (months 1–4) and $99,500 (months 5–6), yielding a cap of roughly **$633,500** over the full term.
- TSA § 8.3: cap does not apply to Recipient's § 7.1 indemnity (see Issue 3 above).

**Why It Matters:**
Even after fixing the indemnity inversion (Issue 3), a $633,500 cap on Seller's liability for transition-service failures is low. A single day of Monitoring Platform downtime could generate customer claims, warranty-dispute costs, and operational losses well in excess of that amount. The cap also does not account for data-loss or data-breach scenarios, which could trigger regulatory notification obligations and third-party claims.

**Recommended Fix:**
Increase the TSA cap to at least 3× total Service Fees (approximately $1.9M), or carve out data-security breaches and loss of Customer Data from the cap entirely (consistent with the severity of such an event). At minimum, exclude from the cap any liability arising from Provider's breach of Section 5 (Customer Data and Systems).

## MODERATE-TO-LOW ISSUES

### 9. TSA Billing Service Ends at Month 4 of 6-Month Term

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 2.1: Term = 6 months.
- TSA Schedule A, Service A-4: "Accounting and Billing Support" runs only for the "First 4 months of the Term."

**Why It Matters:**
Buyer must have its own invoicing and collections infrastructure operational by month 5. If the IT/data migration (Service A-5) is not complete by then, there is a four-week gap in billing capability. Given that the business collects on 6,842 residential and 87 commercial contracts, a billing disruption could affect cash flow and customer relationships.

**Recommended Fix:**
Either extend Service A-4 to the full Term, or add a milestone requirement that the billing migration be completed and tested no later than month 3, with a service credit or termination right if it is not.

### 10. 23 Systems Without Final Inspection Sign-Off

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Part B, item 4: "final inspection sign-off had not been recorded for 23 residential Installed Systems completed in 2025 and 2026. Each of these systems has received utility permission to operate. Seller is scheduling re-inspection."

**Why It Matters:**
Although the systems have PTO (permission to operate) from the utility, the absence of final municipal inspection sign-off is a code-compliance gap. If a jurisdiction later enforces (e.g., after a loss event), Buyer—as owner—could face penalties, required remediation, or insurance complications. This is disclosed, so it qualifies the § 4.9(b) representation, but the 90-day survival period (Issue 1) means Buyer has no indemnification if the problem surfaces later.

**Recommended Fix:**
Require Seller to complete all 23 re-inspections before the Closing (add to § 3.2 deliverables) or, if that is not feasible, require Seller to provide a post-closing covenant to complete them within 30 days with costs borne by Seller, backed by a specific indemnity outside the general survival period.

### 11. 18 Remaining Racking Retrofits Due by October 31, 2026

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Part B, item 5: "a racking manufacturer issued a voluntary field notice for a rail splice component... Seller had completed the manufacturer-funded retrofit on 196 of them. The remaining 18 are scheduled for completion by October 31, 2026."

**Why It Matters:**
These 18 systems have a known safety-related component deficiency. The retrofit is "manufacturer-funded," but if the manufacturer delays or the work is not completed, Buyer (as post-closing owner) bears the risk of a structural failure. The work is scheduled for post-closing (October 2026), meaning Buyer will own the systems during the retrofit period.

**Recommended Fix:**
Require Seller to complete all 18 retrofits before the Closing, or add a specific post-closing covenant with Seller bearing all costs and delay risk, with a liquidated damage or indemnity if the retrofit is not completed by the stated date.

### 12. No Service-Level Agreements in the TSA

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 3.1: Service Standard = "substantially the same manner, scope, priority and quality as it performed them... during the twelve (12) months before the Closing Date."
- No SLA, uptime requirement, response-time commitment, or service-credit mechanism appears anywhere in the TSA.

**Why It Matters:**
The Monitoring Platform (Service A-1) is customer-facing and operationally critical. A vague "same as before" standard provides no objective benchmark for performance. If the platform experiences extended downtime or degraded service during the transition, Buyer's remedy is a general breach claim subject to the low cap (Issue 8).

**Recommended Fix:**
Add an SLA schedule to the TSA specifying minimum uptime (e.g., 99.5% for the Monitoring Platform), response times for alerts and customer calls, and service credits for failures. Tie material SLA breaches to a termination right under § 10.2.

### 13. 38 Open Warranty Claims (Individually Sub-$10,000)

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Part B, item 2: "38 open warranty claims under residential Customer Contracts. None of them exceeds $10,000 individually."
- APA § 4.9(d): representation qualified to claims "in excess of $10,000 individually."

**Why It Matters:**
The representation is drafted to exclude sub-$10,000 claims, and the disclosure confirms all 38 are below that threshold. So there is no breach. However, 38 claims × up to $10,000 = up to $380,000 of known, open exposure that Buyer inherits as part of the Assigned Contracts. This is below the $385,000 basket, so even if characterized as a representation breach, it would not trigger indemnification. Buyer is effectively absorbing this cost.

**Recommended Fix:**
Factor the aggregate value of the 38 open claims into the purchase price negotiation or require Seller to resolve them pre-closing. At minimum, confirm the aggregate exposure in the data room and ensure the Closing Statement reflects it.
