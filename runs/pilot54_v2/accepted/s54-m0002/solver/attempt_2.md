## Summary

I have reviewed all nine documents in the matter folder. I identify **thirteen issues** requiring attention before signing, ranging from critical structural gaps to moderate operational risks. The three most urgent are: (1) the 90-day survival period for Section 4.9 (Installed Systems) representations, which leaves Buyer exposed on 10- to 25-year customer warranties; (2) a provision in the Transition Services Agreement under which Buyer indemnifies Seller for Seller's own negligence; and (3) a materially narrower "Business" definition in the Key Employee Agreement that undermines Renfeld's non-compete.

---

## Issue 1 – Section 4.9 Survival Limited to 90 Days (CRITICAL)

**Documents:** APA § 9.1(b) (executed); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9 Part A; APA § 1.1 (definition of "Specified Warranty Period"); prior draft APA § 9.1 (for comparison).

**Description.** The executed APA adds a new proviso to § 9.1: "the representations and warranties in Section 4.9 shall survive until the expiration of the Specified Warranty Period." Schedule 4.9 Part A defines the Specified Warranty Period as "the period beginning on the Closing Date and ending ninety (90) days after the Closing Date." The prior draft contained no such limitation—Section 4.9 survived for the general 18-month period.

**Why it matters.** Buyer is acquiring 6,842 residential Customer Contracts carrying 10-year or 25-year workmanship warranties and 10-year roof-penetration warranties (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.8 Part A), plus 87 commercial contracts with 10-year workmanship warranties (Schedule 4.8 Part B). If a roof leak or installation defect manifests in year 2, year 5, or year 12, the Section 4.9 representations will have long expired. Buyer's recourse would be limited to § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities), which is an indirect and uncertain theory—Seller will argue the disclosed items in Schedule 4.9 Part B (112 historical roof-penetration claims, 38 open claims, 23 permit close-outs) evidence Buyer's assumed risk. The 90-day window is also shorter than the racking recall completion date (October 31, 2026, per Schedule 4.9 Part B, Item 5), meaning even that known remediation may fall outside the survival period if Closing occurs in early September.

**Recommendation.** Restore the 18-month general survival for Section 4.9, or better, tie survival to the expiration of the longest Customer Contract warranty (25 years) with a corresponding increase in the escrow or a specific indemnity for pre-Closing installation defects. At minimum, extend the Specified Warranty Period to 18 months to align with the general survival and the escrow release date. If Seller resists, negotiate a specific indemnity (outside the cap and basket) for defects in Installed Systems attributable to pre-Closing workmanship, surviving for the full warranty term.

## Issue 2 – TSA § 7.1: Buyer Indemnifies Seller for Seller's Negligence (CRITICAL)

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement § 7.1.

**Description.** Section 7.1 provides: "Recipient shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless Provider and its officers, directors and employees from and against any and all Losses arising out of or relating to the Services, Recipient's receipt or use of the Services, or Recipient's operation of the Business, **including Losses arising from Provider's negligence in performing the Services**, except to the extent such Losses result from Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct."

**Why it matters.** This inverts the expected risk allocation. During the 6-month transition, Seller operates the Monitoring Platform, the customer call center, permitting support, and billing. If Seller's call center mishandles a warranty claim, misfiles a permit, or experiences a data breach on the Monitoring Platform, Buyer would owe Seller indemnification for Seller's own ordinary negligence. Combined with § 7.2 (Seller indemnifies Buyer only for gross negligence or willful misconduct) and § 8.2 (cap at total Service Fees, approximately $635,000), Buyer's protection against Seller's transition failures is virtually nonexistent while Buyer's exposure to Seller's claims is broad.

**Recommendation.** Delete the language "including Losses arising from Provider's negligence in performing the Services" from § 7.1. Buyer should indemnify Seller only for losses arising from Buyer's own acts or omissions (e.g., failure to provide access or information under § 3.4). Seller should indemnify Buyer for all losses arising from Seller's negligence (not just gross negligence) in performing the Services.

## Issue 3 – Key Employee Agreement "Business" Definition Too Narrow (CRITICAL)

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); APA § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); Key Employee Agreement § 7.1(a).

**Description.** The Key Employee Agreement defines "Business" as "the installation of residential solar photovoltaic systems." The APA defines "Business" as "the design, sale, installation, maintenance and monitoring of residential and commercial solar energy and battery storage systems." Renfeld's non-compete in § 7.1(a) prohibits him from engaging in "the Business" (i.e., only residential solar PV installation) anywhere in the United States during the Restricted Period.

**Why it matters.** Renfeld is the founder, CEO, and majority shareholder of Seller. He possesses deep knowledge of Seller's commercial solar operations, battery storage business, monitoring platform, design practices, and customer relationships. As drafted, he could lawfully launch a competing commercial solar EPC firm, a battery storage installation company, or a solar monitoring software business the day after Closing, without violating his non-compete. The entity-level non-compete in APA § 6.6 binds "Seller" and its "Affiliates," but if Renfeld forms a new entity that is not an Affiliate of Seller (which may be dissolving post-Closing), that covenant may not reach him individually.

**Recommendation.** Conform the "Business" definition in the Key Employee Agreement to the APA definition, or at minimum expand it to cover "the design, sale, installation, maintenance and monitoring of residential and commercial solar energy and battery storage systems." This is a material inducement to the deal (see Recital D of the Key Employee Agreement), and the current scope undermines the protection Buyer is paying for.

## Issue 4 – Installer Classification Matter Subject to 15% Cap (HIGH)

**Documents:** Side Letter § 2; APA § 9.5(a), (c); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.13(c) Item 1.

**Description.** Side Letter § 2 confirms that Losses from the Installer Classification Matter are recoverable under § 9.2(a) (breach of § 4.13(c)) and that the disclosure does not qualify the representation. However, § 4.13 is not a Fundamental Representation, so the § 9.5(a) cap applies: aggregate liability for § 9.2(a) claims is limited to 15% of the Purchase Price = **$5,775,000**. The § 9.5(b) Basket ($385,000 tipping basket) also applies.

**Why it matters.** The DOL audit covers 137 independent contractors from January 2023 to present (over 3.5 years). Potential exposure includes back wages, overtime, liquidated damages under the FLSA, and civil money penalties. For 137 field workers in the solar installation industry, a reasonable exposure estimate could range from $2M to well over $10M depending on hours worked, wage rates, and penalty assessments. The $5,775,000 cap may be insufficient, leaving Buyer to absorb the excess as an Excluded Liability it must fund from operations.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate a specific cap for the Installer Classification Matter that is either (a) uncapped (treating it as a Fundamental Representation matter), or (b) set at a level reflecting the realistic maximum exposure (e.g., $10M–$15M). Alternatively, increase the escrow amount or require Seller to maintain a separate dedicated reserve. At minimum, confirm with the client's employment counsel a quantified exposure estimate before accepting the 15% cap.

## Issue 5 – TSA Liability Cap Too Low (HIGH)

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement § 8.2; Schedule A (fee table).

**Description.** Section 8.2 caps each party's aggregate liability under the TSA at "the total Service Fees paid by Recipient to Provider." Over the 6-month Term, total Service Fees are approximately $635,000 (A-1: $252,000; A-2: $111,000; A-3: $162,000; A-4: $38,000; A-5: $72,000).

**Why it matters.** Seller is hosting the Monitoring Platform (the operational nerve center for 6,929 installed systems), running the customer call center (handling warranty and service requests), and managing permitting for in-progress and new projects. A data breach, extended platform outage, or systematic permitting error could cause losses far exceeding $635,000—particularly if customer contracts are terminated or regulatory penalties are imposed. The cap effectively limits Seller's accountability to the fee it is being paid.

**Recommendation.** Increase the cap to at least 3x total Service Fees (~$1.9M) or tie it to a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $2M–$5M). Carve out from the cap any liability arising from a breach of § 5 (Customer Data and Systems), including data security failures, given the sensitivity of customer information and the platform's criticality.

## Issue 6 – TSA § 12.3: Seller May Delegate Services Without Buyer Consent (HIGH)

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement § 12.3.

**Description.** "Provider may assign this Agreement, in whole or in part, or delegate the performance of any of the Services, to any Person without the consent of Recipient."

**Why it matters.** Buyer is paying for continuity and quality during a 6-month transition of critical operations (monitoring, call center, permitting, billing). If Seller delegates the Monitoring Platform hosting to a third-party provider of unknown competence, or outsources the call center to a low-cost vendor, Buyer has no contractual right to object. This is particularly risky given that Renfeld is simultaneously the Seller's CEO and Buyer's new division President—he may have incentives to direct services to entities he controls or favors.

**Recommendation.** Require Buyer's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any delegation or assignment of Services, or at minimum for Services A-1 (Monitoring Platform) and A-3 (Customer Call Center). Alternatively, require that any delegate meet the Service Standard in § 3.1 and that Seller remains fully liable for the delegate's performance.

## Issue 7 – 23 Systems Without Final Permit Sign-Off (HIGH)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9 Part B, Item 4.

**Description.** As of July 31, 2026, 23 residential Installed Systems completed in 2025 and 2026 have not received final inspection sign-off, though each has utility permission to operate. Seller is "scheduling re-inspection."

**Why it matters.** This is disclosed as an exception to § 4.9(b) (code compliance and permits), so Buyer cannot claim indemnification for breach of that representation with respect to these systems. However, the absence of final sign-off creates regulatory exposure: local jurisdictions may impose fines, require remediation, or (in extreme cases) order de-energization. The cost of re-inspection, potential corrections, and any associated penalties is not clearly allocated. Because these are pre-Closing installation deficiencies, they should be Excluded Liabilities under § 2.4(a), but the disclosure muddies the analysis.

**Recommendation.** Add a specific covenant in the APA (or a side agreement) requiring Seller to complete all 23 re-inspections and obtain final sign-off within a specified period (e.g., 60 days post-Closing) at Seller's sole cost, with indemnification for any fines, remediation, or customer claims arising from the lack of sign-off. Confirm the obligation is an Excluded Liability under § 2.4.

## Issue 8 – "Closing Statement" Referenced but Not Defined (MODERATE)

**Documents:** APA § 2.3(a).

**Description.** Section 2.3(a) limits Assumed Liabilities to trade payables "that are reflected in the Closing Statement." No document in the folder defines the Closing Statement, specifies who prepares it, when it is delivered, what format it takes, or what happens if the parties disagree about its contents.

**Why it matters.** Without a defined mechanism, there is a risk of post-Closing disputes over which payables are "reflected in the Closing Statement" and therefore assumed by Buyer. Seller could argue that a payable not itemized in a loosely defined statement is not an Assumed Liability, shifting it to Buyer as an Excluded Liability it must then seek to recover through indemnification (subject to the cap and basket).

**Recommendation.** Add a definition of "Closing Statement" in § 1.1 and a procedural provision (e.g., in Article III) specifying that Seller delivers a draft Closing Statement at least 5 Business Days before Closing, that it itemizes all trade payables, and that Buyer has the right to object to items before the Closing Date. Alternatively, reference a standard form to be attached as a schedule.

## Issue 9 – Racking Recall: 18 Systems Remaining, Labor Cost Unclear (MODERATE)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9 Part B, Item 5.

**Description.** A manufacturer-issued field notice for a rail splice component affects 214 residential systems. As of July 31, 2026, 196 are complete and 18 remain, scheduled for completion by October 31, 2026. The retrofit is "manufacturer-funded."

**Why it matters.** While the component cost is covered by the manufacturer, the labor cost to perform the retrofit on the remaining 18 systems is not addressed. If Closing occurs in early September, these 18 retrofits will be performed post-Closing. The obligation arises from a pre-Closing installation defect, making it an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(a). However, the disclosure in Schedule 4.9 qualifies the § 4.9 representations, and the 90-day Specified Warranty Period (Issue 1) may expire before the October 31 completion date. There is no explicit covenant assigning the labor cost to Seller.

**Recommendation.** Add a specific provision (in the APA or a closing deliverable) confirming that Seller bears all labor costs for the remaining 18 racking retrofits and will complete them by October 31, 2026, or authorize Buyer to complete them and charge Seller. Treat as an Excluded Liability.

## Issue 10 – Commercial Warranty Claim ($46,000) – Allocation Uncertainty (MODERATE)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9 Part B, Item 3; APA § 2.3(b), § 2.4(a).

**Description.** A commercial customer has noticed a claim for inverter pad drainage and conduit water intrusion at a 410 kW carport. Seller estimates remediation at ~$46,000 and has scheduled work for September 2026 (post-Closing).

**Why it matters.** This is a pre-Closing installation defect (water intrusion attributable to design or installation), making it an Excluded Liability under § 2.4(a) and not an Assumed Liability under § 2.3(b) (which excludes liabilities relating to "any failure to perform, improper performance, warranty or other breach by Seller on or before the Closing"). However, it is disclosed in Schedule 4.9, qualifying the § 4.9 representations. Buyer's recovery path is § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities), which is not subject to the cap or basket per § 9.5(c). The practical issue is ensuring Seller actually performs the September remediation and does not walk away post-Closing.

**Recommendation.** Require Seller to complete the remediation before Closing (or within a short post-Closing window) at its sole cost, with a specific covenant and indemnity. If Seller will not complete pre-Closing, add it to the list of Excluded Liabilities in a side letter or closing certificate, with Seller responsible for all costs if Buyer must step in.

## Issue 11 – Key Employee Agreement § 9.2 Forfeiture Does Not Cover Retention Award (MODERATE)

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.2, § 6.5(b), § 4.3.

**Description.** Section 9.2 provides: "If Executive materially breaches Article VII, the Company's obligations under Section 6.5 end, and Executive shall repay any amounts paid under Section 6.5(a)." Section 6.5(a) is the 12-month base salary continuation. Section 6.5(b) (unpaid retention installments) and § 6.5(c) (COBRA) are not subject to clawback.

**Why it matters.** The retention award is $500,000 (two $250,000 installments at years 1 and 2). If Renfeld receives the first $250,000 installment at year 1 and then breaches the non-compete at year 2, Buyer must pay the second $250,000 installment (because § 9.2 only stops "obligations under Section 6.5"—arguably including the second installment—but does not require repayment of the first). More critically, the forfeiture does not reach the retention award at all, only the base salary continuation. This undermines the retention incentive and the deterrence value of the restrictive covenants.

**Recommendation.** Expand § 9.2 to require repayment of all amounts paid under § 6.5 (including retention installments and COBRA premiums) upon a material breach of Article VII. Alternatively, make the retention installments subject to a forfeiture condition tied to compliance with Article VII through the second anniversary.

## Issue 12 – Collection Action: Ambiguous Asset Classification (MODERATE)

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.15, Item 3; APA § 2.1(a), § 2.2(a).

**Description.** Schedule 4.15, Item 3 states: "The receivable is an Excluded Asset under Section 2.2(a) of the Agreement only to the extent it is collected before the Closing. Otherwise it is a Purchased Asset under Section 2.1(a)."

**Why it matters.** Section 2.2(a) excludes "cash and cash equivalents." An accounts receivable is not cash until collected. The schedule's language creates a moving target: if the $64,300 judgment is satisfied between signing and Closing, the cash proceeds are excluded (Seller keeps them); if not, the receivable is a Purchased Asset (Buyer gets it). This creates a discrepancy in the Closing Statement and potential confusion about the net working capital adjustment. It also raises the question of who bears the collection costs and risk of uncollectibility if the receivable is assigned to Buyer but the debtor disputes the underlying contract.

**Recommendation.** Clarify in the Closing Statement mechanics that this receivable is treated as a Purchased Asset (included in accounts receivable) unless actually collected in cash before the Closing Date, in which case the cash is an Excluded Asset and the receivable is removed from the Closing Statement. Confirm that collection costs post-Closing are for Buyer's account (as the owner of the receivable) but that any deficiency due to the debtor's set-off or counterclaim relating to pre-Closing performance is an Excluded Liability.

## Issue 13 – TSA Service A-4 (Billing) Ends at Month 4 of 6 (MODERATE)

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement, Schedule A, Service A-4.

**Description.** Accounting and Billing Support (customer invoicing, collections, and remittance) is provided only for the "First 4 months of the Term" (i.e., months 1–4 of the 6-month TSA).

**Why it matters.** Buyer must have its own billing and collections infrastructure operational by month 5. For a business with 6,929 customer contracts generating recurring payments, this is a significant operational transition. If Buyer's systems are not ready, there is a 2-month gap where no one is invoicing or collecting, creating cash flow disruption and customer confusion. The TSA does not require Seller to assist with the billing system migration beyond the general § 3.6 obligation.

**Recommendation.** Either (a) extend Service A-4 to the full 6-month Term, or (b) add a specific milestone requiring Buyer to confirm readiness of its billing system by month 3, with Seller providing extended support (at the existing fee rate) if Buyer is not ready by month 4. Confirm that § 3.6 (Migration Assistance) explicitly covers billing records and that data extracts include all customer payment histories and outstanding balances.

## Additional Observations

- **Assignment and Assumption Agreement** is referenced as a Closing deliverable (APA § 3.2(b), § 3.3(c)) and as an Ancillary Agreement, but is not in the folder. It should be reviewed before signing to confirm it is consistent with the APA's allocation of Assumed and Excluded Liabilities.

- **Order of Precedence (APA § 11.10)** places the Side Letter first, the APA second, the Disclosure Schedule third, and other Ancillary Agreements fourth. This is favorable to Buyer because the Side Letter's protection of the Installer Classification Matter recovery prevails over any conflicting disclosure in the Disclosure Schedule.

- **APA § 11.10 is new** (not in the prior draft). Its addition, along with the exclusion of the Side Letter from the "Ancillary Agreements" definition, was clearly negotiated to give the Side Letter controlling effect. This is consistent with Buyer's interests.

- **Board Consent § 7** appropriately reserves to the Board any amendment changing the Purchase Price or Indemnity Escrow Amount, but grants broad authority to the Authorized Officer for all other changes. Ensure the client's officer executing the APA is aware of this delegation.
