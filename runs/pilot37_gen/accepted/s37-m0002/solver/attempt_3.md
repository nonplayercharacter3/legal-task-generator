## Summary

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA), the Escrow Agreement, the agreed-form Transition Services Agreement (TSA), and the integration call notes of June 24, 2026. I identify **ten issues** that leave Pralor exposed or out of step with its apparent expectations for this deal. The three most significant are: (1) the absence of Torvenne's bareboat charter consent from the Required Consents schedule, creating a post-Closing risk of losing two vessels; (2) the TSA's definition of "Company Vessels" excluding the bareboat chartered vessels, so no transition services will cover them; and (3) the Cygnet Vessel Condition Notice was delivered 37 days after the contractual deadline, triggering the SPA's deemed-acceptance and waiver clause.

## Issue 1 – Torvenne Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Schedule 6.3

**Where:** SPA Schedule 4.7, items 2–3; SPA Schedule 6.3; SPA Section 8.2(c).

**The problem.** Schedule 4.7, items 2 and 3, describe the bareboat charters for M/T Kalvesund Aurora and M/T Kalvesund Borealis and state expressly that Clause 31 of each charter "requires the prior written consent of the Owner to any change of control of the Charterer" and that without consent "the Owner may terminate the charter and repossess the vessel." Schedule 6.3 (Required Consents) lists only one item: Aldmoor Bank's consent or payoff. Torvenne's consent is not listed.

Section 8.2(c) makes it a condition to Buyer's closing obligation that "each Required Consent listed on Schedule 6.3 has been obtained." Because Torvenne's consent is not on the schedule, Pralor can close without it. After Closing, Torvenne would have the contractual right to terminate both bareboat charters and repossess Aurora and Borealis—two of the seven vessels Pralor is paying $186 million for.

**Why it matters.** This is the single most significant exposure in the current document set. Loss of two MR product tankers would materially impair the fleet's earning capacity and the deal economics. The SPA's own disclosure of the consent requirement in Schedule 4.7 makes it difficult for Pralor to argue later that it was unaware of the risk.

**Recommendation.** Add a new item to Schedule 6.3 requiring Torvenne's written consent to the change of control under each bareboat charter, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer, as a condition to Closing under Section 8.2(c). Proposed language for Schedule 6.3, item 2:

> 2. Written consent of Torvenne Ship Leasing Ltd. under Clause 31 (Change of Control) of the additional clauses to each of the Bareboat Charters (M/T Kalvesund Aurora and M/T Kalvesund Borealis) to the sale of the Shares to Buyer and the resulting change of control of the Company, confirming that no right of termination or repossession arises.

**Client decision flag.** If Seller resists adding this (e.g., by arguing Torvenne is unlikely to withhold consent), Pralor needs to decide whether to accept the risk or make this a walk-away point. Given that two vessels are at stake, I would recommend Pralor treat this as a condition to proceeding.

## Issue 2 – TSA Excludes Bareboat Chartered Vessels from Scope

**Where:** TSA Section 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels"); SPA Section 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels"); SPA Section 11.9 (order of precedence).

**The problem.** The SPA defines "Company Vessels" as "the seven vessels owned or bareboat chartered-in by the Company listed on Schedule 1.1." The TSA defines "Company Vessels" as "the vessels owned by the Company and registered in its name." Under SPA Section 11.9, the TSA "controls as to Transition Services." The net effect is that the TSA's technical management, crew management, and ISM/ISPS services apply only to the five owned vessels. Aurora and Borealis are outside the TSA entirely.

This means that at Closing, the intra-group management arrangement (SPA Schedule 4.7, item 4) terminates for all seven vessels, but the TSA picks up only five. Pralor would need to arrange immediate management, crewing, and ISM/ISPS coverage for Aurora and Borealis with no transition period.

**Why it matters.** The entire purpose of the TSA (Recital C) is to bridge the gap while Pralor arranges replacement management. Without TSA coverage for the bareboat vessels, Pralor faces an immediate operational gap for two vessels on day one post-Closing. The ISM Code requires continuous DOC/SMC coverage; a gap could result in class or flag state issues, port state control detentions, or charterer claims.

**Recommendation.** Amend TSA Section 1.1 to define "Company Vessels" consistently with the SPA:

> "**Company Vessels**" means the seven vessels owned or bareboat chartered-in by the Company and listed on Schedule 1.1 to the Purchase Agreement.

This should be a straightforward conforming edit. If Seller resists on the basis that it does not manage bareboat chartered vessels in the same way, the alternative is to add a specific schedule or section confirming that the services in Section 2.1 extend to Aurora and Borealis on the same terms.

**Client decision flag.** Pralor should confirm with its fleet operations team whether it expects to manage the bareboat vessels in-house or through a third party from day one. If so, the TSA gap is less acute, but the ISM/SMC transition still needs to be addressed.

## Issue 3 – Cygnet Vessel Condition Notice Was Untimely

**Where:** SPA Section 6.4(a)–(c); Escrow Agreement Recital C.

**The problem.** The SPA Signing Date is March 2, 2026. The Inspection Period is 45 days after signing, ending April 16, 2026. Section 6.4(b) requires a Vessel Condition Notice to be delivered "within 20 days after the last day of the Inspection Period"—i.e., by **May 6, 2026**. The Escrow Agreement Recital C confirms that Pralor delivered the Cygnet Notice on **June 12, 2026**—37 days after the deadline.

Section 6.4(c) provides that if Buyer does not deliver a timely notice, Buyer "shall be deemed to have accepted the condition of that Company Vessel" and "shall also be deemed to have waived any claim for repair, or for a reduction of the Purchase Price, under this Section 6.4 for that vessel."

**Why it matters.** Seller has a colorable argument that the Cygnet Notice is void under the SPA's own terms. The $2.4 million Disputed Repair Amount is in escrow, but if the dispute is litigated, Pralor faces the deemed-acceptance clause as a significant headwind. The Escrow Agreement (Section 6.4) confirms the Escrow Agent will not determine the timeliness issue—it must be resolved by agreement or court order.

**Recommendation.** This is primarily a negotiation and litigation-risk item rather than a drafting fix. Options:

- **Negotiated resolution:** Accept a reduced repair credit (e.g., $1.5M–$2.0M) in exchange for Seller dropping the timeliness argument. This preserves the relationship and avoids litigation cost and delay.
- **If Pralor wants to press the full $2.4M:** Be prepared for Seller to invoke Section 6.4(c). Pralor's counter-arguments would need to focus on (a) whether the inspection period was effectively extended by Seller's failure to provide adequate port access (Section 6.4(a) requires Seller to "cause the Company to give Buyer reasonable access"), or (b) whether the defects were not discoverable within the inspection period given the no-drydocking limitation. These are fact-dependent and uncertain.
- **Process fix going forward:** Ensure the deal team calendar tracks all SPA notice deadlines from signing. The 45-day inspection period and 20-day notice window are short for a seven-vessel fleet.

**Client decision flag.** Pralor needs to decide its litigation posture on the Cygnet claim. If the client's view is that the defects are real and the amount is justified, we should assess the strength of the timeliness defense before the next call with Seller. I would recommend a realistic assessment that the deemed-acceptance clause is a material weakness in Pralor's position.

## Issue 4 – TSA Excludes Loss of Hire and Consequential Loss

**Where:** TSA Section 7.4.

**The problem.** Section 7.4 provides: "Neither party is liable to the other for loss of profit, loss of hire or any indirect or consequential loss arising out of this Agreement, except in the case of fraud or willful misconduct."

**Why it matters.** For a shipping company, loss of hire is the most common and significant commercial loss arising from management failure. If Provider's negligence results in a vessel being detained by port state control, failing a class survey, or suffering a casualty due to inadequate maintenance, the resulting off-hire period would be excluded from recovery. The only route around the exclusion is to prove fraud or willful misconduct—a very high threshold.

Combined with the per-incident cap in Section 7.1 (10× annual fees = $2,880,000 per vessel), Pralor's recovery for a serious management failure is limited to direct damages up to $2.88M, with no hire loss.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate a carve-out from Section 7.4 for loss of hire directly caused by Provider's breach of its obligations under Section 3.2 (Compliance) or Section 2.1(a) (Technical management, including maintaining class and statutory certificates). Proposed language:

> Section 7.4 shall not apply to loss of hire or loss of profit directly resulting from Provider's failure to maintain the class, statutory certificates or trading certificates of a Company Vessel in accordance with Section 3.2.

If Seller resists a full carve-out, a compromise is to include loss of hire within the Section 7.1 cap (i.e., countable as direct loss up to $2.88M per incident) rather than excluding it entirely.

**Client decision flag.** This is a commercial point. Pralor should decide how hard to push given that the TSA is in "agreed form" and the parties have already signed off on the structure. However, the exclusion of loss of hire is unusual in a ship management context and worth raising.

## Issue 5 – "Net Working Capital" Undefined in SPA

**Where:** SPA Section 2.4; Integration Call Notes Section 8.3.

**The problem.** Section 2.4 provides for a dollar-for-dollar adjustment based on the Company's "net working capital" as of Closing, measured against a target of $14,500,000. The term "net working capital" is not defined anywhere in the SPA. The integration notes confirm that Finance is setting up "closing balance sheet procedures in advance," but the SPA itself provides no formula.

**Why it matters.** In the absence of a definition, the parties could disagree on whether to include or exclude items such as: current portions of long-term debt, deferred revenue, accrued but unpaid voyage expenses, or the working capital associated with the bareboat charter obligations (hire payments). For a shipping company with significant voyage-level working capital (bunkers, port charges, crew advances), the difference between a broad and narrow definition could be material.

**Recommendation.** Add a definition or schedule specifying the components of net working capital. A standard formulation for a shipping company:

> "Net Working Capital" means, as of the Closing Date, the Company's current assets (excluding cash and cash equivalents and any amount held in the operating accounts under the Transition Services Agreement) minus its current liabilities (excluding the current portion of indebtedness under the Aldmoor Facility and any accrued but unpaid voyage expenses that are the subject of a pending invoice).

Alternatively, attach a pro forma balance sheet as of the SPA Signing Date showing the agreed treatment of each line item, with the target of $14.5M calibrated to that pro forma.

**Client decision flag.** Pralor's Finance team should confirm the target was set based on a particular definition. If the $14.5M target was negotiated with a specific balance sheet in mind, Pralor should insist that definition be codified to avoid a post-Closing dispute.

## Issue 6 – Indemnity Basket Is a True Deductible, Not a Tipping Threshold

**Where:** SPA Section 9.5(a).

**The problem.** Section 9.5(a) states: "Seller shall have no liability under Section 9.2(a) until aggregate Losses exceed $930,000, after which Seller shall be liable for all Losses **above that amount**." The bolded language confirms this is a deductible: Pralor absorbs the first $930,000 of losses and recovers only the excess.

**Why it matters.** If Pralor's aggregate post-Closing losses (excluding Fundamental Reps and fraud) total, say, $2 million, Pralor recovers only $1,070,000 from the escrow. Under a tipping basket, Pralor would recover the full $2 million. The difference is $930,000—meaningful but not deal-changing.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate Section 9.5(a) to a tipping basket:

> (a) Seller shall have no liability under Section 9.2(a) until aggregate Losses exceed $930,000, after which Seller shall be liable for **all such Losses from the first dollar**.

If Seller resists, the deductible is a reasonable commercial compromise given the $9.3M cap and 6-month survival. This is a lower-priority item.

## Issue 7 – Six-Month Survival for Non-Fundamental Representations

**Where:** SPA Section 9.1; Escrow Agreement Section 1.1 (definition of "Escrow Release Date").

**The problem.** Non-Fundamental Reps survive only until the Escrow Release Date, which is six months after Closing. Combined with Section 9.6 (escrow is the exclusive source of recovery for non-Fundamental Rep claims) and Section 9.5(b) (cap equals the escrow amount), Pralor's window to discover and claim for breaches of reps such as vessel condition (Section 4.8), environmental compliance (Section 4.9), material contracts (Section 4.7), and litigation (Section 4.10) is six months.

**Why it matters.** For a tanker fleet, certain issues have long lead times: latent hull or cargo tank corrosion, environmental contamination that doesn't manifest until the next cargo or the next survey, class recommendations that arise at the next annual or intermediate survey. If the next class survey falls outside the six-month window, Pralor may discover a pre-Closing defect after the survival period has expired with no recourse.

**Recommendation.** This is a structural point that is difficult to change at this stage (the escrow is already signed). However, if Pralor is comfortable with the risk, no action is needed. If not, options include:

- Negotiate an extension of the Escrow Release Date to 12 months (would require amending the Escrow Agreement, which all three parties must sign under Section 11.2).
- Add specific reps with longer survival (e.g., environmental and class-related reps survive until 12 months or the next scheduled survey, whichever is earlier).

**Client decision flag.** Pralor should decide whether six months is acceptable given the fleet's survey schedule. If a major class survey (e.g., intermediate or special survey) is due within six months of the expected Closing date, the risk is lower. If the next survey is 8–10 months out, Pralor has a gap.

## Issue 8 – No Defined Mechanism for Tax Adjustment on Escrow Earnings

**Where:** Escrow Agreement Section 8.1.

**The problem.** Section 8.1 provides that Buyer is treated as the tax owner of the Escrow Funds and "shall report all income on the Escrow Funds and pay any resulting tax, **subject to adjustment between Buyer and Seller when funds are disbursed**." No mechanism for that adjustment is specified—no formula, no timeline, no dispute resolution.

**Why it matters.** The escrow holds $11.7 million ($9.3M + $2.4M). If invested in a money market fund, even at modest rates, the interest could be several tens of thousands of dollars. Pralor pays the tax now; if the funds are ultimately released to Seller, Pralor is entitled to an adjustment but has no contractual process to enforce it.

**Recommendation.** Add a sentence to Section 8.1:

> When any portion of the Escrow Funds is disbursed to Seller, Seller shall pay to Buyer, within 30 days after such disbursement, the portion of any tax paid by Buyer attributable to income earned on the disbursed amount, calculated in proportion to the amount disbursed to Seller.

This is a minor point but avoids a future dispute.

## Issue 9 – Gannet Time Charter Not Addressed in Schedule 6.3

**Where:** SPA Schedule 4.7, item 6; SPA Schedule 6.3; Integration Call Notes Section 6.1.

**The problem.** The Gannet is on a 24-month time charter from January 2026 (so it runs to approximately January 2028). The integration notes state that the VP "does not expect the charterer to require anything for the share sale beyond a notification letter" and that counsel will "review the charter for any notice requirement." However, no consent or notification from the Gannet charterer is listed in Schedule 6.3.

**Why it matters.** If the time charter contains a change-of-control clause, a notice requirement, or a termination right triggered by a change of ownership, the absence of that consent from Schedule 6.3 means it is not a condition to Closing. Pralor could close and then face a charterer claim or termination right. Even if the risk is low (as the VP suggests), the SPA should reflect the actual state of play.

**Recommendation.** Once counsel completes the review (Action Item 5 in the integration notes), if any notice or consent is required, add it to Schedule 6.3. If only a notification letter is required (not a consent), consider adding a covenant in Section 6.3 requiring Seller to deliver that notification before Closing, or adding it to the Closing deliveries in Section 3.2(a).

## Issue 10 – SPA Section 6.4(a) Inspection Limitation (No Drydocking)

**Where:** SPA Section 6.4(a).

**The problem.** The inspection is to be conducted "without drydocking and without interference with the vessel's commercial operations." For the Cygnet, the identified defects relate to "the vessel's hull and cargo system" (Escrow Agreement Recital C). Hull and cargo tank defects are often not fully assessable without drydocking or tank entry. The no-drydocking limitation means Pralor's inspection was inherently constrained.

**Why it matters.** This cuts both ways. For Pralor, it explains why the inspection may not have revealed the full extent of the Cygnet defects within the 45-day period (supporting an argument that the notice deadline should have been extended or that Seller's access obligation was not met). For Seller, it provides a defense that Pralor accepted the vessels with the benefit of a limited inspection and cannot now claim for defects that a drydock would have revealed.

**Recommendation.** No drafting change is possible at this point (the inspection has occurred). However, this limitation should be factored into Pralor's negotiating position on the Cygnet dispute. If Pralor argues that the defects were not reasonably discoverable without drydocking, it undermines the deemed-acceptance clause's application (which assumes Pralor had a meaningful opportunity to inspect).
