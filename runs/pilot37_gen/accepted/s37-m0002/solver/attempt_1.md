## Summary

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA), the Escrow Agreement, the agreed-form Transition Services Agreement (TSA), and the June 24 integration call notes. I identify three issues that require a client decision before we go back to the other side, followed by a series of drafting points for our markup. ## A. Issues Requiring a Client Decision

### 1. Torvenne Change-of-Control Consent Is Not a Closing Condition

**Where:** SPA Schedule 6.3 (Required Consents); SPA Schedule 4.7, items 2–3; SPA Section 8.2(c).

**The problem.** Schedule 4.7, items 2 and 3, disclose that both Torvenne bareboat charters (M/T Kalvesund Aurora and M/T Kalvesund Borealis) contain a Clause 31 requiring Torvenne's prior written consent to any change of control of the Charterer, with the right to terminate and repossess the vessel if consent is not obtained. However, Schedule 6.3 lists only one Required Consent: Aldmoor Bank. Torvenne's consent is not listed. Section 8.2(c) makes it a condition to Closing only that "each Required Consent listed on Schedule 6.3 has been obtained."

**Why it matters.** Pralor can be forced to close (or may elect to close) without Torvenne's consent. Post-Closing, Torvenne could terminate both charters and repossess the vessels. Pralor would then hold a claim against Seller for breach of the Section 4.7 representation (that Material Contracts are valid and in full force), but that claim would be subject to the $930,000 deductible (SPA § 9.5(a)), the $9,300,000 cap (SPA § 9.5(b)), and the six-month survival period (SPA § 9.1 read with Escrow Agreement § 1.1). The actual loss—two MR product tankers removed from the fleet—would almost certainly exceed the cap. The integration call notes make no reference to Torvenne, suggesting the deal team has not yet addressed this.

**Recommendation.** Add Torvenne's written consent to the change of control under both bareboat charters to Schedule 6.3 as a second Required Consent. This makes it a condition to Closing under Section 8.2(c) and gives Pralor the right to walk if consent is not obtained. If Seller resists (e.g., if Torvenne is affiliated with Seller or if obtaining consent is uncertain), the fallback is to require delivery of Torvenne's consent as a Closing deliverable under Section 3.2(a) and to increase the escrow or add a specific indemnity for loss of the bareboat-chartered vessels. **This is a commercial point for the client to weigh against the importance of the two vessels to the acquisition thesis.**

### 2. TSA Excludes the Bareboat-Chartered Vessels

**Where:** TSA Section 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels"); compare SPA Section 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels") and SPA Schedule 1.1.

**The problem.** The SPA defines "Company Vessels" as all seven vessels "owned or bareboat chartered-in by the Company." The TSA defines "Company Vessels" as only "the vessels owned by the Company and registered in its name." M/T Kalvesund Aurora and M/T Kalvesund Borealis are bareboat chartered-in from Torvenne; they are not owned by the Company. They are therefore excluded from every service in the TSA: technical management, crew management, ISM/ISPS services, the Migration Plan, the budget covenants, and the liability framework.

**Why it matters.** At Closing, the intra-group services arrangement terminates (TSA Recital B). If the TSA does not cover the two bareboat-chartered vessels, Pralor has no contractual right to any transition services for them. It would need to arrange management, crewing, and ISM compliance for Aurora and Borealis on day one post-Closing, with no bridge. The integration call notes (Section 4.1) state that the VP, Fleet Operations expects "all vessels" to be moved to in-house or third-party management within the six-month TSA term, confirming that the client's operational plan assumes TSA coverage for the full fleet.

**Recommendation.** Amend TSA Section 1.1 to define "Company Vessels" as "the vessels listed on Schedule 1.1 to the Purchase Agreement, whether owned or bareboat chartered-in by the Company." This is a one-line fix. If Seller objects on the ground that it does not manage bareboat-chartered vessels in the same way (e.g., because the owner retains some responsibilities under a BARECON charter), the fallback is to add a specific schedule or annex describing the subset of services applicable to the bareboat-chartered vessels. **The client should confirm whether it expects Pralor (or its replacement manager) to step into the Charterer's management role for these two vessels, or whether Torvenne retains day-to-day management as Owner.**

### 3. The Cygnet Vessel Condition Notice Appears Untimely

**Where:** SPA Section 6.4(a)–(c); Escrow Agreement Recital C; Escrow Agreement Section 6.

**The problem.** The SPA Signing Date is March 2, 2026. The Inspection Period is 45 days (SPA § 6.4(a)), ending April 16, 2026. A Vessel Condition Notice must be delivered "within 20 days after the last day of the Inspection Period" (SPA § 6.4(b)), i.e., by May 6, 2026. The Escrow Agreement Recital C states that Buyer delivered the Cygnet Notice on June 12, 2026—37 days after the deadline. Seller has asserted the notice is untimely. Section 6.4(c) provides that failure to deliver a timely notice results in deemed acceptance of the vessel's condition and a waiver of any claim for repair or Purchase Price reduction.

**Why it matters.** If the notice is held untimely, Pralor loses its $2,400,000 claim entirely. The parties have agreed to deposit the amount in the Disputed Repair Account (Escrow Agreement § 2.1(b), § 6.1), but the Escrow Agent has no role in determining timeliness (Escrow Agreement § 6.4). The dispute will ultimately be resolved by a Final Order under SPA § 11.7 (Delaware Court of Chancery) or by written agreement. Pralor's position is weak on the plain language of the SPA.

**Recommendation.** This is a **client decision**. Options:

- **Press the dispute.** Argue that the 45-day Inspection Period should be measured from a later date (e.g., from the date the last vessel was actually inspected, if access was delayed), or that Seller's failure to provide timely port access (SPA § 6.4(a) requires Seller to "cause the Company to give Buyer reasonable access") tolled or extended the period. This is a factual argument that depends on what actually happened during the inspection window.
- **Settle for a reduced amount.** Given the strength of Seller's timeliness argument, a negotiated resolution (e.g., $1,200,000–$1,500,000) may be the pragmatic outcome.
- **Trade the point.** Use the Cygnet dispute as leverage in the broader negotiation (e.g., in exchange for Seller agreeing to add Torvenne consent to Schedule 6.3 or to fix the TSA vessel definition).

The client should be advised of the strength of Seller's position before deciding how to proceed.

## B. Drafting Issues for Our Markup

### 4. Disclosure Schedule Carve-Out Is Overbroad

**Where:** SPA Section 9.2(d).

**Current language:** "Seller shall have no liability under Section 9.2(a) for any matter disclosed in the Disclosure Schedule."

**The problem.** This eliminates all liability for anything mentioned in the Disclosure Schedule, even if the disclosure is vague, incomplete, or misleading. The market-standard approach is to treat the disclosure as qualifying the representation (i.e., the representation is made "except as disclosed"), not as a complete bar to recovery.

**Proposed fix:** Replace with: "For purposes of Section 9.2(a), each representation and warranty of Seller shall be deemed qualified by the Disclosure Schedule to the extent, and only to the extent, that the Disclosure Schedule specifically identifies the matter giving rise to the Losses." This preserves the effect of specific disclosures while preventing Seller from hiding behind a general or boilerplate schedule entry.

---

### 5. Cap on Non-Fundamental Representation Claims Is Low

**Where:** SPA Section 9.5(b).

**Current language:** "Seller's aggregate liability under Section 9.2(a) shall not exceed the Indemnity Escrow Amount." ($9,300,000 = 5% of the Purchase Price.)

**The problem.** A 5% cap is at the low end of market for a shipping acquisition of this size. Combined with the $930,000 deductible and the six-month survival period, Pralor's effective protection for non-Fundamental rep breaches is thin. If a significant environmental or vessel-condition issue surfaces in month five, Pralor's maximum recovery is $9.3M minus $930K = $8.37M.

**Recommendation.** Seek to increase the cap to 10% of the Purchase Price ($18,600,000), with the excess held as a general indemnity (not escrowed) for the full survival period. If Seller insists on the escrow-only structure, at minimum increase the escrow to $18,600,000. **This is a commercial point; the client should indicate its risk appetite before we table it.**

---

### 6. Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations Is Short

**Where:** SPA Section 9.1; Escrow Agreement Section 1.1 (definition of "Escrow Release Date").

**Current structure.** Non-Fundamental reps survive until the Escrow Release Date, which is six months after Closing. Environmental and regulatory issues in shipping (MARPOL violations, ballast water non-compliance, latent hull defects) frequently surface on 12–24 month cycles (e.g., at the next special survey, or when a port state control inspection raises a historical deficiency).

**Recommendation.** Extend survival for Section 4.9 (Environmental and Regulatory Matters) and Section 4.8 (Company Vessels) to 18 months post-Closing, or to the date of the next special survey for the affected vessel, whichever is earlier. This can be achieved by carving those sections out of the general survival rule in Section 9.1 and giving them a longer specific period. The escrow release can remain at six months for other reps; the extended survival for environmental/vessel reps would be backed by a general (non-escrow) indemnity.

### 7. TSA Consequential Damages Waiver Exposes Pralor to Off-Hire Loss

**Where:** TSA Section 7.4.

**Current language:** "Neither party is liable to the other for loss of profit, loss of hire or any indirect or consequential loss arising out of this Agreement, except in the case of fraud or willful misconduct."

**The problem.** If Provider's failure to maintain class, certificates, or the SMS results in a vessel being detained by a port state control authority or losing a charter, the Company (now Pralor's subsidiary) suffers off-hire. The TSA waives that claim. Pralor's only recourse would be through insurance (P&I, H&M) or, if the failure constitutes a breach of the SPA's Section 4.8 or 4.9 representations, through the SPA indemnity—but that is subject to the six-month survival and the cap discussed above.

**Recommendation.** Carve "loss of hire resulting from Provider's failure to maintain class, statutory certificates, or the SMS in compliance with Section 3.2" out of the consequential damages waiver. Alternatively, provide that the waiver does not apply to losses that are directly caused by Provider's breach of Section 3.2 (Compliance) or Section 2.1(c) (ISM and ISPS services). This is a targeted fix that addresses the real risk without opening the door to unlimited consequential claims.

### 8. TSA Co-Assured Insurance Requirement May Conflict with Pralor's Insurance Strategy

**Where:** TSA Section 2.5(d); Integration Call Notes Section 7.1.

**Current language (TSA):** The Company shall maintain "hull and machinery, war risks and P&I insurance on each Company Vessel, naming Provider as co-assured to the extent customary for a ship manager."

**The problem.** The call notes indicate Pralor's broker is preparing to bring H&M and war risks cover onto Pralor's own fleet program at the next renewal. If Pralor's program does not name Brimain as co-assured (which is likely, given Brimain will no longer be the manager after the TSA term), the Company would be in breach of Section 2.5(d) during the TSA term if the renewal falls within it.

**Recommendation.** Amend Section 2.5(d) to: "naming Provider as co-assured or additional insured to the extent required by Provider's P&I club or customary for a ship manager providing the applicable services, provided that the Company shall use reasonable efforts to accommodate Provider's insurance requirements." This gives Pralor flexibility to restructure its H&M program while still meeting Provider's legitimate P&I co-assured requirement.

### 9. Escrow Agreement Tax Adjustment Mechanism Is Vague

**Where:** Escrow Agreement Section 8.1.

**Current language:** "Buyer shall report all income on the Escrow Funds and pay any resulting tax, subject to adjustment between Buyer and Seller when funds are disbursed."

**The problem.** There is no defined mechanism, timeline, or dispute resolution process for the "adjustment." If the Cygnet Dispute is resolved in Seller's favor (i.e., the $2.4M is released to Seller), Pralor will have paid tax on interest earned on those funds. The adjustment should be automatic and prompt.

**Proposed fix:** Add: "Within 30 days after any disbursement from an Escrow Account, the party receiving the disbursement shall reimburse the other party for its pro-rata share of any tax actually paid on interest or earnings attributable to the disbursed amount, based on the period for which each party beneficially owned the funds. Any dispute regarding the adjustment shall be resolved under Section 11.5 of the Purchase Agreement."

### 10. SPA Section 6.4(a) – No Drydocking During Inspection

**Where:** SPA Section 6.4(a).

**Current language:** "Inspections shall be carried at Buyer's cost, without drydocking and without interference with the vessel's commercial operations."

**The problem.** The Cygnet dispute involves hull and cargo system defects. Without drydocking, underwater hull condition is assessed by visual survey and NDT only, which can miss coating breakdown, osmotic blistering, or small-scale corrosion. The $250,000-per-vessel threshold for a Vessel Condition Notice is reasonable, but the inspection scope limitation means Pralor may be accepting latent hull defects it cannot see.

**Recommendation.** This is difficult to fix at this stage (the inspection period has passed), but for the markup we should note that the "without drydocking" limitation means the Section 4.8(d) representation (seaworthiness, good operating condition) is the primary protection for latent defects, and that rep is subject to the short survival period and cap identified in Issues 5 and 6 above. If the client wants stronger protection, the survival and cap fixes become more important. No standalone drafting change to Section 6.4(a) is practical at this point.
