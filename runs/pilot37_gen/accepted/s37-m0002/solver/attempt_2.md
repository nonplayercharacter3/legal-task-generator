## Issue 1 – Torvenne Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Required Consents

**Where:** SPA Schedule 6.3 (Required Consents); SPA Schedule 4.7, items 2–3 (Bareboat Charters); SPA § 8.2(c) (Closing condition).

**The problem.** Schedule 6.3 lists only one Required Consent: Aldmoor Bank's consent or payoff. It does not include the prior written consent of Torvenne Ship Leasing Ltd. under Clause 31 of the Aurora and Borealis bareboat charters. Under SPA § 8.2(c), Buyer's obligation to close is conditioned only on the consents listed on Schedule 6.3. Consequently, **Closing is not conditioned on obtaining Torvenne's consent.**

Schedule 4.7, items 2 and 3, state expressly that if a change of control occurs without Torvenne's consent, "the Owner may terminate the charter and repossess the vessel." The share sale to Pralor is a change of control of the Charterer (the Company).

**Why it matters to Pralor.** Two of the seven Company Vessels (Aurora and Borealis, both DNV-classed MR product tankers) are bareboat chartered-in. If Torvenne's consent is not obtained before or at Closing, Torvenne has the right to terminate both charters and repossess the vessels immediately after Pralor pays $186 million. Pralor would lose roughly 29% of its fleet on day one with no contractual remedy against Seller (the representation in § 4.7 is qualified by the Disclosure Schedule, and the consent is not a closing condition).

**Recommendation – CLIENT DECISION REQUIRED.**

This is not a drafting fix Pralor can make unilaterally. Options:

- **(a) Add Torvenne consent to Schedule 6.3** and make it a condition to Closing under § 8.2(c). This is the cleanest solution but gives Seller a reason to delay or walk if Torvenne is uncooperative.
- **(b) Require Torvenne consent as a condition to Closing but with a long-stop:** if consent is not obtained by a date certain (e.g., 10 Business Days before target Closing), Buyer may either (i) terminate, or (ii) proceed with a specific indemnity from Seller (uncapped or with a higher cap) for loss of the vessels.
- **(c) Proceed without a closing condition but add a special indemnity** in Article IX for loss of the bareboat charters due to absence of Torvenne consent, with survival extending beyond the Escrow Release Date.

I recommend we raise this with the client immediately. The client should also decide whether it is comfortable closing without Torvenne's consent in any scenario. Given that the Call Notes make no reference to Torvenne at all (see Issue 7), there is a real risk the deal team has not been tracking this item.

## Issue 2 – TSA Does Not Cover the Bareboat Chartered Vessels

**Where:** TSA § 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels"); compare SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Company Vessels").

**The problem.** The SPA defines "Company Vessels" as "the seven vessels owned **or bareboat chartered-in** by the Company listed on Schedule 1.1." The TSA defines "Company Vessels" as "the vessels **owned by the Company and registered in its name**." The two bareboat chartered vessels (Aurora and Borealis) are therefore excluded from the TSA.

All TSA obligations—technical management (§ 2.1(a)), crew management (§ 2.1(b)), ISM/ISPS services (§ 2.1(c)), ancillary services (§ 2.2), reporting (§ 3.3), budgets (§ 3.4), migration (§ 4.1), fees (Schedule B)—are scoped to "Company Vessels" or "each Company Vessel." As drafted, **Provider has no obligation to provide any transition service for Aurora or Borealis.**

**Why it matters to Pralor.** Under a bareboat charter, the charterer (the Company) assumes full operational responsibility: crewing, technical maintenance, ISM compliance, insurance. When the intra-group management arrangement terminates at Closing (TSA Recital B), the Company needs a replacement manager for all seven vessels, not just the five owned ones. Without TSA coverage for Aurora and Borealis, Pralor has a gap in transition services for two vessels at the exact moment it may also be facing Torvenne's consent issue (Issue 1).

**Recommendation.** Amend TSA § 1.1 to define "Company Vessels" consistently with the SPA: "the seven vessels owned or bareboat chartered-in by the Company as listed on Schedule 1.1 to the Purchase Agreement." This is a one-line fix but has flow-through effects on Schedule B fees (which should be updated to reflect seven vessels rather than five) and on the operating account structure in § 5.3.

This should be raised in our markup. It is not a client decision item unless the client wants to negotiate fee adjustments for the two additional vessels.

## Issue 3 – Cygnet Vessel Condition Notice Appears Untimely

**Where:** SPA § 6.4(a)–(c); Escrow Recital C; Escrow § 6.

**The problem.** The SPA Signing Date is March 2, 2026. Section 6.4(a) provides a 45-day Inspection Period, which ended **April 16, 2026**. Section 6.4(b) requires a Vessel Condition Notice to be delivered "within 20 days after the last day of the Inspection Period," i.e., by **May 6, 2026**. Per Escrow Recital C, Buyer delivered the Cygnet Notice on **June 12, 2026**—approximately 37 days after the deadline.

Section 6.4(c) provides that if Buyer does not deliver a timely notice, "Buyer shall be deemed to have accepted the condition of that Company Vessel" and "shall also be deemed to have waived any claim for repair, or for a reduction of the Purchase Price, under this Section 6.4 for that vessel."

Seller has expressly asserted the notice is untimely (Escrow Recital C). The parties have agreed to deposit the $2.4 million in the Disputed Repair Account (Escrow § 2.1(b)), but Escrow § 6.4 confirms the Escrow Agent "shall not determine, the merits of the Cygnet Dispute, including whether the Cygnet Notice was timely." Resolution is left to "written agreement of Buyer and Seller or by final determination under Section 11.7" (SPA § 6.4(d)), i.e., litigation in the Court of Chancery.

**Why it matters to Pralor.** If the notice is held untimely, Pralor has waived its right to a price reduction or repair for the Cygnet's hull and cargo system defects (estimated at $2.4 million). The money in the Disputed Repair Account would be released to Seller. Pralor would own a vessel with known, quantified defects and no recourse.

Even if Pralor has a colorable argument (e.g., Seller failed to provide reasonable access under § 6.4(a), tolling the period), the burden is on Pralor, and the contractual language is strict.

**Recommendation – CLIENT DECISION REQUIRED.**

The client needs to make a commercial judgment:

- **Settle the Cygnet Dispute** for a negotiated reduction (e.g., $1.5M–$2.0M) as part of the overall markup package. This avoids litigation cost and uncertainty and frees up the Disputed Repair Account.
- **Litigate timeliness.** If Pralor believes it has a strong argument (e.g., access was not provided, the 45-day period was effectively shortened), it can pursue the claim in the Court of Chancery. But this is expensive, slow, and the contractual language is unfavorable.
- **Trade the Cygnet claim** for concessions on other issues (e.g., Torvenne consent, TSA scope, environmental survival).

I recommend we present the client with the timeliness analysis and a range of settlement positions. We should also investigate whether Seller actually provided reasonable port access during the Inspection Period, as that could support a tolling argument.

## Issue 4 – Environmental Representations Survive Only Until the Escrow Release Date (6 Months)

**Where:** SPA § 4.9 (Environmental Reps); SPA § 9.1 (Survival); Escrow § 1.1 (Escrow Release Date = 6 months post-Closing).

**The problem.** Section 4.9 gives a five-year lookback for environmental compliance. However, § 9.1 provides that non-Fundamental Representations survive only "until the Escrow Release Date," which the Escrow Agreement defines as six months after Closing. Environmental reps are not Fundamental Representations (see SPA § 1.1, definition of "Fundamental Representations," which lists only §§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.12, 4.14, 5.1, and 5.2).

**Why it matters to Pralor.** Maritime environmental liabilities are frequently latent. A pre-closing discharge, ballast water violation, or hull coating issue may not manifest (or may not be discovered by a port state control inspection) for 12–24 months or more. After the Escrow Release Date, Pralor has no indemnity claim under § 9.2(a) for breach of the environmental representation, even though the underlying conduct occurred pre-closing. The five-year lookback in § 4.9 is effectively meaningless if the survival period is six months.

**Recommendation.** Extend survival for environmental representations (and possibly the vessel condition representation in § 4.8) to a longer period—18 months or 24 months is reasonable for a fleet acquisition. Proposed language for SPA § 9.1:

> "The representations and warranties in Sections 4.8 and 4.9 survive the Closing until the earlier of (i) 24 months after the Closing Date and (ii) the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations."

This requires a corresponding adjustment to the Escrow Agreement (either extending the Escrow Release Date for a portion of the escrow, or holding back an additional amount). This is a negotiating point with Seller and may require a client decision on how much additional escrow or extended survival Pralor is willing to trade for other concessions.

## Issue 5 – TSA Excludes "Loss of Hire" from Recoverable Damages

**Where:** TSA § 7.4 (No Consequential Damages).

**The problem.** Section 7.4 provides: "Neither party is liable to the other for loss of profit, **loss of hire** or any indirect or consequential loss arising out of this Agreement, except in the case of fraud or willful misconduct."

**Why it matters to Pralor.** If Provider's negligent maintenance causes a vessel to be detained by port state control, to fail a class survey, or to break down at sea, the Company's primary financial loss is off-hire (lost charter earnings). As drafted, Pralor cannot recover that loss from Provider because it is expressly excluded as "loss of hire." The Company's only recovery would be the direct repair costs, which may be a fraction of the off-hire loss.

For a fleet earning time charter or pool revenue, off-hire is the most significant operational risk of a management transition. Excluding it from the manager's liability is highly unusual and unfavorable to the owner/charterer.

**Recommendation.** Delete "loss of hire" from the exclusion in § 7.4, or carve it back in where the off-hire results directly from Provider's breach of the Service Standard. Proposed language:

> "Neither party is liable to the other for loss of profit or any indirect or consequential loss arising out of this Agreement, except in the case of fraud or willful misconduct. For the avoidance of doubt, loss of hire directly resulting from Provider's breach of Section 3.1 or Section 3.2 is not an indirect or consequential loss and is recoverable subject to Section 7.1."

This is a drafting change for our markup. Seller may resist, but it is a reasonable position for a party that is paying for the service and bearing the commercial risk of the vessels.

## Issue 6 – TSA Liability Cap May Be Inadequate

**Where:** TSA § 7.1 (Limitation of Provider's Liability).

**The problem.** Provider's liability per incident is capped at "ten times the annual Fees for the affected Company Vessel." Annual Fees per vessel = $24,000 × 12 = $288,000. The cap is therefore **$2,880,000 per vessel per incident.**

**Why it matters to Pralor.** A single serious incident—e.g., a cargo pollution event from a failed manifold gasket that Provider should have caught in planned maintenance, or a crew injury from inadequate training—can easily generate liabilities well above $2.88 million, particularly when P&I claims, regulatory fines, and third-party cleanup costs are included. The cap does not apply to gross negligence or willful misconduct, but establishing gross negligence is a high bar.

**Recommendation.** Increase the cap to 20× annual Fees ($5.76M per vessel) or tie it to a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $5,000,000 per vessel per incident). Alternatively, add a carve-out so the cap does not apply to environmental liabilities or personal injury. This is a negotiating point; Seller will likely resist a significant increase, but the current cap is low for the risk profile of product tankers.

## Issue 9 – Migration Plan Requires Mutual Agreement; No Default Mechanism

**Where:** TSA § 4.1.

**The problem.** Section 4.1 requires the parties to "agree" a Migration Plan within 30 days after the Effective Date. There is no mechanism for what happens if they cannot agree. Provider has no obligation to accept the Company's proposed plan, and the Company has no right to impose one.

**Why it matters to Pralor.** The Migration Plan covers the transfer of the SMS, issuance of interim DOC/SMCs to the replacement manager, crew novation, and records transfer. If Provider stonewalls or proposes an unworkable plan, the Company is stuck with no contractual lever short of arguing material breach (which triggers only the 15-day cure period in § 9.3). Given that the entire TSA term is six months (with a possible three-month extension), a dispute over the Migration Plan in month one could consume the entire transition period.

**Recommendation.** Add a default mechanism. Proposed language for a new TSA § 4.1(b):

> "(b) If the parties are unable to agree the Migration Plan within the period specified in clause (a), either party may refer the matter to an independent maritime consultant mutually agreed (or, failing agreement, appointed by the Chair of the International Chamber of Shipping), whose determination of the plan's terms shall be final and binding absent manifest error. The costs of the consultant shall be shared equally."

Alternatively, if the client prefers a simpler approach: "If the parties have not agreed the Migration Plan within 30 days after the Effective Date, the plan proposed by the Company shall apply unless Provider objects in writing within 10 days stating specific reasons, in which case the parties shall meet and confer in good faith to resolve the objections within 5 additional days."

The Call Notes (¶ 4.3) confirm that HSQE will prepare a first draft before Closing, which mitigates this risk practically. But the contractual gap remains.

## Issue 10 – Working Capital Adjustment: No Collar

**Where:** SPA § 2.4.

**The problem.** The Purchase Price adjusts dollar-for-dollar based on whether closing net working capital is above or below $14,500,000. There is no collar (i.e., no de minimis band within which no adjustment is made).

**Why it matters to Pralor.** For a seven-vessel fleet, working capital fluctuates with voyage timing, crew change cycles, pending repairs, and prepaid operating expenses. A small timing difference (e.g., a crew change falling on one side of the Closing Date rather than the other) can swing working capital by several hundred thousand dollars. Without a collar, the parties may end up in a dispute before the independent accounting firm over amounts that are commercially immaterial.

This is a lower-priority item, but it is easy to fix and reduces friction.

**Recommendation.** Add a collar of $500,000 (approximately 3.4% of the target). Proposed amendment to SPA § 2.4:

> "The Purchase Price shall be increased or decreased dollar-for-dollar by the amount by which net working capital is more or less than $14,500,000, **provided that no adjustment shall be made if the difference is less than $500,000 in either direction, and if the difference exceeds $500,000 the adjustment shall be by the amount of the excess only.**"
