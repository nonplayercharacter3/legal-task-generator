## Issue 1 – Product Indemnity Lost Its Exclusion from the Liability Cap (CRITICAL)

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 11.2(a); Amendment No. 1 § 2.1, § 2.4.

**Description.** Section 11.2(a) of the Supply Agreement excludes from the liability cap "Supplier's obligations under Section 10.1." Before the Amendment, Section 10.1 was the Supplier Product Indemnity (covering bodily injury, property damage, product defects, and nonconformity). Amendment No. 1 deleted that provision and inserted a new Section 10.1 (Supplier *Intellectual Property* Indemnity). The former product indemnity was reinserted as new Section 10.3. The cap exclusion in § 11.2(a) was not updated to reference Section 10.3.

**Why it matters.** The product indemnity is Moseth's primary protection against third-party claims arising from defective filter felt (e.g., a baghouse fire caused by felt failure, or a customer product-liability suit). As drafted, that indemnity is now subject to the 12-month-fee cap in § 11.1. Based on the $6.2 million Minimum Annual Purchase Commitment, the cap is approximately $6.2 million (or whatever the actual trailing 12-month fees are). A single serious product-liability event at an industrial customer could exceed that amount. The IP indemnity (new § 10.1) remains uncapped, but it covers only patent and trade-secret claims—not the far more common product-defect and personal-injury scenarios.

The Parent Guaranty (§ 2.4) mirrors the Obligor's liability, so the Guarantor's exposure is equally capped for product-indemnity claims.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 11.2(a) to read: "Supplier's obligations under Section 10.1 or Section 10.3." Alternatively, add a new subsection: "(e) Supplier's obligations under Section 10.3." This should be done by a short Amendment No. 2 or, if the parties are already in negotiation on the Change of Control consent (see Issue 2), folded into that document.

**Caveat.** I cannot confirm from the documents alone whether the parties intended this result. The Amendment's Recital D states the purpose is to "restate Supplier's product indemnity," which suggests the parties viewed the new § 10.3 as a continuation of the old § 10.1. The failure to conform § 11.2(a) appears to be a drafting oversight. I recommend raising this with opposing counsel as a correction, not a renegotiation.

## Issue 2 – Change of Control Consent Not Obtained (CRITICAL)

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 15.3; Amendment No. 1, Recital C.

**Description.** Section 15.3 provides that a Change of Control of Buyer (defined as acquisition of >50% of voting equity) "is deemed an assignment" requiring Supplier's prior written consent. If the Change of Control occurs without consent, Supplier may terminate on 30 days' written notice. Amendment No. 1, Recital C, discloses that Moseth Group Holdings, LLC has agreed to sell all of Moseth's equity to Larchfield Capital Partners, L.P., with closing expected in Q1 2027. The Amendment does not obtain Supplier's consent or waive the § 15.3 requirement.

**Why it matters.** If the Larchfield transaction closes without Supplier's consent, Supplier has the right to terminate the entire supply agreement. While § 13.5 would entitle Moseth to up to six months of transition supply at current prices (because a § 15.3 termination is not a § 13.1 or § 13.2 termination), six months is unlikely to be sufficient for Moseth to qualify an alternate source of needle-punched PPS and polyester filter felt. Loss of the supply agreement would also eliminate the Service Level Schedule protections, the Parent Guaranty (which terminates with the Supply Agreement per § 3.1), and the minimum-purchase-commitment structure that gives Moseth pricing predictability.

**Recommended fix.** Before the Larchfield closing, obtain Supplier's written consent to the Change of Control. The consent should be documented in a side letter or Amendment No. 2. In negotiating, Moseth should:
- Seek consent without condition (i.e., Supplier may not impose new pricing or terms as a condition);
- Confirm that the Parent Guaranty remains in effect post-closing (it should, per Guaranty § 4(c), which provides that a change in ownership of the *Obligor* does not affect the Guaranty—but the Guaranty does not expressly address a change in ownership of the *Beneficiary*); and
- Address whether the Larchfield acquisition constitutes a "permitted assignee" for purposes of Guaranty § 8.5 (which allows Buyer to assign its rights under the Guaranty to "any permitted assignee of Buyer's rights under the Supply Agreement").

**Flag.** I cannot resolve from the documents alone whether Supplier has informally indicated it will consent. The fact that the Amendment acknowledges the pending sale in Recital C but does not resolve the consent issue suggests the topic was discussed but left open. Confirm with the client whether any oral or side understanding exists.

## Issue 3 – Service Level Schedule "Delivery Date" Definition Conflicts with Supply Agreement (CRITICAL)

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 1.3; Supply Agreement § 1.4, § 4.1; Supply Agreement § 17.4(b) (precedence).

**Description.** The Supply Agreement defines "Delivery Date" (§ 1.4) as the date Products *arrive at Buyer's Delivery Location* (i.e., when the carrier makes them available for unloading at Buyer's dock). The Service Level Schedule redefines "Delivery Date" (§ 1.3) for its own purposes as "the date Supplier tenders the Products to the carrier at Supplier's facility for shipment to the Delivery Location, as shown on the bill of lading." Under the precedence clause (§ 17.4(b)), the SLS definition controls "as to service levels."

**Why it matters.** Supplier selects and engages the carrier and the carrier "acts on Supplier's behalf" (Supply Agreement § 4.1). Yet the SLS measures on-time performance from the moment Supplier hands the goods to the carrier, not from the moment they arrive at Moseth's dock. If the carrier experiences a transit delay (e.g., a 3-day delay on the Newark-to-customer route), the SLS treats the shipment as on time, and no Service Credit accrues. Moseth bears the operational consequences of the late arrival (line stoppages, missed customer commitments) without the contractual service-level remedy.

Moseth's only recourse for carrier-caused delays would be a general breach-of-contract claim under § 4.1 (delivery "on or before the requested Delivery Date"), which is subject to the consequential-damages exclusion (§ 11.3) and the liability cap (§ 11.1). In practice, this makes carrier delays a cost Moseth absorbs.

**Recommended fix.** Amend SLS § 1.3 to conform to the Supply Agreement definition, or add a proviso: "Notwithstanding the foregoing, if the carrier selected by Supplier causes the Products to arrive after the Required Date, the Delivery Date for purposes of this Schedule is the actual arrival date at the Delivery Location." Alternatively, add a new SLS provision making carrier transit time part of the lead-time calculation for service-level purposes.

**Flag.** This issue was likely not identified by the business team because the SLS and Supply Agreement use the same defined term with different meanings. It should be raised in the next amendment cycle.

## Issue 4 – June 2026 Force Majeure Dispute: Chronic-Failure Termination Right Likely Expired (CRITICAL)

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recital B; Service Level Schedule §§ 4.1, 4.2, 5.1(a), 5.3, 7.2; Supply Agreement § 16.

**Description.** Recital B of the Amendment states that in June 2026, Supplier failed to deliver several shipments, claiming the failures were excused Force Majeure Events (flooding at its finishing line). The third Critical Delivery Failure occurred on June 12, 2026. Moseth disputed the FM characterization in writing.

Under SLS § 4.2, three or more CDFs in a rolling 90-day period entitle Buyer to terminate, but only if notice is given "within sixty (60) days after the third Critical Delivery Failure." June 12 + 60 days = **August 11, 2026**. The Amendment was not signed until September 15, 2026.

SLS § 5.3 provides that if Buyer disputes an exclusion, the shipment "counts toward the Service Credits and Critical Delivery Failures until the dispute is resolved under Section 7.2." Section 7.2 requires the parties to attempt good-faith resolution within 30 days, after which "Either Party may then pursue its remedies." If Moseth raised the dispute in or around June 2026, the 30-day resolution period would have expired by early July 2026—well before the August 11 termination deadline. The CDFs would have been confirmed as counting, and the 60-day window would have run.

**Why it matters.** If the FM claim is invalid, Moseth had a right to terminate the entire agreement (or as to affected Products) for chronic failure, with no liability, a pro-rata reduction in the Minimum Annual Purchase Commitment (SLS § 4.3(b)), and transition supply under § 13.5. That right appears to have lapsed. Moseth is now locked into the agreement (subject to the Change of Control issue) and its remedies for the June 2026 failures are limited to Service Credits (subject to the 15% monthly cap in SLS § 3.3) and general damages.

If the FM claim is valid, the CDFs never counted, and there was no termination right in any event.

**Recommended fix.**
- **Short-term:** Determine whether the FM dispute was ever formally "resolved" under § 7.2. If the parties simply let the 30-day period lapse without agreement, the CDFs are confirmed and the termination right is gone. If the dispute is still technically open (e.g., the parties never completed the § 7.2 process), there is an argument that the 60-day window is tolled. This is fact-dependent and I **cannot resolve it from the documents alone**—confirm with the client when and how the dispute was raised and whether any § 7.2 process was completed.
- **Service Credits:** If the FM claim is invalid, Moseth is entitled to Service Credits for the June 2026 late shipments (SLS § 3.1) and potentially a monthly shortfall credit (§ 3.2). These should be quantified and asserted. The SLS § 5.3 mechanism means the credits accrue during the dispute.
- **Going forward:** Ensure that any future FM claim by Supplier is met with a timely written dispute and that the § 7.2 process is tracked to completion. Calendar the 60-day termination window from the date the third CDF is confirmed.

**Flag.** The Amendment's Recital B acknowledges the dispute but does not resolve it. The parties appear to have moved on to other issues (IP indemnity, insurance) without closing the FM question. This is a significant open item.

## Issue 5 – IP Indemnity Does Not Extend to Customers or Individual Officers (SIGNIFICANT)

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 2.1 (new Supply Agreement § 10.1); compare new § 10.3.

**Description.** The new Section 10.1 (Supplier IP Indemnity) covers "Buyer and its Affiliates." The new Section 10.3 (Supplier Product Indemnity) covers "Buyer, its Affiliates and their respective officers, directors, employees and customers." The IP indemnity omits customers and individual officers/directors/employees.

**Why it matters.** If a third party (e.g., an industrial end-user of Moseth's filter bags) asserts a patent-infringement claim against Moseth *and its customers*, the IP indemnity protects Moseth but not its customers directly. Moseth's customers would need to look to Moseth for indemnification under their own purchase agreements, creating a chain of exposure. Additionally, if a named individual officer is sued personally (which can happen in IP litigation), the IP indemnity does not cover that individual.

**Recommended fix.** Amend new § 10.1 to add "and their respective officers, directors, employees and customers" to match the scope of § 10.3. This is a low-risk ask given that the Amendment was negotiated to *add* this indemnity—Supplier should be amenable to conforming the scope.

## Issue 6 – PPS Price Index Fallback Is Inappropriate (SIGNIFICANT)

**Where:** Order Form § 5.2(b).

**Description.** For PPS Products, the annual price adjustment uses "the published contract price for PPS staple fiber agreed by the Parties in writing at the start of the Contract Year, or, absent agreement, the polyester index in § 5.2(a)." PPS (polyphenylene sulfide) is a high-performance engineering fiber whose pricing dynamics are fundamentally different from commodity polyester. PPS prices are driven by specialty-chemical supply, energy costs, and a much smaller producer base. The BLS Producer Price Index for synthetic fibers manufacturing (the polyester fallback) is a broad commodity index that does not track PPS-specific cost movements.

**Why it matters.** In Contract Year 2 (beginning January 6, 2026) and Contract Year 3 (beginning January 6, 2027), if the parties fail to agree on a PPS index, the polyester index governs. If PPS fiber costs rise 15% but the polyester index rises only 3%, Moseth pays a 4% capped increase (per § 5.3) on PPS products—potentially less than Supplier's actual cost increase, creating pressure on Supplier to under-supply or degrade quality. Conversely, if PPS costs fall but the polyester index rises, Moseth overpays. The 4% cap (§ 5.3) mitigates but does not eliminate the mismatch.

**Recommended fix.** Replace the fallback with a more appropriate PPS-specific index. Options include: (a) the ICIS or ChemOrbis PPS fiber price assessment; (b) a fixed formula tied to a named PPS producer's published price list; or (c) a requirement that if the parties cannot agree on an index within 30 days of the start of the Contract Year, the prior year's PPS prices remain unchanged (no adjustment). Alternatively, split the index: use the polyester index for the polyester component of PPS felt cost and a separate mechanism for the PPS fiber component.

**Flag.** I cannot determine from the documents whether the parties have agreed on a PPS index for Contract Year 2 or 3. Confirm with the client.

## Issue 8 – New Scrim Construction and Finish Chemistry May Lack Proper § 6.3 Approval (SIGNIFICANT)

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 6.3; Amendment No. 1, Recital D.

**Description.** Section 6.3 prohibits Supplier from changing "any raw material, fiber source, scrim, finish chemistry, manufacturing process or manufacturing location" without at least 90 days' prior written notice *and* Buyer's written approval. Amendment Recital D states that "Supplier has introduced a proprietary scrim construction and finish chemistry for certain Products." The Amendment adds an IP indemnity in response to this new technology but does not expressly state that Buyer approves the change under § 6.3.

**Why it matters.** If the new scrim/finish was introduced without the § 6.3 notice-and-approval process, it is a breach of the Supply Agreement. This could give Moseth a termination right under § 13.1 (material breach, 30-day cure period) or, at minimum, a damages claim. It could also affect warranty coverage: if the product no longer conforms to the Specifications (which were not formally amended), Moseth may have rejection rights under § 7.

On the other hand, one could argue that the Amendment itself—being a "written instrument signed by both Parties" that references the new technology—constitutes the "written approval" required by § 6.3. This is a colorable argument but not a strong one, because § 6.3 specifically requires approval of the *change*, and the Amendment's purpose (per Recital D) is to add IP protection, not to approve a specification change.

**Recommended fix.** Obtain a written confirmation from Supplier that the new scrim construction and finish chemistry are approved under § 6.3, and update the Specifications (Exhibit A to the Order Form) to reflect the new parameters. Alternatively, if the new technology is intended to be a product improvement that Moseth wants to keep, execute a short amendment to the Order Form Specifications that incorporates the new scrim/finish and confirms § 6.3 compliance.

**Flag.** I cannot determine from the documents whether the 90-day notice was given before the introduction. Confirm the timeline with the client.

## Issue 9 – Outbound Freight: Carrier Liability Cap May Be Inadequate (MODERATE)

**Where:** Outbound Freight Rate Confirmation § 6.1.

**Description.** Rusk Valley's liability is capped at $100,000 per truckload shipment unless Moseth declares a higher value on the bill of lading. A full 53-foot truckload of finished filter bags, cages, and cartridges could easily exceed $100,000 in value, particularly for PPS-based products (priced at $21.30–$23.75 per linear meter inbound, and converted into higher-value finished goods outbound).

**Why it matters.** If a truckload is lost or damaged in transit, Moseth's recovery from the carrier is capped at $100,000. The shortfall is Moseth's problem—either absorbed as a loss or passed through to the customer (which could damage customer relationships and trigger claims under Moseth's own supply obligations to its customers).

**Recommended fix.**
- Declare a higher value on the bill of lading for high-value shipments (this typically requires paying a declared-value surcharge);
- Obtain cargo insurance from a third-party insurer to cover the gap between the carrier's liability cap and the actual shipment value; or
- Negotiate with Rusk Valley to increase the per-load liability limit (some carriers will agree to $250,000 or $500,000 per load for an additional fee).

## Issue 10 – Forklift Agreement: Liability Cap Is Very Low (MODERATE)

**Where:** Forklift Maintenance Agreement § 7.1; Exhibit C.

**Description.** The liability cap is "fees paid or payable to Contractor in the twelve (12) months preceding the claim." Based on Exhibit C rates and the six forklifts and two chargers in Exhibit A, annual PM fees are approximately $12,240 ((4 electric × $145 + 2 propane × $185 + 2 chargers × $35) × 12). The cap is therefore roughly $12,000–$13,000, plus whatever repair fees were incurred in the trailing 12 months.

**Why it matters.** If Pellingham's negligent maintenance causes a forklift failure that damages product or injures a worker, Moseth's recovery from Pellingham for its *own* losses (as opposed to third-party claims, which are covered by the uncapped indemnity in § 6.2) is limited to approximately $12,000–$13,000. This is far below the potential cost of a single forklift replacement ($50,000–$80,000) or a production line shutdown.

**Recommended fix.** This is a lower-priority item given the overall significance of the supply agreement issues. If Moseth renegotiates or renews the forklift agreement (the current term likely renewed in March 2026 for another year, expiring March 2027), consider increasing the cap to 2–3× trailing fees or a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $100,000).

## Issue 11 – Safety Stock "Average Forecasted Requirements" Is Ambiguous (MODERATE)

**Where:** Order Form § 6.4; Service Level Schedule § 2.4.

**Description.** Section 6.4 requires Supplier to maintain safety stock "equal to at least two (2) weeks of Buyer's average forecasted requirements under Section 3.1." Section 3.1 provides a rolling 6-month forecast updated monthly. "Average forecasted requirements" could mean: (a) the average of the six monthly forecast figures; (b) the average of the most recent month's forecast broken into weekly units; or (c) some other measure.

**Why it matters.** If the forecast is for 100,000 lm in Month 1 and 50,000 lm in Month 6, the "average" could be 75,000 lm (average of all six months) or 50,000 lm (most recent month). Two weeks of 75,000 lm is 3,333 lm; two weeks of 50,000 lm is 2,222 lm. The difference matters for whether Supplier is in compliance with the safety stock obligation, which in turn affects the SLS § 2.4 reporting and could be relevant in a delivery-failure dispute.

**Recommended fix.** Clarify in the next amendment that "average forecasted requirements" means the average of the most recent three (3) monthly forecast figures, expressed on a weekly basis (i.e., divided by 4.33). This provides a stable but responsive measure.

## Issue 13 – SLS Monthly Cap on Service Credits (MODERATE)

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 3.3.

**Description.** Service Credits for any Measurement Month are capped at 15% of the total price of all shipments with a Required Date in that month. In a month with heavy delivery failures (e.g., the June 2026 flooding event), the cap limits Moseth's recovery.

**Why it matters.** If Supplier misses 50% of a month's deliveries by more than 5 business days each, the uncapped credits would be 10% × 50% = 5% of total monthly shipment value, plus the monthly shortfall credit (§ 3.2). In a severe scenario, the 15% cap could bite. However, the cap is backstopped by the CDF/chronic-failure termination right (§ 4.2) and general breach remedies.

**Recommended fix.** The 15% cap is within the range of market-standard service-level agreements. I would not prioritize changing it, but if Moseth negotiates the Change of Control consent (Issue 2), it could ask for an increase to 20% or for the cap to not apply in months where a CDF occurs.

## Issue 14 – Insurance Requirement Does Not Expressly Cover the Product Indemnity Post-Amendment (MODERATE)

**Where:** Amendment No. 1 § 4 (amended Supply Agreement § 12.1(a)); Supply Agreement § 11.2(a).

**Description.** Amendment § 4 requires Supplier's CGL policy to include "coverage for Supplier's contractual liability under Section 10." This is broad and covers both the IP indemnity (§ 10.1) and the product indemnity (§ 10.3). However, CGL policies typically exclude or limit coverage for contractual liability, and the "products-completed operations" endorsement may not cover all scenarios under § 10.3 (e.g., a defect that causes pure economic loss to Moseth without third-party bodily injury or property damage).

**Why it matters.** If a product defect causes Moseth to lose a customer contract (pure economic loss, no bodily injury or property damage to a third party), the CGL policy may not respond. The indemnity under § 10.3(b) would cover it contractually, but if the cap applies (per Issue 1), Moseth's recovery is limited. The insurance is meant to backstop the indemnity, but it may not do so for all covered scenarios.

**Recommended fix.** In addition to fixing the cap issue (Issue 1), consider requiring Supplier to maintain a separate products liability policy (distinct from CGL) with a higher limit, or to add a "contractual liability" endorsement that expressly covers § 10.3 obligations including pure economic loss. At minimum, require Supplier to confirm in writing that its current policy responds to a hypothetical claim under § 10.3(b) for economic loss.

## Issue 15 – Forklift Agreement: No Express Survival of Safety-Tagging Obligation Post-Termination (MINOR)

**Where:** Forklift Maintenance Agreement § 2.5, § 8.4.

**Description.** Section 2.5 requires Contractor to tag unsafe equipment out of service and notify Moseth's facility manager. Section 8.4 provides that §§ 5, 6, 7, and 9 survive termination. Section 2 does not survive. If a forklift is tagged out of service near the end of the term and the agreement terminates before the tag is removed or the issue resolved, the contractual obligation to address it technically ends.

**Why it matters.** Low practical risk, but in a safety-regulated environment (29 C.F.R. § 1910.178), Moseth wants clarity that any outstanding safety deficiencies must be resolved regardless of termination.

**Recommended fix.** Add § 2.5 to the survival list in § 8.4, or add a sentence: "Any unit tagged out of service under Section 2.5 before the effective date of termination shall remain tagged until repaired and inspected, and Contractor shall complete the repair within thirty (30) days after termination at Contractor's cost if the deficiency arose from Contractor's Services."
