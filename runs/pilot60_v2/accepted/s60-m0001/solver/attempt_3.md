## I. CRITICAL ISSUES

### 1. Liability Cap No Longer Excludes the Product Indemnity (Drafting Error in Amendment No. 1)

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 11.2(a); Amendment No. 1 § 2.1, § 2.4.

**Issue.** Original § 10.1 contained Supplier's product indemnity (baghouse fires, product defects, personal injury). § 11.2(a) excluded "Supplier's obligations under Section 10.1" from the liability cap in § 11.1 (12 months of fees). Amendment No. 1 deleted original § 10.1 and inserted a new § 10.1 (IP indemnity) and a new § 10.3 (the product indemnity, renumbered). The Amendment did **not** update § 11.2(a), which still references "Section 10.1."

As a result, after the Amendment:
- The **IP indemnity** (§ 10.1) is excluded from the cap — uncapped.
- The **product indemnity** (§ 10.3) is **not** excluded from the cap — limited to 12 months of fees (at minimum, $6.2M per the Order Form § 3.1, but potentially less if actual purchases are lower).

**Why it matters.** The product indemnity is Moseth's primary protection against a failed filter felt causing a baghouse fire, explosion, or emissions event at an end-user facility. A single serious incident could generate liability well in excess of $6.2M. As drafted, Moseth's recovery for such a claim is capped at trailing 12-month fees, and the consequential damages waiver in § 11.3 would further limit recovery for downstream losses (e.g., end-user production shutdowns).

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 11.2(a) to read: "Supplier's obligations under Sections 10.1 and 10.3." Alternatively, add a new sub-clause: "(e) Supplier's obligations under Section 10.3." Execute as Amendment No. 2 or a corrective amendment to Amendment No. 1.

### 2. Service Level Schedule Uses "Ship Date" as Delivery Date, Nullifying Service Levels for Carrier Delays

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 1.3 (defining "Delivery Date" as the date Supplier tenders Products to the carrier at Supplier's facility, per the bill of lading); Supply Agreement § 1.4 (defining "Delivery Date" as the date Products arrive at Buyer's Delivery Location); Supply Agreement § 17.4(b) (SLS prevails "as to service levels").

**Issue.** The SLS redefines "Delivery Date" for its own purposes as the **ship date**, not the arrival date. Every service level metric — On-Time Delivery Rate (§ 2.1), Late Shipment determination (§ 1.4), Service Credits (§ 3.1), and Critical Delivery Failure (§ 4.1) — is calculated from the ship date. If Supplier tenders goods to the carrier on time but the carrier sits on the load for two weeks, the shipment is "on time" under the SLS and no Service Credit or Critical Delivery Failure accrues.

**Why it matters.** Moseth is a converter with downstream customers. Late arrival at Moseth's Newark facility means Moseth cannot convert and ship finished bags on time, potentially breaching Moseth's own customer commitments. The current SLS gives Moseth no remedy for carrier-side delays, even though Supplier selects and engages the carrier (Agreement § 4.1) and the carrier "acts on Supplier's behalf" (§ 4.1).

**Recommended fix.** Amend SLS § 1.3 to define "Delivery Date" consistently with Agreement § 1.4 (arrival at Buyer's Delivery Location during normal receiving hours). If Supplier resists, at minimum add a provision that if a carrier selected by Supplier causes a delay exceeding a stated threshold (e.g., 2 business days beyond the standard transit time), the shipment is treated as late for SLS purposes. Alternatively, require Supplier to use carriers with performance benchmarks and make Supplier responsible for carrier delays.

### 3. Change of Control: Larchfield Acquisition Requires Supplier Consent; Not Addressed in Amendment

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 15.3; Amendment No. 1, Recital C.

**Issue.** Amendment Recital C discloses that Moseth Group Holdings, LLC (Buyer's parent) has agreed to sell all of Buyer's equity to Larchfield Capital Partners, L.P., with closing expected in Q1 2027. Section 15.3 deems a Change of Control of Buyer to be an assignment requiring Supplier's prior written consent. If consent is not obtained, Supplier may terminate on 30 days' notice.

The Amendment's operative provisions do **not** obtain Supplier's consent to the Change of Control, waive § 15.3, or condition the Amendment on the Larchfield closing. The recital is purely informational.

**Why it matters.** If the Larchfield deal closes in Q1 2027 without Supplier's consent, Supplier can terminate the Agreement on 30 days' notice. This would eliminate Moseth's supply of filter felt at the worst possible time — during an ownership transition. It would also trigger the Minimum Annual Purchase Commitment shortfall analysis and potentially leave Moseth without the transition supply protection in § 13.5 (which applies to terminations "other than by Supplier under § 13.1 or § 13.2" — a Supplier termination for lack of consent under § 15.3 is arguably neither, but the point is moot if Supplier terminates).

Additionally, the Parent Guaranty § 8.5 permits Buyer to assign the Guaranty only to a "permitted assignee of Buyer's rights under the Supply Agreement." If the Change of Control is not consented to, the new owner may not be a permitted assignee, potentially orphaning the Guaranty.

**Recommended fix.** Before the Larchfield closing, obtain Supplier's written consent to the Change of Control under § 15.3, or negotiate a waiver. This should be done as a condition to closing in the Stock Purchase Agreement or as a side letter with Supplier. If Supplier's consent is conditioned on economic terms (e.g., price increase, extended term), Moseth should be aware of that leverage before the SPA closes.

**Cannot resolve from documents:** Whether Supplier has informally indicated it will consent. Whether the Larchfield SPA contains any provision requiring Moseth to obtain Supplier's consent as a condition to closing.

### 4. Chronic Failure Termination Right Likely Lapsed

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 4.2 (60-day notice window); § 5.3 (disputed exclusions count as CDFs); Amendment No. 1, Recital B (third CDF occurred June 12, 2026); Amendment No. 1, dated September 15, 2026.

**Issue.** Recital B states that the third Critical Delivery Failure in the June 2026 series "occurred on June 12, 2026." SLS § 4.2 requires Buyer to give termination notice "within sixty (60) days after the third Critical Delivery Failure." Sixty days after June 12, 2026 is **August 11, 2026**. The Amendment was executed on **September 15, 2026** — 35 days after the window closed.

SLS § 5.3 provides that while Buyer disputes Supplier's force majeure exclusion, the shipments "count toward the Service Credits and Critical Delivery Failures until the dispute is resolved." This confirms the CDFs are counted for purposes of the § 4.2 test during the dispute. It does not extend the 60-day notice window.

The Amendment contains no provision preserving, extending, or re-activating the chronic failure termination right.

**Why it matters.** The chronic failure termination right in SLS § 4.2 carries favorable consequences for Moseth: no liability for termination (§ 4.3(a)), pro rata reduction of the Minimum Annual Purchase Commitment (§ 4.3(b)), elimination of the minimum for terminated Products going forward (§ 4.3(c)), and transition supply under § 13.5 (§ 4.3(d)). If this right has lapsed, Moseth's only remaining termination paths are:
- Material breach under Agreement § 13.1 (30-day cure period; higher threshold), or
- Force majeure exceeding 45 days under Agreement § 16.4 (if applicable).

**Recommended fix.** If Moseth still wishes to terminate for the June 2026 failures, it should assess whether those failures constitute a material breach under § 13.1 (independent of the SLS chronic failure mechanism). Alternatively, negotiate with Supplier to extend the window or to agree that the chronic failure right is preserved. If Moseth intends to continue the relationship (particularly given the Larchfield transition), it may be better to forgo termination and instead pursue Service Credits and the amended late-delivery notice/recovery plan requirements (Amendment § 3).

**Cannot resolve from documents:** Whether the force majeure dispute has been resolved. Whether the 60-day window is subject to any tolling during the dispute (the SLS is silent on this). Whether Supplier has asserted the force majeure exclusion in a monthly report as required by SLS § 5.2.

## II. SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 5. PPS Price Index Fallback Is Inappropriate

**Where:** Order Form § 5.2(b).

**Issue.** For PPS Products, the price adjustment index is "the published contract price for PPS staple fiber agreed by the Parties in writing at the start of the Contract Year, or, absent agreement, the polyester index in § 5.2(a)." PPS (polyphenylene sulfide) is a high-performance engineering polymer whose pricing dynamics differ substantially from commodity polyester. Using the polyester PPI as a fallback could result in price increases for PPS Products when PPS fiber costs are flat or declining, or fail to pass through PPS cost increases that exceed polyester movements.

**Why it matters.** PPS Products (SNW-PPS-500S at $21.30/lm and SNW-PPS-550M at $23.75/lm) are roughly 2.7× the price of polyester Products. Even a small misalignment in the index could represent meaningful dollar exposure given the $6.2M minimum annual purchase.

**Recommended fix.** Replace the fallback with a more appropriate mechanism: (a) a named PPS-specific index (e.g., ICIS PPS price assessment); (b) a formula based on actual Supplier purchase invoices for PPS fiber (with audit rights); or (c) a requirement that the Parties agree on the PPS index no later than 90 days before the Contract Year start, with the prior year's PPS index carrying forward if no agreement is reached.

### 6. Guarantor's Substitute Performance Is Subject to 90-Day Manufacturing Location Change Requirement

**Where:** Parent Guaranty § 2.2; Supply Agreement § 6.3.

**Issue.** If Supplier fails to deliver Products, Guarantor may cure by "causing Products conforming to the Specifications to be supplied from another facility owned by Guarantor or its subsidiaries, subject to Section 6.3 of the Supply Agreement." Section 6.3 requires 90 days' prior written notice and Buyer's written approval before any change in manufacturing location.

**Why it matters.** In the scenario where the Guaranty is most needed — Supplier's facility is damaged, insolvent, or otherwise unable to produce — the 90-day notice and approval requirement could prevent Guarantor from stepping in for a full quarter. If the alternative facility uses different equipment, raw material sources, or processes, § 6.3 also requires approval of those changes. This could render the Guaranty practically unenforceable in the very circumstances it is designed to address.

**Recommended fix.** Amend Guaranty § 2.2 to provide that the § 6.3 requirement is satisfied by Guarantor's delivery of a written notice identifying the substitute facility and its processes, with Buyer's approval not to be unreasonably withheld. Alternatively, add a short-form approval mechanism (e.g., Buyer has 15 days to approve or reject, and failure to respond constitutes approval) for emergency substitute performance under the Guaranty.

### 7. Force Majeure Dispute Status and Service Credit Exposure

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recital B; SLS §§ 5.1(a), 5.2, 5.3, 7.2; Agreement § 16.

**Issue.** Supplier claims the June 2026 delivery failures were excused by flooding (a Force Majeure Event under § 16.1). Buyer disputes this characterization. The documents do not indicate:
- Whether Supplier identified the excluded shipments in a monthly report as required by SLS § 5.2;
- Whether the 30-day good-faith dispute resolution period under SLS § 7.2 has run;
- Whether the flooding actually meets the § 16.1 definition (it must be "beyond [Supplier's] reasonable control" and not avoidable "by reasonable precautions" — a finishing line flooding could be argued to be within Supplier's control if adequate flood mitigation was not in place);
- Whether Supplier complied with § 16.2 (notice within 3 business days) and § 16.3 (proportional allocation).

SLS § 5.1(a) requires compliance with both § 16.2 and § 16.3 for the force majeure exclusion to apply. If Supplier failed to give timely notice or to allocate production proportionally, the exclusion is unavailable regardless of whether the flooding was a true Force Majeure Event.

**Why it matters.** If the exclusion fails, Moseth is entitled to Service Credits for the June 2026 late shipments (up to 10% per shipment under SLS § 3.1, plus monthly shortfall credits under § 3.2, subject to the 15% monthly cap in § 3.3). Given the $6.2M annual minimum, a single bad month could generate Service Credits in the tens of thousands of dollars. More importantly, the unresolved dispute affects the chronic failure analysis (Issue 4 above).

**Recommended fix.** Send a formal notice under SLS § 7.2 initiating (or confirming the status of) the dispute resolution process. Request Supplier's monthly report for June 2026 and evidence of § 16.2 compliance (date and content of force majeure notice) and § 16.3 compliance (allocation methodology). If Supplier cannot demonstrate compliance, the exclusion fails as a matter of contract, and Service Credits should be calculated and applied.

**Cannot resolve from documents:** Whether Supplier gave the required 3-business-day force majeure notice. Whether Supplier allocated production proportionally. The actual volume and value of the affected June 2026 shipments.

### 8. Carrier Liability Limit May Be Inadequate for Outbound Finished Goods

**Where:** Rusk Valley Rate Confirmation § 6.1.

**Issue.** Carrier liability is limited to $100,000 per truckload unless Shipper declares a higher value on the bill of lading. A 53-foot dry van carrying finished filter bags, cages, and cartridges on 48"×40" pallets could reasonably have a value exceeding $100,000 depending on the product mix (PPS-based products are significantly more valuable per unit than polyester).

**Why it matters.** If a load is lost or damaged in transit, Moseth's recovery from Rusk Valley is capped at $100,000 per truckload absent a declared value. Moseth would bear the loss above that threshold.

**Recommended fix.** (a) Evaluate the typical value per truckload by lane and product mix. (b) If values regularly exceed $100,000, declare higher values on bills of lading (which will increase the freight rate) or negotiate a higher liability limit with Rusk Valley. (c) Confirm that Moseth's own cargo insurance (if any) covers the gap. The documents do not indicate whether Moseth carries separate cargo insurance for outbound freight.

**Cannot resolve from documents:** Typical value per truckload. Whether Moseth carries cargo insurance. Whether any higher value has been declared on prior bills of lading.

## III. MODERATE ISSUES

### 9. Forklift Agreement Liability Cap Is Very Low

**Where:** Pellingham Agreement § 7.1.

**Issue.** The cap is 12 months of fees. Based on Exhibit A and C: 3 electric forklifts × $145 + 2 propane forklifts × $185 + 2 battery chargers × $35 = $875/month = $10,500/year. The cap is therefore approximately **$10,500** (plus any repair fees in the trailing 12 months).

The indemnity (§ 6.2) and gross negligence/willful misconduct are excluded from the cap, but ordinary negligence in maintenance (e.g., failure to replace a worn brake component during scheduled PM, leading to equipment damage but not a third-party injury claim) would be capped at ~$10,500.

**Why it matters.** If a forklift malfunction caused by negligent maintenance damages product or facility (but does not give rise to a third-party bodily injury or property damage claim covered by the indemnity), Moseth's recovery is limited to ~$10,500.

**Recommended fix.** Negotiate a higher cap (e.g., 2× trailing 12-month fees, or a fixed dollar amount of $50,000–$100,000). Alternatively, add equipment damage to the indemnity in § 6.2 (currently limited to third-party claims).

**Where:** Supply Agreement, signature blocks; Amendment No. 1, signature blocks.

**Issue.** The signature blocks provide only "Attention" lines (e.g., "Attention: General Counsel" for Buyer, "Attention: President" for Supplier) without full street addresses. This could invalidate time-sensitive notices (e.g., the 60-day chronic failure window, the 30-day cure period under § 13.1).

**Recommended fix.** Confirm the actual notice addresses for both parties and execute a short notice-designation letter under § 17.3, or include the addresses in the next amendment.

### 13. SLS Section 2.3 (Certificate of Analysis) Creates Asymmetric "On-Time" Determination

**Where:** SLS § 1.3 (Delivery Date = ship date); § 1.7 (On-Time Shipment = Delivery Date ≤ Required Date AND quantity ≥ 98%); § 2.3 (no CoA = not On-Time until CoA delivered).

**Issue.** A shipment can have a "Delivery Date" (ship date) that is before the Required Date, satisfying the first prong of the On-Time Shipment definition, but still fail to be an On-Time Shipment because the CoA has not yet been delivered. This creates a temporal mismatch: the "Delivery Date" is fixed at the ship date, but the On-Time determination remains open until the CoA arrives. If the CoA arrives 10 days after the ship date (but still before the Required Date + 5 business days), the shipment is not "late" (no Service Credit under § 3.1) but is not "on time" either (affects the On-Time Delivery Rate under § 2.1 and the monthly shortfall credit under § 3.2).

**Why it matters.** This is a minor drafting inconsistency that could create disputes over whether a shipment is "late" (triggering per-shipment Service Credits) or merely "not on time" (affecting the monthly rate). The practical impact is small but could matter in close cases near the 95% target.

**Recommended fix.** Clarify in the SLS that a shipment without a CoA is treated as a Late Shipment for purposes of § 3.1 (per-shipment Service Credit) from the day after the Required Date, not merely as a failure to meet the monthly On-Time Delivery Rate. Alternatively, define a separate "CoA Delivery Date" and specify the consequences of its lateness.
