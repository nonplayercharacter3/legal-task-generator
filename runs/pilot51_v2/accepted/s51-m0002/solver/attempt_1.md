## Summary

I have reviewed all ten documents in the matter folder. I have identified twelve issues that warrant comment, ranging from a fundamental departure from the agreed deal structure (environmental liability allocation) to drafting gaps that could leave Doureth without a remedy when it needs one most. The three most critical are: (1) the APA shifts all pre-Closing environmental liabilities to Doureth, contradicting the LOI; (2) the escrow releases at nine months while the environmental reps survive for eighteen months, but the escrow is the *sole* remedy for those reps, creating a nine-month window with no recourse; and (3) the Transitional Supply Side Letter lets Routand assign its supply obligation to anyone without Doureth's consent.

---

## Issue 1 – Environmental Liabilities: APA Contradicts the LOI

**Where:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 2.3(c); Letter of Intent § 4.

**The problem.** APA § 2.3(c) provides that Buyer assumes "all Liabilities arising under Environmental Laws relating to the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility, **whether arising prior to, at or after the Closing**." The LOI, by contrast, states in § 4: "All pre-Closing environmental liabilities will be retained by Routand. These are all liabilities under environmental laws arising out of or relating to the ownership or operation of the Business, the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility **before the Closing**, including liabilities for any release of hazardous materials … occurring before the Closing and any related investigation or remediation, whether known or unknown at the Closing."

The APA is a material departure from the commercial deal as understood by both sides. Doureth would be taking on unlimited, potentially catastrophic pre-Closing environmental exposure (the Hazardous Materials definition in APA § 1.1 expressly includes PFAS, petroleum, chlorine, and caustic soda) while its recourse for any inaccuracy in Seller's environmental representations is capped at the $8.64 million escrow (see Issue 2 below).

**Why it matters.** Environmental liabilities at a chemical blending facility can easily exceed tens of millions of dollars. The LOI made clear that pre-Closing environmental risk stays with Routand. As drafted, the APA puts that entire risk on Doureth.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 2.3(c) to limit Buyer's assumption to Environmental Law liabilities arising **after** the Closing. Pre-Closing environmental liabilities should be added to the Excluded Liabilities in § 2.4 and remain with Seller, consistent with the LOI. If Seller resists, at minimum the environmental reps in § 4.12 should be reclassified as Fundamental Representations (so they are not subject to the Basket or the Cap and survive longer) and the sole-remedy provision in § 9.4(b) should be carved back to permit direct recovery from Seller for pre-Closing environmental matters.

## Issue 2 – Escrow Release Date Precedes Survival Period, Creating a Remedy Gap

**Where:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.1; Asset Purchase Agreement §§ 9.1, 9.4(b), 9.5(a).

**The problem.** APA § 9.1 provides that General Representations (which include the environmental reps in § 4.12) survive for **18 months** after the Closing. APA § 9.4(b) makes the Escrow Fund the "**sole and exclusive source of recovery**" for breaches of those General Representations (other than Fundamental Reps and fraud). But Escrow Agreement § 4.1 releases all remaining escrow funds to Seller on the first Business Day after **9 months** post-Closing (i.e., approximately July 1, 2027), less only amounts subject to Claim Notices already delivered.

The result: if a breach of a General Representation is discovered in month 10 through month 18 (within the survival period), no Claim Notice was delivered before the Release Date, so the full escrow has gone to Seller. Because the escrow was the *sole* remedy, Doureth has no recourse. The 18-month survival period is effectively meaningless for the last nine months.

**Why it matters.** This is the single most structurally dangerous provision in the document set. Environmental contamination (PFAS, legacy solvents, heavy metals) frequently does not manifest until well after closing. Doureth could discover a § 4.12 breach in month 14 and have no remedy.

**Recommended fix.** Align the Release Date with the survival period. Either (a) extend the Release Date in Escrow Agreement § 4.1 to 18 months after the Closing (matching the General Rep survival), or (b) shorten the General Rep survival in APA § 9.1 to 9 months, or (c) amend APA § 9.4(b) to provide that the Escrow Fund is the *first* (not sole) source of recovery for General Rep breaches, permitting direct claims against Seller after the escrow is exhausted or released. Option (a) is cleanest and most protective.

## Issue 3 – Transitional Supply Side Letter: Asymmetric Assignment Right

**Where:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 7.

**The problem.** Section 7 provides: "Seller may assign this letter agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under it, to **any Person without the consent of Buyer**." Buyer, by contrast, "may not assign … without the prior written consent of Seller."

Seller can transfer its supply obligation to a third party—potentially an entity with no chlor-alkali production capacity, no financial stability, or no commitment to the Specifications—without Doureth's approval. Doureth's entire sodium hypochlorite production depends on this feedstock for 24 months (extendable to 36).

**Why it matters.** If Routand assigns the supply to an Affiliate or a new entity post-closing, Doureth has no consent right and no practical way to ensure continued reliable supply. The non-compete in APA § 6.5 prevents Routand from competing, but it does not prevent Routand from selling its caustic soda production assets or assigning the supply contract to a competitor of Doureth.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 7 to require Buyer's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment by Seller, or at minimum limit assignment to an Affiliate that assumes the obligations in writing and has demonstrated production capacity. Make the provision symmetric with the restriction on Buyer.

## Issue 4 – Seller Note: "Final Determination" for Set-Off Is Overly Narrow

**Where:** Seller Promissory Note § 5.2; Asset Purchase Agreement § 9.7.

**The problem.** Note § 5.2 defines "finally determined" as only (a) written agreement of the parties or (b) "a final, non-appealable order of a court of competent jurisdiction." This excludes:

- Arbitration awards (even final ones);
- Court orders that are appealable (i.e., most first-instance judgments);
- Settlements that are not reduced to a court order;
- Determinations by the independent accounting firm under APA § 2.8 (working capital disputes).

APA § 9.7 contemplates set-off for amounts "finally determined to be owed … under this Article IX." But the Note's definition of "finally determined" is narrower than what § 9.7 appears to intend.

**Why it matters.** The Seller Note is $12 million and is Doureth's primary post-closing leverage over Routand. If the set-off right is effectively unusable except in the rare case of a non-appealable judgment, Doureth loses a critical self-help remedy. In practice, most disputes will be resolved by agreement or by an appealable order, neither of which qualifies.

**Recommended fix.** Broaden Note § 5.2 to include: (a) written agreement; (b) a final, non-appealable court order; (c) a final and binding arbitration award; and (d) a written settlement agreement between the parties that resolves the claim. Alternatively, conform § 5.2 to the language in APA § 9.7 by deleting the "final, non-appealable" qualifier and using "finally determined" as defined in the APA (which is not separately defined, suggesting the broader intent).

## Issue 5 – Transitional Supply Side Letter: Force Majeure Includes Seller's Own Production Outages

**Where:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 5.

**The problem.** The definition of Force Majeure Event includes "unplanned outages at Seller's chlor-alkali production units." This is not an external, unforeseeable event—it is an operational risk inherent to Seller's own business. A chlor-alkali plant that suffers frequent unplanned outages would be able to invoke force majeure to excuse non-delivery, with only a pro rata allocation obligation and no damages liability.

During a Force Majeure Event, Seller allocates product "on a pro rata basis by reference to volumes over the preceding three months" (including to the Retained Business). Combined with § 1.3 (supply priority only "at least equal to" Seller's internal requirements), Doureth could receive significantly less than its firm orders for an extended period.

**Why it matters.** Doureth's sodium hypochlorite production depends on continuous caustic soda feedstock. If Routand's chlor-alkali unit has a multi-week outage, Doureth may face a production shutdown with no remedy (the limitation of liability in § 9 excludes consequential damages, which would include lost production).

**Recommended fix.** (a) Delete "unplanned outages at Seller's chlor-alkali production units" from the Force Majeure definition, or (b) add a requirement that Seller maintain a minimum inventory buffer (e.g., 30 days of expected deliveries) at or near the Lake Charles Facility to absorb short outages, or (c) provide that during any Force Majeure Event, Seller's allocation to Buyer shall be at least equal to Buyer's firm order volume for the affected period (rather than pro rata). At minimum, confirm that the § 6.3 termination right (50%+ of firm orders for 60+ days) is adequate and that the replacement-purchase provision in § 5 does not create a cost trap for Doureth.

## Issue 6 – Transitional Supply Side Letter: Limitation of Liability May Bar Recovery for Supply Failure

**Where:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 9.

**The problem.** Section 9 excludes "consequential, incidental, indirect, special or punitive damages, or for lost profits." If Seller fails to deliver caustic soda (outside of Force Majeure) and Doureth must purchase replacement product from a third party at a higher price, or suffers a production shutdown, the cost differential and lost production are likely to be characterized as consequential damages. The only carve-outs are for breaches of confidentiality (§ 8), third-party indemnification, and gross negligence/willful misconduct.

**Why it matters.** The practical remedy for a supply failure is the cost of cover (buying replacement caustic soda) plus any production losses. If those are barred as consequential, Doureth's only recoverable damages are the direct price of the undelivered product—which may be nominal if the product was to be delivered in the future.

**Recommended fix.** Add an express carve-out from the consequential damages waiver for Seller's failure to deliver conforming Product in accordance with a firm or binding order, providing that Buyer's recovery includes the cost of purchasing replacement Product from a third party (the "cover" damages under UCC § 2-712, as adopted in Louisiana). Alternatively, specify that the direct damages for non-delivery include the difference between the contract Price and the then-current market price for equivalent Product.

## Issue 7 – Seller Note: Cross-Default to Senior Debt Acceleration

**Where:** Seller Promissory Note § 7.1(d).

**The problem.** An Event of Default under the Note occurs if "the Senior Indebtedness is accelerated following an event of default under the Senior Credit Agreement." This means that if Doureth's senior lender accelerates the term loan (even for a technical or curable default that is subsequently cured), the Seller Note is also in default, triggering the default interest rate (+200 bps under § 2.3) and potentially acceleration of the Note under § 7.2.

**Why it matters.** The $55 million term loan is the primary funding source. If a technical default under the senior facility occurs (e.g., a missed financial covenant that is cured within the grace period), the senior lender might still accelerate before the cure is complete. That would put the Seller Note in default simultaneously, increasing Doureth's cost of capital and creating a cascading default risk.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 7.1(d) to provide that the cross-default is triggered only if the Senior Indebtedness is accelerated **and not cured or waived within [30/60] days**, or delete the cross-default entirely and rely on the subordination mechanics in § 6 (which already protect the senior lender). The subordination in § 6 already ensures that the senior debt is paid first; the cross-default is an additional, harsher remedy that benefits Seller at Doureth's expense.

## Issue 8 – APA § 9.5(a): Cap on General Rep Breaches Is Inadequate for Environmental Risk

**Where:** Asset Purchase Agreement §§ 9.5(a), 1.1 (definition of "General Representations"), 4.12.

**The problem.** The Cap on Seller's liability for breaches of General Representations (which include all environmental reps in § 4.12) is the Escrow Amount: $8,640,000. The Basket is $864,000. For a chemical facility with PFAS, petroleum, chlorine, and caustic soda in the Hazardous Materials definition, $8.64 million is likely inadequate to cover a single significant environmental remediation.

**Why it matters.** Even if Issue 1 is resolved (pre-Closing environmental liabilities stay with Seller), Doureth still needs adequate recourse if Seller's environmental reps turn out to be inaccurate. An $8.64 million cap on a facility that handles the chemicals listed in the Hazardous Materials definition is a thin protection.

**Recommended fix.** (a) Reclassify § 4.12 (Environmental Matters) as a Fundamental Representation, which would take it outside the Basket and Cap (APA § 9.5(b) caps Fundamental Rep breaches at the full Purchase Price of $86.4 million) and extend survival to 60 days past the applicable statute of limitations. Or (b) negotiate a separate, higher cap specifically for environmental rep breaches (e.g., 2x or 3x the Escrow Amount). Option (a) is stronger and more standard for environmental matters in asset purchases involving regulated facilities.

## Issue 9 – APA § 9.1: 18-Month Survival for Environmental Reps Is Too Short

**Where:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 9.1; § 1.1 (General Representations includes § 4.12).

**The problem.** General Representations survive only 18 months after the Closing Date. For environmental matters at a chemical facility, 18 months is insufficient. PFAS contamination, for example, may not be detected until a comprehensive Phase II assessment or regulatory investigation occurs years later. The Louisiana Environmental Quality Act and federal CERCLA provide long (sometimes unlimited) statutory periods for environmental claims.

**Why it matters.** If a significant environmental condition is discovered in year 3, the rep has expired and Doureth has no claim for breach of representation. Combined with Issue 2 (escrow already released) and Issue 8 (cap), Doureth could be left bearing the full cost of pre-Closing contamination with no recourse.

**Recommended fix.** Extend the survival period for § 4.12 (Environmental Matters) to the later of (a) 60 months after the Closing Date or (b) 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (matching the Fundamental Rep survival in § 9.1). If Seller resists, a compromise of 36 months for environmental reps is more defensible than 18.

## Issue 10 – Governing Law Mismatch: APA (Delaware) vs. Seller Note (New York)

**Where:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 12.8; Seller Promissory Note § 9.1.

**The problem.** The APA is governed by Delaware law; the Seller Note is governed by New York law. APA § 12.4 provides that the Note "shall control with respect to its payment, set-off and enforcement." This means that for any dispute about whether a set-off is proper, whether a payment was made in the correct amount, or whether the Note has been accelerated, New York law applies—not Delaware law. New York and Delaware can differ on issues of set-off rights, the enforceability of liquidated damages, and the interpretation of "final determination."

**Why it matters.** In a dispute over the set-off right (which is central to Doureth's post-closing protection), the applicable law will be New York's. Doureth's Delaware-law-based expectations about the scope of the set-off right may not be honored under New York law.

**Recommended fix.** Conform the Note's governing law to Delaware (matching the APA and all other Ancillary Agreements). If Seller insists on New York law for the Note (perhaps because its senior lender required it), add a proviso to Note § 9.1 that, in the event of a conflict between the Note and the APA on set-off rights, the APA's provisions (and Delaware law) shall control.

## Issue 11 – Plant Director Employment Agreement: Equity Award Is Conditional

**Where:** Plant Director Employment Agreement § 3.4.

**The problem.** The $200,000 restricted stock unit grant is "Subject to approval by the Compensation Committee." This is not a guaranteed component of the compensation package. If the Compensation Committee does not approve the grant (or approves a reduced grant), the Executive has no claim for it.

**Why it matters.** The Plant Director's employment is a condition to Closing (APA § 7.2(e)). The equity award is a key retention tool for a 24-month non-compete. If the award is not granted, the Executive's economic incentive to stay through the non-compete period is reduced, increasing the risk that she leaves before the non-compete expires or that she feels undercompensated relative to the package presented to the Board (Board Consent § 5 references "terms consistent with those presented to the Board").

**Recommended fix.** Delete "Subject to approval by the Compensation Committee" and make the grant unconditional (the Board has already approved the terms in the written consent). Alternatively, provide that the Compensation Committee's approval is deemed given unless it acts within 30 days of the Effective Date, and that failure to grant the full amount constitutes a Good Reason trigger under § 5.3(a) (material reduction in compensation).

## Issue 12 – Integration Meeting Notes: Sampling Contractor May Not Be an Assigned Contract

**Where:** Integration Meeting Notes § 3.4; Asset Purchase Agreement Schedule 2.1(c) (referenced but not in folder).

**The problem.** Annika Solberg-Reyes noted that the quarterly discharge monitoring report for Q3 is due after the Closing and recommended retaining the current Routand sampling contractor. The action item (No. 2) assigns Doureth Procurement to "confirm status of sampling contractor contract" by September 17. If that contract is not on Schedule 2.1(c) (Assigned Contracts), it is not a Purchased Asset and Doureth has no right to it. The contractor would be a Routand vendor with no obligation to Doureth.

**Why it matters.** Environmental monitoring and reporting are mandatory under the permits. If the sampling contractor relationship does not transfer and a replacement is not in place by the Q3 reporting deadline, Doureth could be in technical non-compliance with its wastewater discharge permit within weeks of Closing.

**Recommended fix.** Confirm before Closing that the sampling contractor agreement is listed on Schedule 2.1(c). If it is not, either (a) add it to the schedule, or (b) require Seller to enter into a short-term transition agreement (30–60 days) to allow Doureth to complete the Q3 reporting cycle, or (c) have Doureth's EHS team engage a replacement contractor before Closing so there is no gap.
