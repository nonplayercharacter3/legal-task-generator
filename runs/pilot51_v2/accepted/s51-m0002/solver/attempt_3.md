## 1. Buyer Assumes All Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities – Contradicts the LOI

**Documents:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 2.3(c); Letter of Intent § 4

**Issue.** APA § 2.3(c) provides that Buyer assumes "all Liabilities arising under Environmental Laws relating to the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility, **whether arising prior to, at or after the Closing**." The LOI § 4 expressly provides the opposite: "All pre-Closing environmental liabilities will be retained by Routand," including "liabilities for any release of hazardous materials at, on, under or from the Lake Charles Facility occurring before the Closing and any related investigation or remediation, whether known or unknown at the Closing." The LOI further states that "Routand will indemnify Doureth for those liabilities."

**Why it matters.** The Lake Charles Facility is a chemical blending plant handling caustic soda, chlorine, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (all defined as Hazardous Materials in APA § 1.1). Pre-Closing contamination is a realistic risk. As drafted, Doureth would own all environmental liability for the facility going forward, including legacy problems that may not surface for years, while its indemnification recourse for the related rep breach (APA § 4.12) is severely constrained (see Issue 2 below). This is a material departure from the deal the Board approved and the terms the parties agreed in the LOI.

**Recommendation.** Amend § 2.3(c) to exclude pre-Closing environmental liabilities from the Assumed Liabilities, consistent with the LOI. Alternatively, if Seller insists on the current language, add pre-Closing environmental liabilities to the Excluded Liabilities in § 2.4 and confirm that § 9.2(c) (indemnification for Excluded Liabilities, which is not subject to the Basket or Cap per § 9.5(c)) covers them.

## 2. Escrow Releases at 9 Months but General Representations Survive 18 Months – Creating a Remedy Gap

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.1; Asset Purchase Agreement §§ 9.1, 9.4(b)

**Issue.** The Escrow Agreement § 4.1 releases all remaining Escrow Funds to Seller on the first Business Day after 9 months post-Closing, "without any further action by Buyer." APA § 9.1 provides that General Representations (which include the Environmental Matters rep in § 4.12) survive 18 months after Closing. APA § 9.4(b) makes the Escrow Fund the "sole and exclusive source of recovery" for Buyer Parties for losses under § 9.2(a) (breach of representations), other than Fundamental Representations or fraud.

**Why it matters.** Between months 9 and 18 after Closing, the escrow has been released to Seller, yet the General Reps are still alive. If a breach of a General Rep (e.g., an environmental rep) is discovered in month 12, Doureth has no practical remedy: the escrow is gone, and § 9.4(b) bars recovery from Seller directly. The only carve-outs are Fundamental Reps and fraud, both of which are narrow. This gap is especially acute given Issue 1 (environmental liability assumption).

**Recommendation.** Extend the Escrow Release Date to at least 18 months after Closing to match the General Rep survival period. If Seller resists, at minimum carve environmental claims out of § 9.4(b) so they are not limited to the escrow as the sole source of recovery. Alternatively, shorten the General Rep survival period to 9 months (though this is less favorable to Doureth and may not be acceptable given the environmental risk profile).

## 3. Basket Is a True Deductible, Not a Tipping Basket

**Documents:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 9.5(a)

**Issue.** § 9.5(a) states: "Seller shall not be liable under Section 9.2(a) until the aggregate Losses under it exceed $864,000 (the 'Basket'), **after which Seller shall be liable only for Losses in excess of the Basket**." This is a deductible: the first $864,000 of losses is entirely absorbed by Doureth.

**Why it matters.** In a chemical facility with environmental risk, even a single moderate claim (e.g., a soil remediation estimate) could easily exceed the deductible but the structure means Doureth eats the first $864,000 of every aggregate loss. A tipping basket (where once the threshold is crossed, all losses from dollar one are recoverable) would be meaningfully more favorable.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate a tipping (or "first-dollar") basket: once aggregate losses exceed $864,000, Seller is liable for all losses from the first dollar. If Seller insists on a deductible, consider reducing the threshold.

## 4. Transitional Supply Letter – Seller May Assign Unilaterally

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 7

**Issue.** § 7 provides: "Seller may assign this letter agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under it, to **any Person without the consent of Buyer**." The reverse is not true: Buyer may not assign without Seller's consent.

**Why it matters.** Doureth is dependent on this supply arrangement for caustic soda feedstock for up to 24 months (plus extensions). If Seller assigns the supply obligation to a third party with different production capabilities, quality controls, or financial stability, Doureth's feedstock supply could be disrupted. The assignment right is particularly concerning because the Product must meet specific membrane-grade specifications (Annex A), and a new supplier may not be able to match them.

**Recommendation.** Require Buyer's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment by Seller, or at minimum limit assignment to an Affiliate of Seller that has demonstrated ability to meet the Specifications.

## 5. Transitional Supply Letter – Consequential Damages Waiver Exposes Doureth to Unrecoverable Supply Shortfall Costs

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 9

**Issue.** § 9 provides that, except for confidentiality breaches, third-party indemnification, and gross negligence/willful misconduct, "neither party is liable to the other for consequential, incidental, indirect, special or punitive damages, or for lost profits, arising out of this letter agreement."

**Why it matters.** If Seller fails to supply (other than by gross negligence or willful misconduct), Doureth's primary economic harm would be lost profits from the sodium hypochlorite it cannot produce, plus the price premium paid for cover from a third-party supplier. The lost profits are almost certainly "consequential" or "indirect" and would be barred. The cost of cover may be characterized as direct damages, but the argument is not clear-cut, and the § 5 force majeure provision (which allows pro rata allocation) could further limit recovery.

**Recommendation.** Carve out of the consequential damages waiver any failure by Seller to supply the Product in accordance with its commitments (Sections 1 and 2), so that Doureth can recover the cost of cover and resulting production losses. Alternatively, add a specific liquidated damages provision for supply failure that is not subject to the waiver.

## 6. Transitional Supply Letter – Supply Priority Is Only "At Least Equal" to Seller's Own Needs

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 1.3

**Issue.** § 1.3 states: "Seller shall give Buyer's firm orders priority **at least equal to** the priority Seller gives its own internal requirements, including those of the Retained Business."

**Why it matters.** This means in a supply-constrained situation, Seller's own chlor-alkali production (including for the Retained Business) is not subordinate to Doureth's needs. If the Retained Business also consumes caustic soda, Seller could allocate feedstock to itself first or equally, leaving Doureth short. Combined with the force majeure pro rata allocation in § 5, Doureth has no guaranteed priority.

**Recommendation.** Negotiate for Buyer's firm orders to receive priority **over** Seller's internal requirements (including the Retained Business), at least up to the maximum monthly volume in § 2.3. If Seller resists, add a commitment that Seller will use commercially reasonable efforts to expand production capacity to meet Buyer's firm orders during periods of tight supply.

## 7. Seller Note – Cross-Default to Senior Debt Triggers Automatic Acceleration

**Documents:** Seller Promissory Note § 7.1(d)

**Issue.** § 7.1(d) makes it an Event of Default if "the Senior Indebtedness is accelerated following an event of default under the Senior Credit Agreement." Under § 7.2, this allows Seller to declare the entire Note immediately due and payable.

**Why it matters.** If Doureth experiences a temporary cash-flow issue that triggers a default under its senior credit facility (even one that is later cured or waived), the Seller Note could be accelerated. This creates a cascading default risk: a senior debt problem becomes a $12 million immediate liability to Seller, potentially triggering further financial distress. The provision does not include a cure period for the senior default.

**Recommendation.** Add a cure period (e.g., 30 days) after the Senior Indebtedness is accelerated before it constitutes an Event of Default under the Note. Alternatively, limit the cross-default to cases where the Senior Indebtedness remains accelerated and unpaid for more than 60 days.

## 8. Seller Note – New York Governing Law Inconsistent with the APA

**Documents:** Seller Promissory Note § 9.1; Asset Purchase Agreement § 12.8

**Issue.** The Note is governed by New York law (§ 9.1) with exclusive jurisdiction in New York courts (§ 9.2). The APA is governed by Delaware law (§ 12.8) with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (§ 12.9). While APA § 12.4 provides that the Note "shall control with respect to its payment, set-off and enforcement," the different governing laws create the risk of conflicting interpretations on issues that straddle both documents (e.g., the interaction between the set-off right in Note § 5 and the indemnification framework in APA Article IX).

**Why it matters.** If a dispute arises over whether a set-off is valid, a court in New York applying New York law might reach a different conclusion than a Delaware court applying Delaware law to the APA's indemnification provisions. This could lead to inconsistent outcomes or forum shopping.

**Recommendation.** Conform the Note's governing law and jurisdiction to Delaware, matching the APA. If Seller insists on New York (perhaps due to its own legal team's preference), at minimum add a provision that any dispute involving both the Note and the APA shall be resolved in the Delaware Court of Chancery under Delaware law.

## 9. Board Consent Does Not Specifically Authorize Assumption of Environmental Liabilities

**Documents:** Doureth Board Consent §§ 1.3, 1.6, 2; Asset Purchase Agreement § 2.3(c); Letter of Intent § 4

**Issue.** The Board consent approves the Purchase Agreement "substantially in the form provided to the Board" (§ 2). The consent describes the consideration (§ 1.3) and references the Board's review of "environmental... due diligence" (§ 1.6) but does not specifically mention or authorize the assumption of pre-Closing environmental liabilities. Given that the LOI (which likely formed the basis of the Board's review) provided that pre-Closing environmental liabilities would be retained by Seller, the executed APA's § 2.3(c) may fall outside the scope of what the Board approved.

**Why it matters.** If the assumption of environmental liabilities was not in the form presented to the Board, there is a corporate governance gap. In a future dispute (e.g., a shareholder derivative action after an environmental claim arises), the opposing party could argue the Board did not authorize this material obligation.

**Recommendation.** If § 2.3(c) is not amended (see Issue 1), obtain a supplemental Board consent or ratification specifically acknowledging and approving the assumption of pre-Closing environmental liabilities. If § 2.3(c) is amended to conform with the LOI, no further action is needed.

## 10. Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS) – License Gap at Closing

**Documents:** Integration Meeting Notes § 6.3; Action Item 7

**Issue.** The LIMS is "licensed to Routand enterprise-wide." A replacement license is being procured, and Routand has agreed to provide only "read-only access to historical batch data until the replacement is live." The action item to finalize the replacement license is due September 30, 2026 – one day before the target Closing.

**Why it matters.** Batch records are critical for quality control, regulatory compliance, and product traceability in a chemical manufacturing facility. If the replacement LIMS is not live at Closing, Doureth would have only read-only access to historical data and no system for recording new batches. This could create a compliance gap (e.g., inability to document GMP-compliant batch records) and operational disruption.

**Recommendation.** Make the LIMS replacement a condition to Closing (or at minimum a post-Closing covenant with a short deadline and specific remedies). Alternatively, negotiate a short-term license from Routand that includes full (not just read-only) access for a defined period (e.g., 90 days) after Closing, with Routand bearing the cost of any transition support.

## 11. Sampling Contractor for Q3 Discharge Monitoring – Continuity Risk

**Documents:** Integration Meeting Notes § 3.4; Action Item 2

**Issue.** The Q3 wastewater discharge monitoring report is due after Closing. Solberg-Reyes recommends retaining the current Routand sampling contractor for continuity. The action item is for Doureth Procurement to "confirm that the contract is on the Assigned Contracts list or arrange a replacement" by September 17, 2026.

**Why it matters.** If the sampling contractor's agreement is not on Schedule 2.1(c) (Assigned Contracts), it is an Excluded Asset and does not transfer to Doureth. Doureth would need to either (a) have Seller add it to the schedule before Closing, or (b) arrange a replacement contractor on short notice. A gap in sampling could result in a missed regulatory deadline and a compliance violation under the wastewater discharge permit.

**Recommendation.** Confirm immediately whether the sampling contractor agreement is on Schedule 2.1(c). If not, request that Seller add it before Closing, or enter into a short-term extension with the contractor directly (with Seller's consent if required). Ensure the compliance calendar handover (Action Item 1) specifically identifies this deadline.

## 12. Transitional Supply Letter – Monthly Maximum May Constrain Growth

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 2.3

**Issue.** § 2.3 caps Seller's supply obligation at 3,000 dry short tons per month "unless it agrees in writing." The integration notes state that historical consumption "runs comfortably above the quarterly minimum" (1,800 tons/quarter = 600 tons/month) but do not confirm whether it approaches the 3,000-ton monthly maximum.

**Why it matters.** If Doureth wins a new municipal contract or increases production after Closing, its caustic soda requirements could exceed 3,000 tons/month. Seller has no obligation to supply above that level without its written agreement, giving Seller leverage to demand a price increase or refuse additional volume.

**Recommendation.** Before Closing, obtain from Solberg-Reyes (via the clean team) the 12 months of feedstock receipt data (Action Item 3, due September 17) and confirm that projected volumes through the 24-month Term will not approach the 3,000-ton monthly cap. If there is any risk of exceeding it, negotiate a higher cap or a commitment that Seller will use commercially reasonable efforts to accommodate volumes up to a specified level above 3,000 tons/month.

## 13. Escrow Tax Treatment – Doureth Pays Tax on Earnings of Funds That May Be Released to Seller

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 8.1; Closing Funds Flow Memo § 6.4

**Issue.** § 8.1 provides that "Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds, and all earnings on them are reported as income of Buyer, until disbursement." If no claims are made, the full $8,640,000 (plus earnings) is released to Seller at 9 months. Doureth would have reported the interest/earnings as income but would not receive the principal.

**Why it matters.** This is a modest but real tax inefficiency. On $8.64 million in a money market fund, even at conservative rates, the earnings could be several hundred thousand dollars of taxable income to Doureth that ultimately benefits Seller.

**Recommendation.** This is a minor point and likely not worth significant negotiation capital. If raised, suggest that earnings be allocated proportionally: Buyer keeps earnings attributable to the period the funds were held as security for its claims, and Seller receives earnings after the Release Date. Alternatively, accept as-is given the small dollar amount relative to the deal.
