## Issue 1 – Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities: APA Departs from LOI

**Documents:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 2.3(c); Letter of Intent § 4

**What the documents say:**

- **LOI § 4** states: "All pre-Closing environmental liabilities will be retained by Routand. These are all liabilities under environmental laws arising out of or relating to the ownership or operation of the Business, the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility before the Closing, including liabilities for any release of hazardous materials at, on, under or from the Lake Charles Facility occurring before the Closing and any related investigation or remediation, whether known or unknown at the Closing. Routand will indemnify Doureth for those liabilities."

- **APA § 2.3(c)** provides that Buyer assumes "all Liabilities arising under Environmental Laws relating to the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility, **whether arising prior to, at or after the Closing**."

**Why it matters:**

The APA directly contradicts the LOI. Under the APA as drafted, Doureth takes on 100% of historical environmental liability at the Lake Charles Facility—legacy solvent contamination, PFAS (expressly included in the "Hazardous Materials" definition in APA § 1.1), chlor-alkali process waste, and any other pre-Closing condition. Because these are *Assumed Liabilities*, they are not *Excluded Liabilities*, so Seller's indemnity for Excluded Liabilities (APA § 9.2(c)) does not apply. Doureth's only protection is the environmental representation in APA § 4.12, which is a General Representation subject to the Basket, the Cap, and—critically—the escrow-as-sole-source limitation (see Issue 2 below).

The § 4.12(c) representation is also narrow: it covers only releases "in violation of Environmental Laws **or** requiring investigation or remediation." A historical condition that does not technically violate a law or trigger a regulatory response is not a rep breach, yet Doureth still owns the liability under § 2.3(c).

**Recommended fix:**

Amend APA § 2.3(c) to exclude pre-Closing environmental liabilities, consistent with the LOI. Alternatively, if Seller insists on the current structure, add a specific environmental indemnity in Article IX (outside the General Rep framework) that covers all pre-Closing environmental conditions, is not subject to the Basket or Cap, and is secured by the escrow for the full 18-month survival period (or longer, given the long-tail nature of environmental claims). At minimum, extend the environmental rep survival to match the applicable statute of limitations (as is done for Fundamental Reps in § 9.1).

## Issue 2 – Escrow Release at 9 Months Renders 18-Month Survival Illusory for General Reps

**Documents:** APA § 9.1 (survival); APA § 9.4(b) (sole source of recovery); Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 4.1 (Release Date)

**What the documents say:**

- **APA § 9.1:** General Representations survive **18 months** after the Closing Date.
- **APA § 9.4(b):** "The Escrow Fund shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** for the Buyer Parties for any Losses under Section 9.2(a), other than Losses arising from a breach of any Fundamental Representation or from fraud."
- **Escrow Agreement § 4.1:** The Release Date is **9 months** after the Closing Date. On that date, all remaining Escrow Funds (less amounts retained for pending claims) are released to Seller.

**Why it matters:**

The General Representations (which include the environmental rep in § 4.12, the financial statements rep in § 4.5, the material contracts rep in § 4.7, the condition of assets rep in § 4.8, and others) nominally survive for 18 months. But because the escrow is the *sole and exclusive* remedy for their breach, and the escrow is released at month 9, Doureth has **no recovery** for any General Rep breach discovered between months 9 and 18. The 18-month survival period is effectively 9 months.

This is particularly acute for the environmental rep (see Issue 1). Environmental conditions often manifest slowly—groundwater plumes, emerging contaminants like PFAS, and latent soil contamination may not be identified within 9 months.

**Recommended fix:**

Either (a) extend the escrow Release Date to 18 months to align with the General Rep survival period, or (b) amend APA § 9.4(b) to provide that, after the Release Date, the Buyer Parties may recover directly from Seller for General Rep breaches (i.e., delete or qualify the "sole and exclusive source" language for the post-release period). Option (a) is cleaner but Seller will resist holding $8.64M for 18 months. A compromise: release 50% of the escrow at 9 months and the remaining 50% at 18 months.

## Issue 3 – Seller Note Governed by New York Law; Jurisdictional Split

**Documents:** Seller Promissory Note § 9.1–9.2; APA § 12.8–12.9; APA § 12.4 (order of precedence)

**What the documents say:**

- **APA § 12.8:** Delaware law governs the APA.
- **APA § 12.9:** Exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery.
- **Note § 9.1:** New York law governs the Note.
- **Note § 9.2:** Exclusive jurisdiction in New York state courts (Manhattan) or the SDNY.
- **APA § 12.4:** The APA prevails over Ancillary Agreements, "provided, however, that the Seller Note shall control with respect to its payment, set-off and enforcement."

**Why it matters:**

Any dispute involving the Seller Note's payment, set-off, or enforcement (which includes the indemnity set-off under APA § 9.7 and Note § 5) will be litigated in New York under New York law, while all other disputes under the APA go to the Delaware Court of Chancery under Delaware law. This creates:

- A risk of inconsistent rulings on interrelated issues (e.g., whether a set-off is proper may implicate both the APA's indemnity provisions and the Note's set-off mechanics).
- Increased cost and complexity if Doureth needs to pursue claims in both forums simultaneously.
- New York law may interpret set-off rights, acceleration, or subordination differently than Delaware law.

**Recommended fix:**

Conform the Note's governing law and jurisdiction to Delaware (matching the APA). If Seller insists on New York law for the Note (perhaps for its own internal reasons), at minimum add a provision that the Note's set-off and subordination provisions are to be construed consistently with Article IX of the APA, and that the parties will seek to file related actions in the same forum where procedurally permissible.

## Issue 4 – Transitional Supply Letter: One-Sided Assignment Right

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 7

**What the document says:**

"Seller may assign this letter agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under it, to **any Person** without the consent of Buyer. Seller shall give Buyer written notice of the assignment within ten (10) Business Days after it becomes effective. Buyer may not assign this letter agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under it, without the prior written consent of Seller."

**Why it matters:**

Doureth is dependent on Routand for caustic soda feedstock for up to 24 months (potentially 36 with extensions). If Routand assigns the supply obligation to a third party—whether an affiliate, a competitor, or an entity with different operational standards—Doureth has no consent right and no ability to object. The assignee could have different quality controls, logistics capabilities, or financial stability. Meanwhile, Doureth is locked into minimum purchase volumes (1,800 tons/quarter) and cannot assign its own rights without Seller's consent.

**Recommended fix:**

Require Buyer's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment by Seller, or at minimum limit assignment to an Affiliate of Seller that has demonstrable chlor-alkali production capacity and agrees in writing to be bound by all terms of the letter. Make the assignment restriction mutual.

## Issue 5 – Transitional Supply Letter: No Remedy for Seller's Failure to Supply

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter §§ 2.4, 5, 6.1, 9

**What the documents say:**

- **§ 2.4** provides a liquidated damages remedy if *Buyer* under-orders (15% of Price × shortfall volume).
- There is **no equivalent remedy** if *Seller* fails to deliver the Product.
- **§ 5 (Force Majeure):** During a Force Majeure Event, Seller allocates available Product pro rata among all customers "including the Retained Business."
- **§ 9 (Limitation of Liability):** Neither party is liable for "consequential, incidental, indirect, special or punitive damages, or for **lost profits**."
- **§ 6.1:** Buyer may terminate for material breach, but termination does not provide a supply source.

**Why it matters:**

If Seller fails to supply (other than by Force Majeure), Doureth's remedies are limited to: (a) terminate the letter and find an alternative supplier (disruptive, potentially more expensive), or (b) claim direct damages. But the consequential damages waiver in § 9 likely bars recovery of the price differential if Doureth must buy replacement caustic soda at a higher market price, lost production, or customer penalties—these are all arguably consequential. The pro rata allocation during Force Majeure means the Retained Business (industrial cleaning chemicals) competes with the Business for the same caustic soda supply, and Doureth has no priority.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a liquidated damages or price-protection remedy for Seller's failure to supply (e.g., if Seller cannot deliver, it reimburses the difference between the contract Price and the price Doureth actually pays for replacement Product, up to a stated cap).
- Carve out "cost of cover" (the price differential for replacement purchases) from the consequential damages waiver in § 9.
- Give Buyer's firm orders *superior* priority over the Retained Business during Force Majeure, not merely "at least equal" priority under § 1.3.

## Issue 6 – Transitional Supply Letter: No Price Cap

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 3.1

**What the document says:**

"The price for the Product (the 'Price') for each calendar month is the average of the U.S. Gulf Coast liquid caustic soda contract price published by the pricing service named in Annex B for the prior month, less a discount of four percent (4%), plus the freight charges set out in Annex B."

**Why it matters:**

The Price is entirely formula-driven with no cap, collar, or floor. If caustic soda prices spike (due to energy costs, supply disruptions, or market dynamics), Doureth's feedstock costs increase proportionally with no limit. For a 24-month term with minimum quarterly purchases of 1,800 dry short tons, the exposure is material. The 4% discount is fixed and does not scale with price increases.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a price cap (e.g., no more than 110–115% of the trailing 12-month average of the index) or a collar. Alternatively, add a most-favored-customer provision: if Seller sells the same Product to any other customer (including the Retained Business) at a lower effective price during the Term, the Price to Buyer is reduced to match.

## Issue 7 – Seller Note Cross-Default: Senior Debt Acceleration Triggers Note Default

**Documents:** Seller Promissory Note § 7.1(d), § 7.2

**What the document says:**

- **§ 7.1(d):** An Event of Default occurs if "the Senior Indebtedness is accelerated following an event of default under the Senior Credit Agreement."
- **§ 7.2:** On an Event of Default under § 7.1(b) or (c) (bankruptcy), the entire Note is automatically accelerated. For other Events of Default (including (d)), the Holder "may, subject to Section 6, declare by written notice" the entire Note due.

**Why it matters:**

If Doureth experiences a technical default under its senior credit facility (e.g., a covenant breach that is curable, or a reporting delay) and the senior lender accelerates, the entire $12M Seller Note becomes payable on demand. This creates a cascading liquidity risk: a senior debt issue instantly converts $12M of long-term seller paper into a current obligation. The subordination in § 6 only blocks *scheduled* payments during a Blockage Notice; it does not prevent acceleration of the Note itself upon senior cross-default.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a cure period to § 7.1(d): the cross-default should apply only if the senior acceleration is not cured or waived within a specified period (e.g., 30–60 days). Alternatively, limit the consequence to a payment blockage (under § 6) rather than full acceleration, giving Doureth time to resolve the senior issue before the Seller Note becomes due.

## Issue 8 – Environmental Rep Survival vs. Nature of Environmental Risk

**Documents:** APA § 9.1; APA § 4.12; APA § 1.1 (Hazardous Materials definition)

**What the documents say:**

- General Representations (including § 4.12 Environmental Matters) survive **18 months** (APA § 9.1).
- The "Hazardous Materials" definition expressly includes "per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances" (PFAS) (APA § 1.1).
- The environmental rep in § 4.12(a) looks back only **five years** for compliance.

**Why it matters:**

PFAS and other emerging contaminants can have very long detection and manifestation timelines. A 18-month survival period (and, per Issue 2, an effective 9-month period) is inadequate for environmental risk. If PFAS contamination is discovered 20 months post-Closing, the rep has expired and Doureth has no claim—even though the contamination pre-dated Closing and was assumed by Doureth under § 2.3(c). The five-year look-back in § 4.12(a) also may not reach the full history of the facility.

**Recommended fix:**

Carve the environmental representation out of the General Rep category and give it a longer survival period (e.g., the full statute of limitations, matching the Fundamental Rep treatment in § 9.1), or make it a Fundamental Representation. Extend the look-back period to the full ownership history of the facility. Ensure the environmental indemnity (if added per Issue 1) is not subject to the standard survival period.

## Issue 9 – Escrow Claim Procedure: First-Come, First-Served with No Priority

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement § 3.5

**What the document says:**

"If the Escrow Funds are insufficient to pay a Claimed Amount in full, the Escrow Agent shall disburse the Escrow Funds then available. It has no further obligation with respect to that claim. Claims are paid from the Escrow Funds **in the order in which the Escrow Agent receives the Claim Notices**."

**Why it matters:**

If multiple indemnity claims arise and the escrow is insufficient (which is likely if a significant environmental issue emerges, given the $8.64M escrow against an $86.4M purchase price), claims are paid strictly by notice date. A small, early-noticed claim (e.g., a minor contract issue) could consume escrow funds that a larger, later-noticed environmental claim would need. There is no priority for environmental claims or for claims involving greater loss severity.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a priority provision: environmental claims and claims involving Excluded Liabilities (§ 9.2(c)) rank ahead of other General Rep claims. Alternatively, require that a minimum percentage of the escrow (e.g., 50%) be reserved for environmental claims until the Release Date.

## Issue 10 – Transitional Supply Letter: Buyer Forecast Information Disclosed to Competitor

**Documents:** Transitional Supply Side Letter § 2.1, § 8; APA § 6.5

**What the documents say:**

- **§ 2.1:** Buyer must deliver rolling six-month forecasts with "the demand data supporting it in reasonable detail," including "anticipated sodium hypochlorite production volumes and the municipal and industrial customer requirements driving those volumes" (defined as "Buyer Forecast Information").
- **§ 8:** Buyer Forecast Information is Seller's confidential information, protected for three years after the letter expires.
- **APA § 6.5:** Seller is restricted from competing in water treatment chemicals for five years, but the Retained Business (industrial cleaning chemicals) is carved out.

**Why it matters:**

Doureth is providing Routand with detailed, customer-level demand data for its water treatment business. While confidentiality is contractually protected, Routand remains a separate corporate entity with its own management, and the Retained Business (industrial cleaning chemicals) is not subject to the non-compete. If the Retained Business ever adjacent to water treatment (e.g., industrial cleaning for water treatment plants), the forecast data could be commercially valuable. The three-year confidentiality tail may also expire before the five-year non-compete, creating a window where the data is no longer protected but the non-compete still theoretically prevents direct competition.

**Recommended fix:**

- Limit the granularity of Buyer Forecast Information: require only volume data by product category and delivery month, not customer-level detail.
- Extend the confidentiality survival for Buyer Forecast Information to match the non-compete period (5 years post-Closing).
- Add a specific prohibition on using Buyer Forecast Information for the benefit of the Retained Business or any Affiliate.

## Issue 11 – Plant Director Employment Agreement: Good Reason Trigger Is Broad

**Documents:** Plant Director Employment Agreement § 5.3(b); APA § 7.2(e)

**What the document says:**

- **§ 5.3(b):** "Good Reason" includes "a material diminution in her title, authority or duties, **including removal as the senior manager responsible for the Lake Charles Facility**."
- **§ 6.2:** A Good Reason resignation triggers 12 months' base salary, prorated bonus, unpaid retention bonus, and COBRA.
- **APA § 7.2(e):** Solberg-Reyes's employment agreement is a condition to Closing.

**Why it matters:**

Post-Closing, if Doureth reorganizes its manufacturing operations (e.g., consolidates plant reporting under a regional VP, adds a new layer of management, or shifts certain duties to corporate), Solberg-Reyes could argue a "material diminution" and resign for Good Reason, triggering approximately $300K+ in severance (12 months' salary of $248K + bonus + retention bonus + COBRA). As Plant Director, she has intimate knowledge of the facility, its environmental compliance posture, and its operations—her departure would be operationally disruptive beyond the cash cost.

**Recommended fix:**

Narrow the Good Reason definition to exclude reorganizations that do not reduce her base compensation by more than a stated percentage (e.g., 10%) or that do not eliminate her direct responsibility for the Lake Charles Facility. Add a requirement that the diminution be "material and adverse" and that the Executive consult with the Company before resigning (beyond the existing notice/cure mechanics).

## Issue 12 – Integration Dependency: LIMS and Sampling Contractor

**Documents:** Integration Meeting Notes § 6.3, § 3.4

**What the notes say:**

- **§ 6.3:** "The laboratory information management system is licensed to Routand enterprise-wide. A replacement license is being procured, and Routand has agreed to provide read-only access to historical batch data until the replacement is live."
- **§ 3.4:** Solberg-Reyes recommended retaining the current Routand sampling contractor through year-end. The Director of Procurement will "confirm that the contract is on the Assigned Contracts list or arrange a replacement."

**Why it matters:**

- **LIMS:** If the replacement license is not live by Closing, Doureth has only *read-only* access to its own historical batch data. This could impair quality control, regulatory reporting, and customer compliance certifications in the immediate post-Closing period. The "agreement" to provide read-only access is informal (meeting notes, not a contractual commitment in the APA or any Ancillary Agreement).
- **Sampling contractor:** If the contract is not on the Assigned Contracts schedule (Schedule 2.1(c)), it is an Excluded Asset and does not transfer. Doureth would need to negotiate a new contract or rely on a short-term arrangement with no contractual protection.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add the LIMS transition (including read-only access and data migration) as a post-Closing covenant in the APA or a side letter, with a specified deadline and remedy for non-performance.
- Confirm before Closing that the sampling contractor agreement is on Schedule 2.1(c). If not, either add it or require Seller to enter a short-term extension through year-end as a Closing condition or post-Closing covenant.

## Issue 14 – APA § 2.4(d) vs. § 2.3(c): Product/Environmental Overlap

**Documents:** APA § 2.3(c); APA § 2.4(d)

**What the documents say:**

- **§ 2.3(c):** Buyer assumes "all Liabilities arising under Environmental Laws relating to the Purchased Assets or the Lake Charles Facility, whether arising prior to, at or after the Closing."
- **§ 2.4(d):** Seller retains "all Liabilities for product sold by the Business before the Closing."

**Why it matters:**

If a pre-Closing product (e.g., a batch of coagulant sold by Routand to a municipal customer) causes environmental harm post-Closing (e.g., contamination of a water treatment system), is that an "Environmental Liability relating to the Purchased Assets" (assumed by Buyer under § 2.3(c)) or a "Liability for product sold before the Closing" (retained by Seller under § 2.4(d))? The two provisions overlap and are potentially inconsistent. Seller will likely argue § 2.4(d) controls (product liability is retained). But the environmental harm "relates to" the product, which is a Purchased Asset category (inventory). This ambiguity could lead to a dispute over who bears the cost of remediation.

**Recommended fix:**

Add clarifying language to § 2.4(d): "For the avoidance of doubt, Liabilities for product sold by the Business before the Closing include any environmental Liabilities arising from the use, disposal, or Release of such product by any Person after the Closing." Alternatively, add a corresponding carve-out to § 2.3(c) excluding product-liability-based environmental claims for pre-Closing sales.

## Issue 15 – Outside Date May Be Inadequate for Permit Transfers

**Documents:** APA § 8.1(b); Integration Meeting Notes § 3.1

**What the documents say:**

- **APA § 8.1(b):** Either party may terminate if Closing has not occurred by **December 31, 2026**.
- **Meeting Notes § 3.1:** LDEQ permit transfer applications were filed August 21, 2026. "LDEQ has acknowledged receipt and has not raised any deficiency to date."
- **APA § 7.2(d):** Transfer of environmental permits (or issuance of replacements) is a condition to Buyer's Closing obligation.

**Why it matters:**

The target Closing is October 1, 2026. If LDEQ raises deficiencies or delays the permit transfers, the outside date of December 31 provides only about three months of runway. Louisiana DEQ permit transfers can take 60–120 days or longer, and if a replacement permit is needed (rather than a simple transfer), the timeline could be longer. If the permits are not transferred by December 31, either party can walk, and Doureth would have incurred significant transaction costs with no asset.

**Recommended fix:**

Extend the outside date to February 28, 2027 (or add a mechanism for automatic 30-day extensions if the only unsatisfied condition is the permit transfer, at Buyer's election). Alternatively, make the permit transfer a condition that can be waived by Buyer (it already is, as a condition to *Buyer's* obligation, but adding an explicit extension right provides more certainty).

**What the document says:**
