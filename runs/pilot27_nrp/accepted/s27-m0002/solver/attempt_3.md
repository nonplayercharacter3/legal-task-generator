### Issue 1: Glarol Has Missed the Contractual Deadline to Dispute the Closing Statement

**Documents:** Asset Purchase Agreement § 2.5(b)–(c); Glarol Board Consent §§ 1.C–1.D, 2 (Resolutions 1–2)

**Facts:**

- Seller's Closing Statement was dated July 17, 2026, and received by email at notices@glarol.com on **July 20, 2026** (Board Consent § 1.C).
- APA § 2.5(b) requires Buyer to deliver a Notice of Disagreement "within 30 days after Seller delivers the Closing Statement." Thirty days from July 20, 2026 is **August 19, 2026**.
- APA § 2.5(c) provides: "If Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement within the period specified in Section 2.5(b), the Closing Statement shall be **final and binding** on the parties."
- The Board Consent is dated **August 27, 2026**—eight days after the deadline expired.
- Board Consent § 1.D expressly acknowledges: "As of the date of this consent, the Company has **not delivered** a Notice of Disagreement or any other written objection to the Closing Statement to Seller."
- Resolutions 1 and 2 direct that a Notice of Disagreement be delivered "promptly following the date of this consent."

**Why it matters:** Under the plain language of § 2.5(c), the Closing Statement is now final and binding. Seller's stated Closing Inventory Value exceeds the Target Inventory Value (Schedule 2.5), meaning Glarol owes Seller an upward adjustment under § 2.5(d). Management estimates the overstatement at approximately $1,900,000 (Board Consent § 1.D), but we do not have Schedule 2.5 and cannot verify the exact figure. If the Closing Statement is truly final, Glarol is contractually obligated to pay the adjustment within five Business Days of "final determination"—which, under § 2.5(c), has already occurred by operation of the missed deadline.

**Potential arguments (uncertain):**

- The "final and binding" language is unqualified. There is no fraud carve-out in § 2.5(c) itself.
- § 9.7 (Exclusive Remedy) carves out fraud, and § 9.5(c) removes the indemnity cap for fraud. If Seller's assay results or pricing references were knowingly false, a fraud claim under § 9.2(a) might survive. But this is a high bar and would require evidence of intentional misrepresentation, not mere error.
- Equitable doctrines (unconscionability, mutual mistake) are possible but fact-intensive and uncertain under Delaware law.
- If Seller has not yet demanded payment, there is an argument that Seller's acceptance of the status quo or course of dealing could support a waiver, but this cuts both ways.

**Recommendation:** We need a call with the client **before** delivering the Notice of Disagreement directed by the Board. Delivering it now does not cure the missed deadline under the contract's text, but it preserves the record and may support equitable arguments. The client needs to understand that the contractual right to dispute has likely lapsed and that the $1.9 million (or whatever the actual adjustment is) may be owed. We should also assess whether there is evidence supporting a fraud or willful-misrepresentation theory, which would be the strongest path to recovery. I recommend we speak with the client's CFO and the outside accounting advisors referenced in the Board Consent to understand the strength of the factual basis before we commit to a position.

### Issue 2: Nine-Month Environmental Survival Period Combined with the Sampling Restriction Creates a Coverage Gap

**Documents:** APA §§ 7.6(a), 7.6(c), 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 1.1 ("Release Date")

**Facts:**

- APA § 9.1: Environmental Representations (Section 4.12) survive only **nine (9) months** after the Closing Date. Closing was June 1, 2026, so the survival period expires **March 1, 2027**.
- APA § 7.6(a): Seller may pursue the Permit Transfer Approval for up to **twelve (12) months** after Closing (i.e., until June 1, 2027). Until then, Seller remains the permittee of record and Glarol operates as Seller's "designated operator."
- APA § 7.6(c): Before the Permit Transfer Approval is obtained, Glarol and its consultants are **prohibited from conducting any invasive or subsurface sampling** at the Facility. This includes soil borings, groundwater monitoring well installation, sub-slab vapor sampling, and sampling of process sumps or containment areas. Only "visual inspections and non-invasive Phase I-type review" are permitted.
- Escrow Agreement § 1.1: The "Release Date" (when the $4.2M escrow is released to Seller, less pending claims) is the first Business Day after nine months from Closing—i.e., approximately **March 2, 2027**.

**Why it matters:** This is the most structurally dangerous combination in the deal documents. Glarol is operating a hazardous-waste facility (lithium compounds, cobalt, nickel, electrolyte solvents, fluorinated compounds, heavy metals—see the "Hazardous Materials" definition in § 1.1) but is contractually barred from performing the very sampling that would reveal subsurface contamination, groundwater migration, or process-sump residues. Meanwhile, the window to assert an environmental claim against Seller closes in nine months, and the escrow security disappears at the same time.

In practical terms: if a soil or groundwater contamination issue exists at the Facility, Glarol may be unable to *discover* it within the survival period because it cannot sample. Even if a visual inspection raises a red flag, Glarol cannot confirm the extent of the problem without invasive testing. By the time the Permit Transfer Approval is obtained (potentially 12 months post-Closing) and sampling becomes permissible, the environmental reps will have expired and the escrow will have been released.

**Recommendation:**

- Flag this to the client as a structural gap that was baked into the deal. It cannot be fixed retroactively, but it informs how aggressively Glarol should pursue non-invasive assessments now (Phase I review, review of historical sampling data delivered under § 4.12(e), regulatory records, and any available NDEP inspection reports).
- If the Permit Transfer Approval is not obtained before March 1, 2027, consider whether a claim can be asserted based on a *reasonable suspicion* identified through non-invasive means, even if the full extent is not yet quantified. The Claim Notice under § 9.4 requires the claim to be "reasonably estimable" in amount, which may be difficult without sampling data.
- Going forward, in any renegotiation or amendment, seek to (a) extend the environmental survival period to at least 18–24 months, (b) carve out the sampling restriction for purposes of environmental due diligence, or (c) require Seller to fund a pre-Closing environmental assessment that would survive the sampling restriction.

### Issue 3: Pollution Legal Liability Policy Is Claims-Made, the Policy Has Not Been Delivered, and the Retroactive Date Is Unknown

**Documents:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter §§ 2–3; APA § 2.2 (Seller retains pre-Closing environmental liabilities)

**Facts:**

- The Pollution Legal Liability (PLL) policy is written on a **claims-made** basis with a three-year term (June 1, 2026 to June 1, 2029) (Cover Letter § 2, table).
- The cover letter states: "Its terms and conditions, including the **retroactive date**, any **exclusions for known conditions**, and the reporting requirements, are set out in the policy itself. We will deliver the policy to you when the insurer issues it." (Cover Letter § 3.)
- As of the date of the cover letter (June 3, 2026), only **binders** are in place. The actual policy forms have not been delivered.
- APA § 2.2 provides that Seller retains all liabilities arising under Environmental Laws from conditions existing at or before the Closing.

**Why it matters:**

- If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026 (the policy effective date), the PLL policy will **not** cover claims arising from pre-existing contamination at the Facility. This is the most common structure for claims-made pollution policies, and it would mean Glarol's primary environmental insurance does not protect it from the exact risk (legacy contamination) that the APA allocates to Seller.
- Even if the retroactive date is earlier (e.g., the date of Seller's prior policy or the date of first occupancy), the "known conditions" exclusion could carve out any contamination that was identifiable from the Phase I/II assessments or regulatory records that Seller delivered under § 4.12(e).
- Because the policy has not been delivered, Glarol is operating without confirmed knowledge of its actual environmental coverage. The binder may differ from the final policy form.
- The CGL policy is an occurrence form, but standard CGL policies exclude pollution. The PLL is therefore Glarol's primary (and possibly only) source of insurance for environmental liability.

**Recommendation:**

- **Urgent:** Follow up with Harwick & Doyle to obtain the final PLL policy form immediately. Do not rely on the binder.
- Once received, review the retroactive date, the known-conditions exclusion, the definition of "pollution condition," and the reporting requirements in detail.
- If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026, assess whether a "prior acts" or "retroactive date buy-back" endorsement is available, or whether Seller should be required to maintain a tail or run-off policy covering pre-Closing conditions.
- The client should understand that if a pre-existing contamination issue is discovered, the PLL policy may not respond, and the indemnity claim against Seller must be made within the nine-month survival period (see Issue 2).

### Issue 4: Ostrander Fleet Recovery LLC Feedstock Supply Agreement – Consent Not Obtained, No Price Adjustment Available

**Documents:** Bill of Sale, Annex A, Item 3; APA §§ 2.1(c), 2.6; Schedule 2.1(c), Item 3

**Facts:**

- The Feedstock Supply Agreement with Ostrander Fleet Recovery LLC is listed as an Assigned Contract (Schedule 2.1(c), Item 3). It is the supply of end-of-life electric vehicle battery packs—presumably the primary feedstock for the business.
- Bill of Sale, Annex A, Item 3: "The agreement requires Ostrander Fleet Recovery LLC's prior written consent to any assignment of the agreement or any change of control of Seller. **Status: consent not obtained as of the Closing.**"
- APA § 2.6: For 90 days after Closing (i.e., until approximately **August 30, 2026**), Seller shall use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain the consent. Seller is "not required to pay any consent fee or commence any litigation."
- APA § 2.6 (final sentence): "The failure to obtain any such consent shall **not entitle Buyer to any adjustment of the Purchase Price**."

**Why it matters:**

- Without the Ostrander feedstock supply, Glarol's core business (converting end-of-life battery packs into black mass) is impaired. This is not a peripheral contract; it is the input side of the operation.
- The 90-day consent period expired (or is expiring imminently) around August 30, 2026. The documents in the folder do not indicate whether consent was ultimately obtained.
- Even if consent is obtained, the "commercially reasonable efforts" standard is low. Seller had no obligation to pay a consent fee or litigate. If Ostrander demands a fee or refuses consent, Seller can walk away with no consequence to Glarol.
- Glarol has no price adjustment, no specific performance right, and no termination right if the consent is not obtained. The only remedy is the general covenant indemnity under § 9.2(b) (breach of covenant), subject to the $465,000 basket and the $6,975,000 cap.

**Recommendation:**

- Confirm immediately whether Ostrander's consent was obtained by the 90-day deadline. If not, assess whether Seller's efforts met the "commercially reasonable" standard and whether a covenant breach claim is viable.
- Evaluate the business impact: Does Glarol have alternative feedstock sources? What is the revenue at risk?
- If the consent was not obtained and the supply agreement is critical, consider whether the economic loss (lost feedstock, reduced throughput) exceeds the $465,000 basket and whether a claim against the escrow is warranted.
- Going forward, ensure that any amendment or side letter with Ostrander does not further limit Glarol's position.

### Issue 6: Split Governing Law and Forum Between the APA and the Escrow Agreement

**Documents:** APA §§ 11.8–11.9, 11.12; Escrow Agreement § 12.1

**Facts:**

- APA: Governed by **Delaware** law; exclusive jurisdiction in the **Delaware Court of Chancery** (or other Delaware courts) (APA §§ 11.8–11.9).
- Escrow Agreement: Governed by **New York** law; exclusive jurisdiction in **Manhattan** state and federal courts (Escrow Agreement § 12.1).
- APA § 11.12 (Order of Precedence): The APA controls over Ancillary Documents, "except that the Escrow Agreement shall control all matters relating to the Escrow Fund, including disputes regarding any Claim Notice."

**Why it matters:**

- A dispute over whether a Claim Notice is valid, whether the Escrow Agent should disburse, or whether a claim is "pending" for purposes of the Release Date will be governed by New York law and litigated in Manhattan.
- A dispute over the underlying indemnification obligation (e.g., whether a representation was breached) will be governed by Delaware law and litigated in the Court of Chancery.
- This creates the risk of inconsistent rulings, duplicative litigation, and strategic complications (e.g., Seller could file first in Manhattan to shape the escrow dispute on New York law while Glarol's Delaware forum is reserved for the underlying merits).

**Recommendation:**

- In any claim strategy, coordinate the escrow proceedings (New York) with the underlying indemnification claim (Delaware). Consider whether to file the Claim Notice in a way that preserves the Delaware forum for the merits while triggering the New York escrow process.
- This is not something we can fix in the documents at this point, but it should inform litigation strategy.

### Issue 10: PLL Policy Renewal Risk After Three-Year Term

**Document:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter § 2

**Facts:** The PLL policy term is June 1, 2026 to June 1, 2029. It is claims-made.

**Why it matters:** If Glarol does not renew the PLL policy on or before June 1, 2029, any claim first made after that date—even for a pollution condition that occurred during the policy period—will not be covered. **Recommendation:** Calendar the renewal date. Begin the renewal process well in advance (the broker has committed to contacting 90 days before expiration per Cover Letter § 6). Consider whether a "prior acts" endorsement or extended reporting period (tail) should be negotiated at renewal.
