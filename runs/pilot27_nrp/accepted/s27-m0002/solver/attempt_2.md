## Issue 1 – Missed Deadline to Dispute the Closing Statement

**Documents:** APA § 2.5(b)–(c); Board Consent, Recitals C–D and Resolution 2

**Facts.** Seller delivered its Closing Statement by email on July 20, 2026 (Board Consent, Recital C). Section 2.5(b) gives Buyer 30 calendar days to deliver a Notice of Disagreement. That deadline expired on **August 19, 2026**. The Board Consent is dated **August 27, 2026** and expressly states that "the Company has not delivered a Notice of Disagreement or any other written objection to the Closing Statement to Seller" (Recital D). Resolution 2 authorizes delivery of the Notice "promptly following the date of this consent" — i.e., on or after August 27.

**Why it matters.** Section 2.5(c) provides that if Buyer does not deliver a timely Notice of Disagreement, "the Closing Statement shall be final and binding on the parties." Management estimates the Closing Statement overstates inventory value by approximately $1.9 million (Recital D). If the Statement is final, Glarol owes that amount (or the full upward adjustment Seller calculated) to Seller under § 2.5(d), payable within five Business Days of "final determination."

More broadly, acceptance of the Closing Statement as final may undermine any subsequent indemnification claim for breach of the inventory representation in § 4.9. Seller will argue that by letting the Statement become final, Glarol contractually agreed to the inventory value and cannot now claim the representation was inaccurate. While § 4.9 is not a Fundamental Representation and technically survives until March 1, 2027 (§ 9.1), the practical enforceability of a § 9.2(a) claim after the parties have resolved the inventory value through the § 2.5 mechanism is doubtful.

**Recommended fix.** There is no clean contractual fix. Options to explore immediately:

- Determine whether Seller failed to provide the "reasonable access to the books, records, samples and personnel" required by § 2.5(b) during the 30-day window. If access was inadequate, there may be an argument that the deadline was tolled or that Seller waived strict compliance. This is fact-dependent.
- Assess whether the Closing Statement itself was deficient under § 2.5(a) (e.g., lacked "reasonable supporting detail"). The Board Consent says it included assay summaries and pricing references, so this argument is weak on the current record.
- If no procedural defect exists, consider whether a commercial resolution is available (e.g., Seller agrees to reopen in exchange for a concession on another open item, such as the Ostrander consent — see Issue 2).

**⚠️ FLAG – Partner attention and client call required before any response to Seller.** The Board Consent as drafted authorizes a late Notice that will likely be rejected. We need to confirm with the client (i) whether any access issues occurred during the 30-day window, (ii) whether any informal communications with Seller preserved the dispute, and (iii) the client's risk tolerance for proceeding on the assumption that the Statement is final. We should not deliver the Notice under Resolution 2 without first assessing whether doing so prejudices any alternative theories (e.g., it could be characterized as an admission that the deadline was missed, foreclosing a laches or waiver argument against Seller).

## Issue 2 – Ostrander Feedstock Supply Agreement: Consent Not Obtained, No Price Adjustment

**Documents:** Bill of Sale, Annex A, Item 3; APA § 2.6; APA § 4.8; Schedule 2.1(c), Item 3

**Facts.** The Feedstock Supply Agreement with Ostrander Fleet Recovery LLC (supply of end-of-life EV battery packs) requires Ostrander's prior written consent to assignment. As of the Closing (June 1, 2026), consent had **not** been obtained (Bill of Sale, Annex A, Item 3). Under § 2.6, Seller has 90 days post-Closing (through approximately **August 30, 2026**) to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain consent. Critically, § 2.6 states: "The failure to obtain any such consent shall not entitle Buyer to any adjustment of the Purchase Price."

**Why it matters.** The feedstock supply is the input to the entire recycling operation. Without it, the Business model is impaired. Glarol is paying $46.5 million for a package that includes this contract, but if Ostrander withholds consent, Glarol receives no monetary relief. Seller's obligation is only "commercially reasonable efforts" — it need not pay a consent fee or litigate (§ 2.6). The 90-day window is expiring imminently.

**Recommended fix.**

- Confirm with the client whether Seller has made any progress on the Ostrander consent. The 90-day period ends around August 30, 2026.
- If consent is not obtained, evaluate whether the loss of the feedstock agreement constitutes a material adverse effect or a breach of § 4.8 (which represents that each Assigned Contract is "valid and in full force and effect"). The argument is that a contract requiring consent to assignment that has not been consented to is not effectively assigned, but § 4.8 speaks to the contract's status, not its assignability.
- Consider whether the failure to obtain consent, combined with the no-price-adjustment language, gives rise to a claim under § 9.2(b) (breach of covenant) if Seller did not use "commercially reasonable efforts." This is fact-intensive.
- Strategically, this issue may provide leverage in a commercial resolution of Issue 1.

**⚠️ FLAG – Time-sensitive.** The 90-day period is expiring. We should request a status update from Seller this week.

## Issue 3 – Environmental Representations: 9-Month Survival Combined with 12-Month Sampling Restriction

**Documents:** APA § 9.1; APA § 7.6(a) and (c); APA § 4.12

**Facts.** The Environmental Representations (§ 4.12) survive for only **9 months** after Closing — until **March 1, 2027** (§ 9.1). Meanwhile, § 7.6(c) prohibits Buyer and its consultants from conducting "any invasive or subsurface sampling at the Facility" (soil borings, groundwater wells, sub-slab vapor sampling, process sump sampling) until the Permit Transfer Approval is obtained. Under § 7.6(a), Seller may pursue the transfer for up to **12 months** after Closing — until **June 1, 2027**.

**Why it matters.** This creates a structural gap:

| Period | Can Glarol sample invasively? | Are Environmental Reps alive? |
|---|---|---|
| June 1, 2026 – March 1, 2027 (months 1–9) | **No** | Yes |
| March 1, 2027 – June 1, 2027 (months 10–12) | **No** (if permit not yet transferred) | **No** |

For the first 9 months, Glarol is contractually protected by the reps but cannot investigate the site to discover whether they have been breached. After month 9, the reps expire, and Glarol may still be unable to sample for up to 3 additional months. By the time Glarol can investigate (month 12 at the latest), it has no indemnification recourse for pre-Closing environmental conditions (except for fraud, which is a high bar).

For a facility handling lithium compounds, cobalt, nickel, electrolyte solvents, and fluorinated compounds (defined as Hazardous Materials in § 1.1), this is a significant exposure.

**Recommended fix.**

- This is a deal-structure issue that cannot be unilaterally fixed now. However, we should:
  - Document the restriction clearly in our file and advise the client of the risk.
  - Monitor the permit transfer timeline. If NDEP signals the transfer will take the full 12 months, the risk is crystallized.
  - Ensure the PLL insurance (see Issue 4) is structured to cover pre-existing conditions, as the indemnity may be unavailable by the time conditions are discovered.
  - If the client is amenable, consider negotiating a side letter with Seller extending the environmental survival period to 12 months or tying it to the permit transfer date, in exchange for a concession on the Closing Statement (Issue 1).

## Issue 4 – Pollution Legal Liability Insurance: Unknown Retroactive Date and Terms

**Documents:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, §§ 2–3; APA § 4.12

**Facts.** The PLL policy (Caldera Environmental Insurance Company) is **claims-made**, with a 3-year term (June 1, 2026 – June 1, 2029) and $10M limits. The cover letter states: "Its terms and conditions, including the **retroactive date**, any **exclusions for known conditions**, and the **reporting requirements**, are set out in the policy itself. We will deliver the policy to you when the insurer issues it."

The full policy has **not** been delivered. Only binders are in place.

**Why it matters.**

- **Retroactive date:** If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026 (the effective date), the policy does **not** cover conditions that existed before Closing. Pre-existing contamination at a hazardous waste facility is precisely the risk the Environmental Representations are meant to address. If those reps are time-barred (Issue 3) and the PLL policy excludes pre-existing conditions, Glarol has no coverage and no indemnity for the most likely environmental scenario.
- **Known conditions exclusion:** If Seller disclosed certain conditions in the Disclosure Schedule, the insurer may exclude them. We do not yet know the scope of this exclusion.
- **Claims-made mechanics:** A claim must be both made and reported during the policy period. If a condition is discovered in year 3 but not reported until after June 1, 2029, coverage is lost. The 3-year term is relatively short for a facility with this risk profile.
- **Policy not yet in hand:** Glarol is operating a hazardous waste facility on the strength of binders. If a loss occurs before the full policy is issued, there could be a coverage dispute.

**Recommended fix.**

- Request the full PLL policy from Harwick & Doyle immediately. Do not wait for "ordinary course."
- Upon receipt, review the retroactive date, known conditions exclusion, reporting requirements, and any prior-knowledge or prior-ownership exclusions.
- If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026, negotiate with the insurer (through the broker) for a retroactive date that precedes Closing, or at minimum covers the Seller's ownership period. This may require a premium adjustment.
- Evaluate whether a 3-year claims-made term is adequate. If the permit transfer takes 12 months and environmental issues surface in months 10–12 (when reps have lapsed), the policy must still be in force and the claim must be reportable. Confirm the policy's "prior acts" or "extended reporting" provisions.
- Advise the client to calendar the policy expiration (June 1, 2029) well in advance and to begin renewal discussions no later than Q1 2029 to avoid a gap.

**⚠️ FLAG – Partner attention.** The retroactive date question is existential for the client's environmental risk posture. If the policy does not reach back to pre-Closing conditions, the client's primary protections (indemnity + insurance) may both fail for the most probable loss scenario. We should confirm with the client whether they understood the PLL policy to cover pre-existing conditions when they agreed to the deal.

## Issue 5 – Escrow Release Aligned with Survival Expiration

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 1.1 ("Release Date"), § 6.1; APA § 9.1, § 2.4, § 9.6

**Facts.** The Release Date is "the first Business Day after the date that is nine (9) months following the Closing Date" (Escrow Agreement § 1.1) — approximately **March 2, 2027**. On that date, the Escrow Agent releases the remaining Escrow Fund to Seller, less pending claims (§ 6.1). The $4.2M escrow is the **primary source of recovery** under APA § 9.6 ("Buyer Indemnitees shall first seek recovery of Losses under Section 9.2(a) from the Escrow Fund").

**Why it matters.** The escrow and the environmental survival period expire simultaneously. Any environmental claim must be filed (via Claim Notice) before March 2, 2027 to preserve escrow funds. But under Issue 3, Glarol may not be able to investigate the site until as late as June 1, 2027. The window to both discover and assert an environmental claim against the escrow is therefore extremely narrow — potentially nonexistent if the permit transfer takes the full 12 months.

Additionally, the escrow ($4.2M) is less than the non-fundamental indemnity cap ($6.975M under § 9.5(b)). If a claim exceeds $4.2M, Glarol must pursue Seller directly for the balance, which introduces collection risk.

**Recommended fix.**

- Calendar the Release Date (March 2, 2027) as a hard deadline for filing any and all indemnification claims against the escrow.
- If the permit transfer is not expected before March 2027, consider whether to negotiate an extension of the escrow release tied to the permit transfer date. This would require Seller's agreement and an amendment to the Escrow Agreement (which needs all three parties' signatures under § 12.4).
- Assess Seller's post-Closing financial capacity to satisfy claims above the escrow amount. If Seller is a shell or has distributed its assets, the gap between the escrow and the cap is theoretical.

## Issue 6 – Indemnity Cap and Basket May Be Inadequate for Environmental Exposure

**Documents:** APA § 9.5(a)–(b)

**Facts.** The indemnity basket (threshold) is $465,000 (1% of the Purchase Price). The cap for non-fundamental representation breaches is $6,975,000 (15% of the Purchase Price). The overall cap is the Purchase Price ($46.5M). Environmental Representations are **not** Fundamental Representations, so the $6.975M cap applies.

**Why it matters.** For a hazardous waste facility handling lithium, cobalt, nickel, and fluorinated compounds, a single significant environmental event (e.g., a groundwater plume requiring remediation, a NDEP enforcement action, or a third-party CERCLA claim) can easily exceed $7 million. The cap limits Glarol's recovery to $6.975M regardless of actual loss, and the first $465,000 is borne entirely by Glarol.

**Recommended fix.**

- This is a negotiated term and cannot be unilaterally changed. However:
  - Confirm the client's environmental insurance program (including the PLL policy) is calibrated to fill the gap above the indemnity cap.
  - If the PLL policy's retroactive date is adequate (see Issue 4), the insurance should cover losses above the cap. If not, the client bears uninsured environmental risk above $6.975M with no recourse against Seller.
  - Document the client's acceptance of this risk for the file.

## Issue 7 – Governing Law Mismatch Between APA and Escrow Agreement

**Documents:** APA § 11.8 (Delaware); Escrow Agreement § 12.1 (New York)

**Facts.** The APA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (§ 11.9). The Escrow Agreement is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan state and federal courts (§ 12.1). APA § 11.12 provides that the Escrow Agreement "shall control all matters relating to the Escrow Fund, including disputes regarding any Claim Notice."

**Why it matters.** If a dispute arises over whether a Claim Notice was timely, whether a claim is covered by the escrow, or how the Escrow Fund should be disbursed, New York law and New York courts govern. If the dispute is about the underlying indemnification right (e.g., whether a representation was breached), Delaware law and Delaware courts govern. This split creates the possibility of:

- Inconsistent legal standards (e.g., New York and Delaware may interpret "reasonable detail" in a Claim Notice differently).
- Parallel or sequential litigation in two forums.
- Strategic complications if Glarol needs to both assert an indemnity claim (Delaware) and enforce escrow disbursement (New York).

**Recommended fix.**

- This is a low-probability but high-complexity risk. No immediate action required, but:
  - Be mindful of the split when drafting any Claim Notice — ensure it satisfies both the APA's requirements (§ 9.4) and the Escrow Agreement's requirements (§ 5.1) to avoid a technical deficiency in either forum.
  - If a dispute develops, engage New York counsel early for the escrow component.

## Issue 8 – Insurance Policy Forms Not Yet Delivered; Operating on Binders Only

**Documents:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, § 2

**Facts.** The cover letter states: "The insurers have issued binders, and policy forms will follow in the ordinary course." As of the date of the letter (June 3, 2026), only binders are in place for all six policies, including the CGL, umbrella, workers' compensation, and commercial property policies.

**Why it matters.** Binders are temporary evidence of coverage. They do not contain the full terms, conditions, exclusions, or definitions of the policies. If a loss occurs before the full policy forms are issued, there is a risk of a coverage dispute over terms not reflected in the binder. For a newly acquired hazardous waste facility, the first policy period is also the period of highest uncertainty (unknown pre-existing conditions, transitional operations under the permit arrangement).

**Recommended fix.**

- Follow up with Harwick & Doyle to confirm when full policy forms will be issued. Set a target of no more than 30 days from the binder date.
- Upon receipt, review all policies (not just the PLL) for relevant exclusions, conditions, and endorsements.
- Confirm that the commercial property policy's Statement of Values reflects the actual replacement cost of the acquired equipment.
