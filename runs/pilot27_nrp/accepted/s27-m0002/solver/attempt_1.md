### 1. CRITICAL – Missed Notice of Disagreement Deadline on Closing Statement

**Documents:** APA §2.5(b)–(c); Glarol Board Consent, Recitals C–D and Resolution 2.

**What the documents say:**

- APA §2.5(b): Buyer may dispute the Closing Statement by delivering a Notice of Disagreement "within 30 days after Seller delivers the Closing Statement."
- APA §2.5(c): "If Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement within the period specified in Section 2.5(b), the Closing Statement shall be final and binding on the parties."
- Board Consent, Recital C: The Closing Statement was "received by email on July 20, 2026 at the Company's notice address, notices@glarol.com."
- Board Consent, Recital D: "As of the date of this consent [August 27, 2026], the Company has not delivered a Notice of Disagreement or any other written objection to the Closing Statement to Seller."

**Why it matters:** The 30-day window ran from July 20 to August 19, 2026. The Board did not authorize the dispute until August 27—eight days after the deadline expired. On the face of the APA, the Closing Statement is now "final and binding." Seller's statement asserts an upward adjustment (Glarol owes Seller). Management's analysis estimates the overstatement at approximately $1,900,000. If the deadline is enforced, Glarol is contractually bound to pay the adjustment as calculated by Seller.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Immediate:** Call with the client to confirm the exact timeline—when the Closing Statement was received, when management first flagged the discrepancy, and why the Board consent was not executed before August 19. Determine whether any interim communication (email, phone call, partial objection) was sent to Seller before August 19 that could constitute a tolling event or partial compliance.
- **Legal analysis:** Assess whether (a) the email delivery on July 20 constitutes valid "delivery" under §11.2 (it was sent to the notice address, so likely yes); (b) any equitable tolling or waiver argument is available (e.g., did Seller's supporting detail arrive late or incompletely, extending the practical review period?); (c) whether the Board's ratification in Resolution 7 can be argued to validate a later-delivered notice (weak, but worth evaluating).
- **If the deadline is truly missed:** Consider whether Seller's calculation contains errors that could be challenged under a theory of mutual mistake or manifest error (the APA's Independent Accountant mechanism in §2.5(c) is only available after a timely Notice of Disagreement, so this path may be foreclosed). Alternatively, evaluate whether the Inventory representation in §4.9 (which survives for 9 months, i.e., until March 1, 2027) provides an independent indemnification claim for a materially inaccurate inventory statement, even if the §2.5 adjustment mechanism is lost.
- **Process fix:** Implement an internal deadline-tracking protocol for all contractual notice periods. The 30-day window here required Board authorization, and the governance process consumed the entire period.

**⚠ FLAG FOR PARTNER / CLIENT CALL:** This is the single most time-sensitive issue. If Glarol is going to assert any position, it must do so immediately. Every day of delay weakens any equitable argument.

### 2. CRITICAL – Environmental Representations Expire Before Permit Transfer Is Complete

**Documents:** APA §9.1 (Survival); APA §7.6(a) (Permit Transfer); APA §1.1 (definition of "Environmental Representations").

**What the documents say:**

- APA §9.1: General reps and warranties (including the Environmental Representations in §4.12) survive "until the date that is nine (9) months following the Closing Date." Closing was June 1, 2026, so environmental reps expire **March 1, 2027**.
- APA §7.6(a): Seller "may pursue the Permit Transfer Approval for up to twelve (12) months after the Closing Date." Until the permit transfers, "Seller shall remain the permittee of record" and "Buyer shall operate the Facility as Seller's designated operator under the transitional operating arrangement described on Schedule 7.6."
- APA §9.1 (final sentence): "The parties intend the survival periods in this Section 9.1 to replace any otherwise applicable statute of limitations, in accordance with 10 Del. C. § 8106(c)."

**Why it matters:** There is a **three-month gap** (March 1, 2027 through June 1, 2027) during which:

- Glarol is still operating the Facility as Seller's designated operator (i.e., in possession and control of the site);
- The Hazardous Waste Permit is still in Seller's name;
- The Environmental Representations have expired; and
- The statute of limitations for environmental claims has been contractually replaced by the 9-month survival period.

If a contamination issue is discovered in that window—or if a regulatory inquiry arises from pre-Closing conditions that manifest after March 1, 2027—Glarol has no indemnification recourse against Seller for breach of the environmental reps, and the shortened survival period may bar claims that would otherwise be available under CERCLA, RCRA, or Nevada state law. For a facility handling lithium compounds, cobalt, nickel, electrolyte solvents, fluorinated compounds, and heavy metals (APA §1.1, "Hazardous Materials"), this is a material exposure.

Additionally, the §9.1 provision replacing the statute of limitations means that even claims that would have longer limitations periods under environmental statutes are contractually truncated to 9 months. This is aggressive and potentially unenforceable as to certain regulatory claims, but it creates litigation risk and uncertainty.

**Recommended fix:**

- Assess whether the §9.1 SOL-replacement provision is enforceable as to environmental claims under Delaware law and federal environmental statutes (CERCLA contribution claims, for example, have their own limitations regimes that may not be contractually shortenable).
- If the deal is not yet closed (the documents suggest it is, per the Board Consent), this would be a negotiation point. Since the deal appears to have closed on June 1, 2026, the practical remedy is to ensure that:
  - The transitional operating arrangement (Schedule 7.6, not in the folder) allocates environmental liability clearly during the gap period;
  - Glarol's PLL insurance (see Issue 3) has a retroactive date that reaches back to before Closing;
  - Glarol conducts its environmental diligence (to the extent permitted—see Issue 6) before March 1, 2027, so that any issues discovered are within the survival period.
- Consider whether a side letter or amendment extending the environmental rep survival to match the 12-month permit transfer period is feasible. This would require Seller's agreement and is unlikely post-Closing, but worth exploring if a dispute arises.

**⚠ FLAG FOR PARTNER:** The three-month gap is structural and cannot be fixed unilaterally. The client needs to understand that after March 1, 2027, its contractual protection against pre-Closing environmental conditions lapses while it is still operating the site.

### 3. CRITICAL – Pollution Legal Liability Policy Terms Unconfirmed; Retroactive Date Unknown

**Documents:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter §§3, 5; APA §4.12 (Environmental Representations).

**What the documents say:**

- Cover Letter §3: The PLL policy is "written on a claims-made basis" with a three-year term (June 1, 2026 to June 1, 2029). Critically: "Its terms and conditions, **including the retroactive date, any exclusions for known conditions, and the reporting requirements**, are set out in the policy itself. We will deliver the policy to you when the insurer issues it."
- Cover Letter §5: "The certificate is issued as a matter of information only. It confers no rights on the certificate holder. It does not amend, extend or alter the coverage afforded by the policies listed."
- The policy forms have not yet been delivered. Only binders are in place.

**Why it matters:**

- **Retroactive date:** In a claims-made pollution policy, the retroactive date determines how far back coverage reaches for conditions that existed before the policy inception. If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026 (the policy effective date), then **pre-Closing contamination is not covered**. Given that Seller's environmental reps (which are the primary contractual protection against pre-Closing conditions) expire in 9 months (Issue 2), a gap in insurance coverage for legacy contamination would leave Glarol exposed.
- **Known conditions exclusion:** Most PLL policies exclude conditions that are "known" to the insured at the time of application or that are disclosed in the application. If Seller disclosed environmental matters in the APA's Disclosure Schedule, and Glarol (as successor operator) is deemed to have "knowledge" of those conditions, they may be excluded from coverage.
- **Reporting requirements:** Claims-made policies typically require prompt notice of any circumstance that could give rise to a claim. If Glarol discovers a potential issue but fails to report within the policy's timeframe, coverage may be voided.
- **Policy not yet in hand:** Glarol is operating a hazardous waste facility with only binders and a certificate. The actual policy terms, exclusions, and conditions are unknown.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Immediate:** Request the full PLL policy from Harwick & Doyle. Confirm the retroactive date in writing. If the retroactive date is June 1, 2026 or later, negotiate with the broker/insurer to extend it to a date before Closing (ideally before the start of Seller's operations at the Facility, or at minimum to the date of the Phase I/II ESA).
- Review the known-conditions exclusion and cross-reference it against the APA Disclosure Schedule (not in the folder—request it).
- Establish an internal protocol for reporting potential pollution circumstances to the broker within the policy's notice requirements.
- Confirm that the $10M per-condition / $10M aggregate limits are adequate for the Facility's risk profile (battery recycling involves multiple hazardous constituents).
- Calendar the June 1, 2029 expiration and begin renewal planning well in advance. A lapse in a claims-made policy would create a coverage gap for any unreported circumstances.

**⚠ FLAG FOR PARTNER / CLIENT CALL:** The client is operating a regulated hazardous waste facility without confirmed environmental insurance terms. This should be escalated to the client's risk management team immediately.

### 5. HIGH – Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction Between APA and Escrow Agreement

**Documents:** APA §11.8 (Delaware law), §11.9 (Delaware exclusive forum); Escrow Agreement §12.1 (New York law, Manhattan exclusive forum); APA §11.12 (Order of Precedence).

**What the documents say:**

- APA §11.8: Governed by Delaware law.
- APA §11.9: Exclusive jurisdiction in the Court of Chancery of Delaware (or other Delaware courts).
- Escrow Agreement §12.1: Governed by **New York** law. Exclusive jurisdiction in **Manhattan** state and federal courts.
- APA §11.12: "If this Agreement conflicts with any Ancillary Document, this Agreement shall control, **except that the Escrow Agreement shall control all matters relating to the Escrow Fund, including disputes regarding any Claim Notice.**"
- APA §9.6: "Buyer Indemnitees shall first seek recovery of Losses under Section 9.2(a) from the Escrow Fund, to the extent of available funds."

**Why it matters:** The Escrow Fund ($4.2M) is the **primary source of recovery** for Glarol's indemnification claims (§9.6). Disputes about whether a claim is valid, what amount is payable, or when the escrow should be released are governed by the Escrow Agreement, which is subject to New York law and Manhattan jurisdiction. Meanwhile, the underlying indemnification obligation (whether Seller breached a rep, what the scope of loss is) is governed by the APA, subject to Delaware law and Delaware jurisdiction.

This creates several practical problems:

- A single dispute (e.g., "Is this environmental claim valid and for how much?") may require litigation in two forums under two different bodies of law.
- New York and Delaware may interpret key terms (e.g., "material breach," "good faith," scope of indemnification) differently.
- The Escrow Agent's interpleader provision (Escrow Agreement §7.4) allows it to file in "a court specified in Section 12.1"—i.e., Manhattan. If Seller and Glarol disagree on a claim, the Escrow Agent could interplead in NY while the underlying merits are in DE.
- Increased legal costs and strategic complexity.

**Recommended fix:**

- This is a structural issue that cannot be unilaterally fixed post-Closing. However:
  - Ensure that any Claim Notice delivered under §9.4 / Escrow Agreement §5.1 is drafted to be effective under both NY and DE law.
  - If a dispute arises, consider whether the parties can agree to consolidated proceedings or a stipulation regarding forum (the Escrow Agent can act on Joint Written Instructions under §5.4(a) and §6.3, which could resolve some disputes without litigation).
  - For future transactions, negotiate consistent governing law and forum across all deal documents.

### 6. HIGH – No Invasive Environmental Sampling Permitted Until Permit Transfers

**Documents:** APA §7.6(c); APA §1.1 ("Hazardous Materials").

**What the documents say:**

- APA §7.6(c): "Before the Permit Transfer Approval is obtained, Buyer and its consultants shall not conduct any invasive or subsurface sampling at the Facility. This includes soil borings, groundwater monitoring well installation, sub-slab vapor sampling, and sampling of process sumps or containment areas. Buyer may, however, conduct visual inspections and non-invasive Phase I-type review."
- APA §7.6(a): Permit transfer may take up to 12 months after Closing (i.e., until June 1, 2027).

**Why it matters:** Glarol is in possession and operating a facility that handles lithium compounds, cobalt, nickel, manganese, electrolyte solvents, fluorinated compounds, heavy metals, and petroleum products. Yet it is contractually barred from conducting the very types of sampling needed to assess subsurface contamination, groundwater quality, vapor intrusion, or process sump integrity for up to 12 months.

This restriction:

- Prevents Glarol from confirming the accuracy of Seller's environmental reps (§4.12) during the critical pre-survival-expiration period;
- Limits Glarol's ability to identify and document conditions before the 9-month survival period expires (compounding Issue 2);
- May impede Glarol's ability to support or defend insurance claims (the PLL insurer may require sampling data);
- Creates a practical dilemma: if Glarol discovers a visual indicator of contamination, it cannot investigate further without potentially breaching §7.6(c).

**Recommended fix:**

- Conduct a thorough non-invasive Phase I review and visual inspection immediately (this is permitted). Document everything.
- If visual inspections reveal potential issues, assess whether they can be characterized as "non-invasive" investigations (e.g., reviewing existing monitoring data, analyzing process records, conducting desk-top assessments).
- Calendar the earliest date on which the Permit Transfer Approval is expected and plan invasive sampling for the day after transfer.
- Consider whether a narrow exception can be negotiated with Seller for emergency sampling (e.g., if a release is actively occurring). The current language has no emergency carve-out.
- Coordinate with the PLL insurer (Issue 3) to ensure that any sampling conducted after permit transfer satisfies the policy's conditions.

### 7. MODERATE – "Permitted Liens" Not Defined in the APA

**Documents:** APA §1.1 (Definitions); APA §2.1; APA §4.6; APA §4.14; Bill of Sale §2.

**What the documents say:**

- APA §2.1: Purchased Assets are conveyed "free and clear of all liens other than Permitted Liens."
- APA §4.6: Seller represents it has title "free and clear of all liens other than Permitted Liens."
- APA §4.14: "There are no liens for Taxes on the Purchased Assets other than Permitted Liens."
- Bill of Sale §2: "The Purchased Assets are conveyed free and clear of all liens other than Permitted Liens."
- **However, "Permitted Liens" does not appear in the defined terms in APA §1.1.**

**Why it matters:** The term "Permitted Liens" is used four times across the APA and Bill of Sale as a carve-out from the "free and clear" standard, but it is not defined. This creates ambiguity about:

- What liens are permitted to survive on the Purchased Assets;
- The scope of Seller's title representation (§4.6);
- Whether a lien that is not a "Permitted Lien" (because the term is undefined and thus potentially encompasses all liens) would be a breach.

In practice, this likely means the term was intended to be defined in the Disclosure Schedule or was inadvertently omitted from the definitions section. If undefined, a court could interpret it narrowly (no liens are "permitted" because none are specified) or broadly (the term is meaningless and the "free and clear" standard applies absolutely). Either interpretation creates risk.

**Recommended fix:**

- Review the Disclosure Schedule (not in the folder) to determine whether "Permitted Liens" is defined there.
- If it is not defined anywhere, this is a drafting error that should be addressed by amendment or side letter clarifying the intended scope.
- In the interim, conduct a UCC lien search and title review on the Purchased Assets to identify any existing encumbrances. If any are found, assess whether they fall within the likely intended scope of "Permitted Liens" (typically: purchase money security interests, liens in the Disclosure Schedule, statutory liens).

### 8. MODERATE – Indemnity Escrow May Be Inadequate Relative to Potential Exposure

**Documents:** APA §2.4 ($4.2M escrow); APA §9.5(b) ($6,975,000 cap on rep breaches; Purchase Price overall cap); APA §9.6 (escrow is first source of recovery).

**What the documents say:**

- The Indemnity Escrow is $4,200,000 (9% of the $46.5M purchase price).
- Seller's cap for breach of reps (other than Fundamental Reps) is $6,975,000 (15% of purchase price).
- The overall cap is the Purchase Price ($46.5M).
- Under §9.6, Glarol must first recover from the Escrow Fund before pursuing Seller directly.

**Why it matters:** If Glarol's claims exceed $4.2M (the escrow), it must pursue Seller directly for the balance, up to the $6.975M cap (or $46.5M overall cap). Post-Closing, Seller has transferred substantially all of its assets to Glarol. Seller's ability to satisfy a judgment beyond the escrow depends on what assets or cash it retains. If Seller is a shell entity post-closing, the effective cap on recovery is the $4.2M escrow, not the stated $6.975M or $46.5M.

For a facility with significant environmental risk (Issue 2), a single contamination event could easily exceed $4.2M in cleanup costs, regulatory penalties, and third-party claims.

**Recommended fix:**

- Assess Seller's post-Closing financial condition. What assets does it retain? Does it have other revenue streams? Is it a single-purpose entity?
- The PLL insurance (Issue 3) is the primary backstop for environmental claims exceeding the escrow. Confirming adequate coverage is therefore essential.
- If the escrow is released on the Release Date (March 1, 2027) with no claims pending, it will be gone. Ensure that any potential claims are identified and Claim Notices delivered before that date.
- Consider whether the $4.2M escrow is consistent with the risk profile. In future deals, negotiate a higher escrow or a specific environmental escrow with a longer release period.

### 9. MODERATE – Survival Period Replaces Statute of Limitations

**Documents:** APA §9.1 (final sentence).

**What the documents say:**

- "The parties intend the survival periods in this Section 9.1 to replace any otherwise applicable statute of limitations, in accordance with 10 Del. C. § 8106(c)."

**Why it matters:** 10 Del. C. § 8106(c) permits parties to contractually shorten (but not extend) the statute of limitations for contract claims, to a minimum of two years. However, the survival periods in §9.1 are:

- 9 months for general and environmental reps;
- 60 days after expiration of the applicable SOL for Fundamental Reps and Tax reps.

The 9-month period is **less than the two-year minimum** that § 8106(c) appears to require for contractual limitations shortening. If a court finds the 9-month period unenforceable as violating the statutory minimum, the survival period may revert to the default SOL (which for a written contract in Delaware is 20 years under 10 Del. C. § 8106(a), or 3 years for certain claims). This creates uncertainty:

- If the 9-month period is enforced, Glarol has a very short window.
- If it is struck down as violating § 8106(c), the longer default SOL applies, which actually benefits Glarol (more time to bring claims) but also benefits Seller (more time for Seller to bring claims against Glarol under §9.3).

Additionally, it is unclear whether § 8106(c) applies to environmental tort claims or regulatory claims that are not strictly "contract" claims. The provision may be unenforceable as to those.

**Recommended fix:**

- Obtain a Delaware counsel opinion on the enforceability of the 9-month survival period as a limitations shortening under § 8106(c).
- In the interim, treat the 9-month deadline (March 1, 2027) as a hard deadline for asserting all indemnification claims, regardless of the enforceability question. Do not rely on the possibility that a longer period may apply.
- Ensure all potential claims are identified and Claim Notices delivered well before March 1, 2027.

- If email delivery is disputed (e.g., the inbox is full, the email bounces, or the recipient claims non-receipt), there is no physical address to which a courtesy copy could be sent.
- For service of process in Delaware (APA §11.9) or New York (Escrow Agreement §12.1), the registered agent address would control, but the notice address gap is still a best-practice concern.
- The missed deadline in Issue 1 was an internal process failure, but had there been any ambiguity about whether the Closing Statement was properly "delivered" to Glarol's notice address, the lack of a street address could have complicated the analysis.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add Glarol's street address to the notice provisions by amendment or by delivering a notice of address change under §11.2 / §12.3.
- Ensure the email address notices@glarol.com is monitored 24/7 during critical periods and that an auto-forward or backup is in place.
