## Summary

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (the "SPA"), the Bill of Sale for service tooling (the "Bill of Sale"), the Transition Services Agreement (the "TSA"), and the prior draft of the Bill of Sale. I identify **eleven issues** that create material exposure for Lalais, ranked from highest to lowest risk. The three most consequential are: (1) the absence of the Kestrevo sole-source consent from the closing conditions, (2) the 90-day survival period for FDA and product liability representations, and (3) the lack of any representation, warranty, or indemnity backing the Bill of Sale transfer. Several of these issues are interrelated and compound one another. I also flag three items for discussion with the client before we respond to Trasirt.

## Issue 1 — Kestrevo Sole-Source Consent Missing from Closing Conditions

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA, Schedule 4.6(a)(iii) (Disclosure Schedule, page 7): discloses that the Detector Supply Agreement with Kestrevo Sensors GmbH makes Kestrevo the **sole source** of flat-panel detectors for the Company's mobile C-arm product line, and that Section 14.2 of that agreement requires Kestrevo's prior written consent to a change of control, "failing which Kestrevo may terminate the Detector Supply Agreement on written notice."
- SPA, Schedule 7.2(d) (page 7): lists **only one** required third-party consent — the Facility Lease consent from Westbridge Industrial Partners LLC. The Kestrevo consent is **not listed**.
- SPA, Section 7.2(d): makes the listed consents a condition to Buyer's closing obligation.
- SPA, Section 6.4: requires Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain the consents listed on Schedule 7.2(d).

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
Because Kestrevo's consent is not on Schedule 7.2(d), it is not a closing condition. Lalais can be compelled to close (or may choose to close) without Kestrevo's consent. After closing, Kestrevo can terminate the Detector Supply Agreement on written notice, leaving the Company without its sole source of a critical component for its primary product line. The SPA provides no specific remedy for this outcome: it is not a breach of any representation (Schedule 4.6(a)(iii) discloses the change-of-control provision), it is not a failure of a closing condition, and the general "no conflicts" rep in Section 4.5 is qualified by the Disclosure Schedule. The non-compete in Section 6.8 does not help because the risk is supply-chain disruption, not competition.

This is the single most dangerous gap in the documents. A mobile C-arm manufacturer without its sole-source detector supplier cannot build product.

**Recommended fix:**
Add the Kestrevo consent to Schedule 7.2(d) so that it becomes a condition to Buyer's closing obligation under Section 7.2(d) and a Seller covenant under Section 6.4. Proposed language for Schedule 7.2(d):

> 2. Consent of Kestrevo Sensors GmbH, as supplier under the Detector Supply Agreement dated September 15, 2022, to the change of control of the Company resulting from the transactions contemplated by this Agreement.

If Trasirt resists making this a hard closing condition (e.g., because Kestrevo has not yet responded), an alternative is to add a specific representation that Kestrevo will not exercise its termination right, coupled with an extension of the survival period for that representation to at least 18 months. However, a representation is weaker than a closing condition because it does not prevent the close; it only provides post-closing indemnity (subject to the deductible and cap).

**Client discussion point:** Confirm with the client whether Kestrevo has been approached and what its posture is. If Kestrevo is likely to withhold consent, the client needs to make a business decision about whether the deal is viable without it. This should be raised before we respond to Trasirt.

## Issue 2 — FDA and Product Liability Representations Survive Only 90 Days

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA, Section 9.1(b): "The representations and warranties in Section 4.12 (Product Liability; Recalls) and Section 4.14 (FDA and Regulatory Compliance) shall survive the Closing until the Escrow Release Date."
- SPA, Section 2.4(b): defines the Escrow Release Date as "the date 90 days after the Closing Date."
- SPA, Section 4.12: reps regarding product conformance, absence of injury claims, and absence of recalls or field corrections since January 1, 2021.
- SPA, Section 4.14: reps regarding FDA compliance, 510(k) clearances, absence of Form 483s or warning letters, and timely filing of medical device reports.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
Ninety days is an extremely short survival period for the representations most likely to protect a buyer of a medical device company. FDA enforcement actions (Form 483s, warning letters, consent decrees), 510(k) clearance issues, product liability claims from latent defects, and recall obligations can all surface well after 90 days. The MDR regulations at 21 C.F.R. Part 803 require reporting within 30 days of becoming aware of a device-related death or serious injury; a claim that the Company failed to make a timely MDR could emerge months or years after closing. A product liability suit from a patient injured by a device manufactured before closing could be filed well outside the 90-day window.

The practical effect is that after 90 days, Lalais has **no indemnification recourse** under the SPA for any FDA or product liability issue, unless it falls within the Fundamental Representations (which these are not) or constitutes fraud. The escrow, which is the sole security for these claims, is also released at 90 days, so there is no collateral either.

This issue is compounded by the TSA: during the 12-month transition, Provider (Trasirt) will be logging complaints and service events (TSA, Section 3.3 and Schedule A, Part 1, item 4). If Provider mishandles a reportable event, Recipient "remains responsible for all regulatory determinations and submissions" (TSA, Section 3.3, last sentence). The TSA indemnity in Section 11.1 covers Provider's "violation of Applicable Law in performing the Services," and that indemnity is not subject to the liability cap (TSA, Section 11.5(a)). But if the underlying FDA issue is a pre-closing condition (e.g., the Company failed to file an MDR before closing, or a 510(k) clearance is defective), the TSA indemnity does not help because it covers only Provider's conduct during the transition, not the Company's pre-closing compliance.

**Recommended fix:**
Extend the survival period for Sections 4.12 and 4.14 to at least 18 months (matching the general survival period in Section 9.1(a)), or better, to the statute of limitations for the underlying claim. Proposed replacement for Section 9.1(b):

> (b) The representations and warranties in Section 4.12 (Product Liability; Recalls) and Section 4.14 (FDA and Regulatory Compliance) shall survive the Closing until the later of (i) 24 months after the Closing Date or (ii) the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations for the underlying claim.

If Trasirt resists, a fallback is to extend the Escrow Release Date specifically for claims under Sections 4.12 and 4.14 to 18 months, while releasing the remainder of the escrow at 90 days.

**Client discussion point:** The client should understand that if it accepts the 90-day survival, it is effectively self-insuring all FDA and product liability risk after day 90. Given the $186.5M purchase price and the regulated nature of the business, this is a significant risk transfer. The client should decide how hard to push on this point.

## Issue 3 — No Representations, Warranties, or Indemnity Backing the Bill of Sale

**Documents and Sections:**
- Bill of Sale, Section 6: "The representations, warranties, covenants and indemnities of Seller in the Stock Purchase Agreement are not enlarged or diminished by this Bill of Sale."
- Bill of Sale, Section 1.1: covenants that the transfer is "free and clear of all Encumbrances."
- SPA, Section 9.2: indemnifies Buyer for (a) inaccuracy of Seller's reps, (b) breach of Seller's covenants "in this Agreement," and (c) pre-closing taxes.
- SPA, Section 9.9: makes Article 9 the "sole and exclusive remedy" for breaches "in this Agreement."
- SPA, Section 4.8: represents that the Company has good title to assets "used in the Business" and that those assets, "together with the assets transferred under the Bill of Sale," are sufficient for continued operations.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The service tooling, calibration phantoms, and service manuals are **Seller's** assets, not the Company's. They are being transferred to the Company via the Bill of Sale at closing. The SPA's title representation in Section 4.8 addresses the Company's existing assets; it does not represent that Seller has good title to the Transferred Assets. The Bill of Sale contains a covenant (Section 1.1) that the transfer is free and clear of encumbrances, but:

- The Bill of Sale contains no representation of title, no representation of condition, and no representation of completeness.
- The SPA's indemnification in Section 9.2(b) covers breach of Seller's covenants "in this Agreement" (i.e., the SPA). It is unclear whether a breach of the Bill of Sale's covenants falls within Section 9.2(b). The Bill of Sale is a separate document. Even if it does, the exclusive remedy provision in Section 9.9 limits remedies to Article 9 "for any breach of the representations, warranties, covenants and agreements in this Agreement" — again, "this Agreement" being the SPA.
- The prior draft of the Bill of Sale (Section 6) had the same language, so this is not a regression, but it was never fixed.

If the tooling is defective, the phantoms are out of calibration, the service manuals are incomplete, or a lien attaches to the transferred assets, Lalais may have no clear contractual remedy. A direct breach-of-contract claim under the Bill of Sale is theoretically available, but it would be subject to the SPA's order of precedence (Section 11.12: SPA prevails over Bill of Sale) and potentially the exclusive remedy provision.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a specific representation to the SPA (or to the Bill of Sale, with a corresponding indemnity) covering the Transferred Assets. Proposed new Section 4.8(b) in the SPA:

> (b) Seller has good and valid title to all assets described on Schedule A to the Bill of Sale, free and clear of all Encumbrances. The Transferred Assets are in the same condition as they were when used in the Business immediately prior to the Closing Date, and the service manuals and calibration documentation transferred under the Bill of Sale are complete and current as of the Closing Date.

And add to SPA Section 9.2(a) the phrase "or in any Transaction Document" to make the indemnity explicitly cover breaches of the Bill of Sale and TSA:

> (a) any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty of Seller in this Agreement **or in any other Transaction Document** or in any certificate delivered under it;

**Client discussion point:** Confirm with the client whether it has received and reviewed the asset register referenced in Bill of Sale Schedule A, Part 1, items 5 and 6. If the register is incomplete, the scope of the transfer is uncertain.

## Issue 4 — Non-Compete Does Not Cover CT/MRI Service Business

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA, Recital B: the Business includes "service for an installed base of computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging equipment."
- SPA, Section 6.8(a): for three years post-closing, Seller shall not "engage in the design, manufacture, sale or servicing of **mobile C-arm or digital radiography systems** anywhere in the United States."
- Bill of Sale, Schedule A, Part 1, item 6 and Part 2, items 3–4: CT and MRI service tooling and phantoms are transferred, confirming the CT/MRI service business is part of the acquired Business.
- TSA, Schedule A, Part 1, item 1: remote service covers "the CT and MRI equipment in the Company's installed base."

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The non-compete is drafted to cover only the product lines (mobile C-arm and digital radiography). It does not prohibit Seller from servicing CT or MRI equipment. After closing, Trasirt could immediately launch a competing CT/MRI service business, using its own technicians, its own parts supply, and its knowledge of the installed base (which it serviced pre-closing and will continue to service during the 12-month TSA term). The CT/MRI installed base is a revenue-generating asset that Lalais is acquiring, and the non-compete provides no protection for it.

**Recommended fix:**
Expand Section 6.8(a) to cover the full scope of the Business. Proposed replacement:

> (a) engage in the design, manufacture, sale or servicing of mobile C-arm or digital radiography systems, or the provision of service for an installed base of computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging equipment, anywhere in the United States, other than through the performance of the Transition Services Agreement,

## Issue 5 — TSA Permits Provider to Assign or Delegate to Any Person Without Consent

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA, Section 13.2: "Provider may assign this Agreement, or delegate the performance of any Services, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Recipient."
- TSA, Section 9.3: permits Provider to disclose Recipient Confidential Information to "its permitted assignees and delegates under Section 13.2."
- Compare SPA, Section 11.6: Buyer may assign to Affiliates or lenders as collateral, but the SPA requires mutual consent for other assignments.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
Lalais is paying $410,000 per month ($4.92M over the Term) for transition services that include remote diagnostics on medical devices, field service dispatch, and ERP hosting containing customer data and service records. Provider can delegate any or all of these services to any third party — a competitor, an underqualified vendor, or an affiliate with weaker security practices — without Lalais's consent. The confidentiality provision (Section 9.3) expressly permits sharing Recipient Confidential Information (which includes "installed-base service records, Customer site data, equipment configuration and service history, service firmware and diagnostic software, complaint and service event records") with those assignees and delegates.

This is particularly concerning because:
- The non-compete (Issue 4) does not cover CT/MRI service, so a delegate could be a direct competitor.
- The data security standard in Section 8.3 requires safeguards "no less protective than those Provider applied to the corresponding data of the Business before the Closing" — but a delegate may not have the same infrastructure.
- There is no requirement that the delegate be "reasonably satisfactory to Recipient" or bound by equivalent confidentiality and data security obligations (though Section 9.3 implies they must be bound by confidentiality "no less protective").

**Recommended fix:**
Add a consent right and qualification standard. Proposed replacement for the second sentence of TSA Section 13.2:

> Provider may not assign this Agreement or delegate the performance of any Services without Recipient's prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld. Any delegate shall be an entity that (a) is bound by confidentiality and data security obligations no less protective than those in Sections 8.3 and 9 of this Agreement, and (b) is not a competitor of the Business within the meaning of Section 6.8 of the Stock Purchase Agreement.

If Trasirt resists a full consent right, a minimum fallback is to require that any delegate be an Affiliate of Provider (consistent with the SPA's assignment provision) and to add the non-competitor restriction.

## Issue 6 — Escrow Release at 90 Days Leaves No Security for Post-90-Day Claims

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA, Section 2.4(a): Escrow Amount is $18,650,000 (10% of Purchase Price).
- SPA, Section 2.4(b): on the Escrow Release Date (90 days post-closing), the Escrow Agent releases the remaining amount to Seller, less pending claims.
- SPA, Section 9.1(a): general reps survive 18 months.
- SPA, Section 9.4(b): cap on non-fundamental rep claims is $18,650,000.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The escrow is the sole security for Seller's indemnification obligations. After 90 days, the escrow is gone. For the remaining 15 months of the general survival period, any claim against Seller is unsecured — dependent on Seller's general creditworthiness. If Seller distributes the escrow proceeds to its shareholders or uses them to service debt, recovery on a valid indemnity claim becomes a collection problem. This is standard in many deals, but it is more acute here because:
- The most important reps (FDA, product liability) expire at the same time the escrow is released (Issue 2).
- The deductible is $932,500, so smaller claims are not recoverable at all.
- The cap equals the escrow amount, suggesting the parties intended the escrow to be the exclusive source of recovery for non-fundamental claims. Once the escrow is released, the cap is meaningless if Seller is not creditworthy.

**Recommended fix:**
Stagger the escrow release. Proposed replacement for Section 2.4(b):

> (b) On the date 90 days after the Closing Date, the Escrow Agent shall release to Seller fifty percent (50%) of the then-remaining Escrow Amount, less the aggregate amount of all claims for indemnification asserted by Buyer in good faith under Article 9 that remain pending. On the date 18 months after the Closing Date, the Escrow Agent shall release to Seller the remaining Escrow Amount, less the aggregate amount of all claims that remain pending. Each retained amount shall be released to the party entitled to it promptly after the related claim is finally resolved.

This aligns the escrow release with the survival periods and gives Lalais security for the full 18-month general survival period.

## Issue 7 — "Net Working Capital" Is Undefined

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA, Section 2.3(a): references "net working capital" and sets a Target of $14,200,000.
- SPA, Section 1.1 (Definitions): does not define "net working capital."
- SPA, Section 4.4: references Financial Statements prepared in accordance with GAAP, but does not tie the working capital calculation to a specific GAAP methodology.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The working capital adjustment can move the Purchase Price by a significant dollar amount (the target is $14.2M; a $1M swing changes the price by $1M). Without a defined formula specifying which balance sheet items are included (e.g., whether deferred revenue, accrued expenses, income tax payable, or restricted cash are in or out), the parties are likely to dispute the calculation. The SPA provides for an independent accounting firm to resolve disputes (Section 2.3(b)), but that process is slow, expensive, and uncertain.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a definition in Section 1.1:

> "**Net Working Capital**" means, as of any date, the excess of (a) current assets (excluding cash and cash equivalents, short-term investments, and deferred tax assets) over (b) current liabilities (excluding short-term debt, current portion of long-term debt, and deferred tax liabilities), in each case calculated in accordance with GAAP consistently with the methodology used in the Financial Statements.

Or, if the parties prefer, attach a schedule specifying the exact line items to be included and excluded.

## Issue 8 — Asset Register Referenced but Not Attached

**Documents and Sections:**
- Bill of Sale, Schedule A, Part 1, item 5: "Service laptops loaded with Company diagnostic and service software, assigned to the field service personnel listed in the asset register delivered by Seller to Buyer before the Closing."
- Bill of Sale, Schedule A, Part 1, item 6: "Specialized CT and MRI service tooling held at Seller's regional depots ... as identified in that asset register."
- Prior draft Bill of Sale, Schedule A note: "*Note to Draft: Quantities to be confirmed against Seller asset register.*"

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The scope of at least two categories of Transferred Assets (service laptops and CT/MRI tooling) is defined by reference to an "asset register" that is not part of any Transaction Document. If the register is incomplete, inaccurate, or later modified, the scope of the transfer is uncertain. Combined with Issue 3 (no reps backing the Bill of Sale), this creates a risk that Lalais discovers post-closing that key tools or laptops were not transferred, with no clear remedy.

**Recommended fix:**
Attach the asset register as a schedule to the Bill of Sale (e.g., Schedule B) and add a representation in the SPA that the asset register is complete and accurate as of the Closing Date. Alternatively, require Seller to deliver the asset register as a closing deliverable under Section 3.2 and make its accuracy a condition to closing.

## Issue 9 — No Service Level Agreements or Remedies for Underperformance in the TSA

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA, Section 3.2: requires performance "in a professional and workmanlike manner" and "with substantially the same scope, priority, quality, timeliness and level of care" as pre-closing.
- TSA, Section 12.1: termination for material breach with 30-day cure period.
- TSA, Section 11.4: liability cap at total Monthly Service Fees ($4.92M), with carve-outs in Section 11.5.
- TSA, Schedule A: describes the services but sets no specific performance metrics, response times, or uptime requirements.

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The standard of performance is subjective ("professional and workmanlike," "substantially the same"). There are no objective metrics (e.g., response time for remote diagnostics, dispatch turnaround, ERP uptime percentage). If Provider underperforms in a way that is not "material," Lalais has no termination right and no service credit. It must either accept degraded service or build a case for material breach, which is a high bar. During the transition period, degraded service could affect customer relationships, regulatory compliance (e.g., delayed complaint logging), and revenue.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a service level schedule (Schedule B to the TSA) with specific metrics and remedies. For example:

> **Schedule B — Service Levels**
>
> | Service | Metric | Target | Remedy for Miss |
> |---------|--------|--------|-----------------|
> | Remote diagnostics | Response time (acknowledgment) | ≤ 2 hours, 24/7 | 5% fee credit per occurrence |
> | Field service dispatch | Scheduling turnaround | ≤ 4 Business Days | 5% fee credit per occurrence |
> | ERP hosting | Uptime (excluding scheduled maintenance) | ≥ 99.5% monthly | 10% fee credit per 0.1% below target |
> | Complaint forwarding | Time to Recipient quality contact | ≤ 1 Business Day (≤ 24 hrs for MDR-reportable) | No credit; breach of Section 3.3 |
>
> If the same metric is missed in three or more consecutive months, Recipient may terminate the affected Service under Section 12.1 without a cure period.

## Issue 10 — No Insurance Requirement in the TSA

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA: no provision requires Provider to maintain any insurance.
- SPA, Section 4.16: represents that the Company holds all permits necessary to conduct the Business, but says nothing about insurance.
- The services include field service on medical devices (radiation exposure risk), handling of customer data (cyber risk), and ERP hosting (data breach risk).

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
If a Provider employee is injured on a customer site, or if a data breach occurs in the Provider Systems, the TSA indemnity (Section 11.1) covers third-party claims caused by Provider's gross negligence, willful misconduct, data security breach, confidentiality breach, or violation of Applicable Law. But for ordinary negligence (e.g., a slip-and-fall by a Provider technician at a hospital), the indemnity does not apply, and the liability cap in Section 11.4 limits recovery to $4.92M. Requiring Provider to maintain insurance (general liability, professional liability, cyber) would provide an additional layer of protection and would be standard in a services agreement of this type.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a new section to the TSA:

> **6.5 Insurance.** During the Term, Provider shall maintain, at its own expense: (a) commercial general liability insurance with a limit of not less than $5,000,000 per occurrence; (b) professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance with a limit of not less than $5,000,000 per claim; and (c) cyber liability insurance with a limit of not less than $5,000,000 per occurrence. Provider shall name Recipient and the Company as additional insureds under policies (a) and (b) and shall provide certificates of insurance upon request.

## Issue 11 — TSA Data Migration Lacks Structure and Milestones

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA, Section 8.4: Provider shall, "before the end of the Term, or within 30 days after any earlier termination of a Service," provide migration assistance, deliver data, and delete data.
- TSA, Section 7.3: Recipient shall use "commercially reasonable efforts to transition each Service to its own systems or a third-party provider before the end of the Term."
- TSA, Section 6.3: Service Coordinators meet "at least monthly to review performance, open service tickets and transition planning."

**Why it matters to Lalais:**
The data migration obligation is back-ended: Provider has no obligation to begin planning or preparing until the end of the Term. If Provider's ERP system is complex (as is likely for a medical device company with order management, inventory, service contract billing, and financial reporting modules), a 30-day migration window may be insufficient. A failed or delayed migration could disrupt the Business's operations at the worst possible time — when Provider is no longer contractually obligated to provide services. The monthly Service Coordinator meetings reference "transition planning" but there is no requirement for a formal transition plan with milestones, deliverables, and acceptance criteria.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a transition plan requirement:

> **7.4 Transition Plan.** Within 60 days after the Effective Date, the parties shall agree on a written transition plan (the "Transition Plan") setting out, for each Service: (a) the target date for migration; (b) the systems and resources Recipient will use to receive the Service; (c) the format and scope of data to be migrated; (d) testing and acceptance criteria; and (e) Provider's cooperation obligations and timeline. The Transition Plan shall be a schedule to this Agreement. Provider shall use commercially reasonable efforts to complete migration in accordance with the Transition Plan.
