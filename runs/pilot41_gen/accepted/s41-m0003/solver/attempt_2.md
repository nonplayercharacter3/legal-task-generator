## Summary

The MSA deviates materially from the term sheet in three areas that significantly increase Nonaisk's risk: (1) the customer indemnity in § 10.2 is far broader than the term sheet contemplated and is uncapped; (2) the assignment clause is one-way in Provider's favor and drops the "not a Competitor" protection; and (3) the DPA uses a different governing law and forum than the MSA. Additional issues include a gap in the DPA's data definition, an order-of-precedence problem, and a public representation in the press release that warrants a client decision.

---

## Issues (Ranked by Priority)

### 1. Customer Indemnity Is Overbroad and Contradicts the Term Sheet

**Where:** MSA § 10.2; compare Term Sheet § 8.2.

**Problem:** Term Sheet § 8.2 provided that "[e]ach party will indemnify the other against third-party claims arising from **its own negligence or willful misconduct**." MSA § 10.2 instead requires Customer to indemnify Provider for "all losses… arising from **or relating to** the Rental Inventory," and expressly includes "claims for bodily injury, death or property damage occurring at any event at which Rental Inventory is used, **regardless of whether caused in part by Provider's installation or handling** of the Rental Inventory."

**Why it matters:** If a Zouduth crew member installs a stage deck incorrectly and it collapses, injuring an event guest, Nonaisk must indemnify Zouduth for that claim—even though Zouduth's own negligence caused it. The phrase "arising from or relating to" is also extremely broad and could sweep in unrelated claims at venues where Nonaisk's furniture happens to be present (e.g., a guest slipping on a wet aisle).

**Recommended fix:** Replace § 10.2 with a negligence-based indemnity mirroring the term sheet: Customer indemnifies Provider for third-party claims arising from Customer's negligence or willful misconduct, or from a defect in the Rental Inventory itself (i.e., the furniture was defective before tender to Provider). Delete the "regardless of whether caused in part by Provider's installation or handling" language. If Provider wants protection for claims inherent to the Rental Inventory (e.g., a chair that was already broken), that can be addressed in a narrow, fault-based carve-out.

### 2. Customer's Indemnity Obligation Is Uncapped

**Where:** MSA § 11.3(c).

**Problem:** Section 11.3(c) excludes "Customer's obligations under Section 10.2" from both the liability cap (§ 11.1) and the consequential damages waiver (§ 11.2). Combined with the overbroad scope of § 10.2 (Issue 1), Nonaisk faces **unlimited** indemnification exposure for claims that may be caused by Provider's own negligence.

**Why it matters:** A single serious injury at a large event could generate a multi-million-dollar judgment. Nonaisk's annual fees to Provider are $1.85M; the cap would otherwise limit exposure to roughly that amount. As drafted, there is no ceiling.

**Recommended fix:** Delete § 11.3(c) so that Customer's indemnity is subject to the same cap as Provider's. If Provider insists on an uncapped indemnity for a narrow category (e.g., Customer's gross negligence or willful misconduct), that is acceptable. But the current language—uncapped for all claims "relating to" Rental Inventory—is not.

**Flag for client decision:** If Nonaisk's own insurance program (e.g., its CGL policy) already covers product-liability-type claims arising from its furniture, the practical exposure may be limited to the deductible/retention. Confirm with the client's risk manager whether the current insurance tower is adequate before negotiating the cap.

### 3. Assignment Rights Are One-Way and Drop the Competitor Restriction

**Where:** MSA §§ 15.1–15.2; compare Term Sheet § 7.1.

**Problem:** Term Sheet § 7.1 stated: "Neither party may assign without consent, **except to a successor that is not a Competitor**." The MSA deviates in two ways:

- § 15.1: Customer may not assign without Provider's consent (no exception).
- § 15.2: Provider may assign **without Customer's consent** to "any Affiliate or to any successor to all or substantially all of Provider's business or assets."

The "not a Competitor" restriction from the term sheet is absent. The "Competitor" definition in MSA § 1.2 (any entity engaged in renting furniture, staging, tenting or related equipment for events) is never applied to the assignment clause.

**Why it matters:** Zouduth could sell its logistics business to a competitor of Nonaisk (e.g., another event furniture renter with an in-house logistics arm) and assign the MSA freely. Nonaisk's event-client data, booking patterns, and operational details would then be available to a direct competitor. Nonaisk, by contrast, cannot assign even to a non-competitor without Zouduth's consent.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Make assignment mutual: neither party may assign without the other's consent, except to a successor in interest that is not a Competitor (using the § 1.2 definition). (b) Add a requirement that any assignee agree in writing to be bound by the confidentiality and data protection provisions. (c) If Provider resists, at minimum add the "not a Competitor" restriction to § 15.2.

### 4. DPA Uses New York Law and Forum; MSA Uses Delaware

**Where:** DPA §§ 12.1–12.2; compare MSA § 16.1–16.2 and Term Sheet § 10.

**Problem:** The MSA and term sheet both specify Delaware law and Wilmington courts. The DPA specifies **New York** law and **New York County** courts. This creates a split-jurisdiction scenario: a data-breach dispute could be litigated in New York under New York law while a service-quality dispute is litigated in Delaware under Delaware law.

**Why it matters:** New York's data protection and consumer privacy laws (e.g., SHIELD Act) differ from Delaware's. The split also increases litigation cost and complexity and could produce inconsistent outcomes on related claims.

**Recommended fix:** Conform DPA § 12 to Delaware law and Wilmington forum to match the MSA. If Provider insists on New York (perhaps because its data operations are NY-based), at minimum add a provision that the MSA's order of precedence and dispute-resolution mechanism apply to DPA disputes as well, to avoid truly parallel proceedings.

### 5. DPA's "Customer Data" Definition Is Narrower Than the MSA and Term Sheet

**Where:** DPA § 1.3; compare MSA § 1.4 and Term Sheet § 6.1.

**Problem:** MSA § 1.4 defines Customer Data to include "all data Provider receives from or collects for Customer in performing Services," including "venue access information, delivery sign-offs, and any data entered into or generated by the Dispatch Platform." Term Sheet § 6.1 explicitly covers data "whether entered into the Zouduth Dispatch Platform **or collected by Provider crews on site**."

DPA § 1.3, however, defines Customer Data as only "Personal Data **uploaded by Customer to the Zouduth Dispatch Platform**… that Customer enters or imports." This excludes:

- Paper delivery sign-offs collected by crews on site;
- Data entered by Provider personnel (not Customer) into the platform;
- Data generated by the platform in connection with Customer's orders (e.g., routing data, crew GPS).

**Why it matters:** The DPA's protections (security requirements, breach notification, subprocessor controls, return/deletion) apply only to "Customer Data" as defined in the DPA. If on-site paper records or platform-generated data fall outside that definition, they are not covered by the DPA's safeguards. A breach involving crew-collected paper records (e.g., a lost clipboard with guest names and addresses) might not trigger the 48-hour notification obligation in DPA § 6.1.

**Recommended fix:** Align DPA § 1.3 with MSA § 1.4. The DPA definition should cover all Personal Data that Provider receives from, collects on behalf of, or generates for Customer in performing the Services, regardless of whether it is uploaded by Customer or collected by Provider personnel.

### 6. Order of Precedence Favors MSA Over SOWs

**Where:** MSA § 2.3.

**Problem:** The order of precedence is: DPA (for Personal Data matters only) → MSA → SOWs. This means that if a SOW contains service-level credits, scheduling windows, or rate terms that conflict with the MSA, the MSA controls.

**Why it matters:** Nonaisk will negotiate specific service levels and credits in each SOW. If the MSA's general provisions (e.g., § 6.2's reference to "service credits specified in the applicable SOW") are read as the controlling framework, SOW-specific terms could be overridden. More practically, if a SOW promises a 95% on-time delivery rate with a 5% credit, but the MSA's general standards (§ 2.1, "professional and workmanlike manner") are deemed to control in a conflict, the SOW's specific metric could be diluted.

**Recommended fix:** Reverse the order so that SOWs prevail over the MSA for service-specific terms (service levels, rates, scheduling windows). The MSA should control for general terms (confidentiality, indemnity, liability). Proposed language: "For service-specific matters (service levels, rates, scheduling, territory), the applicable SOW controls. For all other matters, the MSA controls. The DPA controls for Personal Data matters."

### 7. Annual Minimum May Survive Termination for Provider's Breach

**Where:** MSA § 5.2; § 14.3.

**Problem:** Section 5.2 requires Customer to pay the $1.85M Annual Minimum, with pro rata reduction only for (a) service-level failures and (b) force majeure affecting Provider. It does not reduce or eliminate the minimum if Customer terminates for Provider's material breach under § 14.3. Read literally, if Provider materially breaches in month 2 and Customer terminates, Customer may still owe the balance of the Annual Minimum for that contract year.

**Why it matters:** The Annual Minimum is a significant commitment. If Provider's breach renders the relationship untenable, Nonaisk should not be locked into paying for services it will no longer receive.

**Recommended fix:** Add to § 5.2: "The Annual Minimum shall be reduced pro rata for any period following Customer's termination of this Agreement for Provider's material breach under Section 14.3." Alternatively, provide that upon termination for Provider's uncured material breach, the Annual Minimum is void for the remainder of the then-current Contract Year.

### 8. Subprocessor Approval Is Notice-and-Object, Not Consent

**Where:** DPA § 5.1–5.2.

**Problem:** Provider need only give 30 days' notice before adding a new subprocessor. Customer may object, but if the objection is not resolved in 30 days, Customer's sole remedy is to "terminate the affected Services without penalty." Customer cannot block the subprocessor from being added.

**Why it matters:** A new subprocessor could have weaker security practices or be located in a jurisdiction Customer does not want. Customer's only lever is to walk away from the affected services—which, for a core logistics relationship, is not a practical option mid-contract.

**Recommended fix:** Change to a consent model: Provider shall not engage a new subprocessor without Customer's prior written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld. If a true notice-and-object model is acceptable to the client, at minimum extend the objection period to 30 days (matching the notice period) and clarify that "terminate the affected Services" means Customer is not liable for any resulting shortfall under the Annual Minimum.

### 9. Press Release Makes Public Representations About Data Handling

**Where:** Press Release § 4.

**Problem:** The release states: "Under the addendum, that information is stored within the United States and protected by security safeguards." This is a public, dated representation. If Provider later migrates data, changes subprocessors, or experiences a security incident, the press release becomes evidence of a representation that was made to the public (and potentially to Nonaisk's event clients who read it).

**Why it matters:** In a data-breach scenario, Nonaisk's event clients could point to this public statement in negligence or misrepresentation claims. It also limits Nonaisk's flexibility to consent to data-location changes under DPA § 10.1 without looking like it broke a public promise.

**Flag for client decision:** Confirm with the client whether the press release has been or will be published, and whether the client is comfortable with the specific data-handling representation. If the release is not yet published, consider softening § 4 to: "The parties have entered into a data processing addendum that addresses the handling of client information in connection with the services." If it is already published, ensure the DPA terms support the representation as written.

### 10. Cargo Insurance Limit May Be Inadequate

**Where:** MSA § 12.1(c).

**Problem:** Cargo and inland marine insurance is required at "not less than $500,000 per conveyance." A single truckload of staging, tenting furniture, and décor for a large corporate event or wedding can easily exceed $500,000 in replacement value.

**Why it matters:** If a truck is totaled in an accident, the insurance may not cover the full value of the inventory, leaving a gap that Provider's indemnity under § 3.3 must fill—but Provider's liability is capped at 12 months of fees (§ 11.1), and the cargo insurance is the primary backstop.

**Flag for client decision:** Ask the client to confirm the typical per-truckload value of its inventory. If it regularly exceeds $500K, push for a higher limit (e.g., $1M per conveyance) or require Provider to confirm that its cargo policy covers the full declared value of each shipment.
