## Summary

Review of the five Contract Documents (Distribution Agreement, Order Form, Service Level Schedule, Amended and Restated SOW No. 1, and the launch pallet labeling email thread) reveals ten issues that expose Fouveirt to commercial, operational, and legal risk. The two most significant are: (1) the Order Form silently expands the definition of "Products" to include Veterinary Diet Products and all future Fouveirt-brand pet food, which—under the order of precedence—overrides the Agreement's narrower definition and likely captures Fouveirt's direct veterinary channel inside the exclusive distribution arrangement; and (2) a provision in the Service Level Schedule overrides the Agreement's food-safety liability carve-out, capping Pruran's exposure for temperature excursions, handling failures, and resulting recalls at roughly $462,000.

Issues are listed in order of significance to the client.

## Issue 1 – Order Form Redefines "Products" to Include Veterinary Diet Products and All Future Products

**Where:** Order Form § 1; Distribution Agreement § 1.1 ("Products" definition); Distribution Agreement § 2.3 (Order of Precedence); Recital B; Agreement § 3.1 (Exclusive Appointment).

**Explanation.** The Agreement defines "Products" as "dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A, **excluding** Veterinary Diet Products" (Agreement § 1.1). Recital B confirms that Supplier "sells its Veterinary Diet Products through its own veterinary clinic channel, directly to licensed veterinary practices and their affiliated pharmacies." The exclusive appointment in § 3.1 prohibits Supplier from "sell[ing] Products directly to Customers in the Territory, except as permitted by Section 3.3" (House Accounts only).

The Order Form, however, redefines "Products" as "all pet food manufactured by Supplier and sold under the Fouveirt brand. This includes the dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A to the Agreement, **including Veterinary Diet Products**, and Supplier's freeze-dried dog and cat treats, **as Supplier's product range is updated from time to time**" (Order Form § 1).

Under the order of precedence (Agreement § 2.3), the Order Form prevails over the Distribution Agreement body. The broader definition therefore controls.

**Why it matters.**

- Fouveirt's direct veterinary channel in New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, and Pennsylvania is now captured by the exclusivity grant. If a licensed veterinary practice in the Territory orders Fouveirt VetCare products directly from Fouveirt, that sale arguably violates § 3.1(b) because Veterinary Diet Products are now "Products" and the practice is a "Customer" (defined in Agreement § 1.1 as "any retail, wholesale or e-commerce account in the Territory that buys Products from Distributor for resale"—though a veterinary practice buying for its own patients may not fit that definition, the risk of a dispute is real).
- The "as Supplier's product range is updated from time to time" language means any new Fouveirt-brand pet food product launched in the future is automatically subject to Pruran's exclusive distribution rights in the Territory, without further negotiation.
- Fouveirt may not have intended this expansion. The Agreement was carefully drafted to exclude Veterinary Diet Products; the Order Form appears to have been drafted without that intent in mind.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend Order Form § 1 to conform to the Agreement's definition. Proposed language:

> **"Products"** means the dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A to the Agreement, excluding Veterinary Diet Products. If the Parties wish to add products to the scope of this Agreement, they shall do so by written amendment to Exhibit A.

If the business intent was to include Veterinary Diet Products in the distribution arrangement, then § 3.1 and the House Account carve-out in § 3.3 must be revised to preserve Fouveirt's direct veterinary channel. This requires a commercial decision from the client.

**Flag for client:** We need to confirm with Fouveirt's commercial team whether the inclusion of Veterinary Diet Products and the open-ended "updated from time to time" language was intentional. If not, this should be the first item raised with Pruran.

## Issue 2 – Service Level Schedule § 5.4 Overrides the Agreement's Food-Safety Liability Carve-Out

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 5.4; Distribution Agreement § 12.2 (Cap); Agreement § 12.3(d) (Exclusions from cap); Agreement § 8 (Product Handling and Food Safety); Agreement § 2.3 (Order of Precedence).

**Explanation.** Agreement § 12.2 caps each Party's aggregate liability at "the Service Fees paid under the Statement of Work in the 12 months before the claim." At the current Service Fee of $38,500/month (SOW § 4.1), that cap is approximately **$462,000**.

Agreement § 12.3(d) carves out from the cap: "Losses arising from Distributor's breach of Section 8 (Product Handling and Food Safety), including Recall and contamination costs and Customer chargebacks." This was intended to ensure that food-safety failures are not subject to the low cap.

However, SLS § 5.4 states: "Distributor's aggregate liability for Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures, **including any indemnity or recall costs**, is subject to Section 12.2 of the Agreement, **notwithstanding Section 12.3 of the Agreement**."

Under the order of precedence (Agreement § 2.3), the Service Level Schedule prevails over the Distribution Agreement body. SLS § 5.4 therefore successfully overrides § 12.3(d), bringing Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures back under the ~$462,000 cap.

**Why it matters.**

- A temperature excursion at either facility could render an entire inventory lot unsaleable. The value of 9,000 pallet positions of pet food could easily exceed $2–3 million.
- A recall triggered by a food-safety failure at Pruran's warehouse (e.g., pest contamination, temperature abuse causing spoilage) could generate costs—product retrieval, disposal, customer chargebacks, regulatory response, brand damage—well in excess of $462,000.
- Fouveirt would bear the excess above the cap with no contractual recourse against Pruran.
- This directly undermines the allocation in Agreement § 8.6, which provides that "Distributor shall bear all costs of a Recall … to the extent caused by Distributor's breach of this Section 8."

**Recommended fix.**

Delete or amend SLS § 5.4. Proposed replacement:

> **5.4 Liability for Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures.** Notwithstanding Section 12.2 of the Agreement, Distributor's liability for Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures, including any indemnity, recall, contamination, and customer chargeback costs arising therefrom, shall not be subject to the cap in Section 12.2 and shall be governed by Section 12.3(d) of the Agreement.

Alternatively, if the Parties want a cap on food-safety liability, it should be set at a level that reflects the actual risk (e.g., the value of inventory on hand plus a recall cost estimate), not the Service Fee.

**Flag for client:** This is a negotiated provision, not a drafting error. Pruran likely inserted it deliberately. We should confirm with the client whether this was discussed during the SOW restatement negotiation and whether the business team understood the effect.

## Issue 3 – 2026 Price Adjustment Notice Was Untimely; Dispute Unresolved

**Where:** SOW § 1.2; Distribution Agreement § 6.4.

**Explanation.** Agreement § 6.4 requires Supplier to deliver a price adjustment notice "at least 60 days before each anniversary of the Effective Date." The Effective Date is March 1, 2025, so the anniversary for the Contract Year beginning March 1, 2026 is March 1, 2026. Sixty days before that date is **January 1, 2026**. Fouveirt delivered the 2026 Price Adjustment Notice on **January 20, 2026**—20 days late. Pruran rejected it in writing on January 27, 2026.

SOW § 1.2 records the dispute and states that the SOW is entered "without prejudice to either Party's position." Section 6 of the Agreement is not amended.

**Why it matters.**

- Fouveirt almost certainly cannot enforce the 2026 price increase. The notice was outside the contractual window, and § 6.4 provides that "[i]f Supplier does not deliver a notice within that period, prices for the following Contract Year remain unchanged."
- The unresolved dispute creates friction and could be used by Pruran as leverage in other negotiations (e.g., the SOW restatement, service level discussions).
- Fouveirt is locked into the old pricing for the entire Contract Year (March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027) with no adjustment right.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Immediate:** Confirm internally that the next price adjustment notice (for the Contract Year beginning March 1, 2027) is calendarized with a deadline of **January 1, 2027**, and assign ownership. Build in internal lead time so the notice is prepared and sent by December 15, 2026 at the latest.
- **Negotiation:** If Fouveirt wishes to revisit the 2026 pricing, it could offer Pruran a concession elsewhere (e.g., on the SLS § 5.4 cap) in exchange for a mid-year price adjustment or a shorter notice period for future adjustments. However, the contractual position is weak, and we should advise the client accordingly.
- **Process:** Implement an internal compliance calendar for all time-sensitive contractual notices.

**Flag for client:** We need to confirm the business impact of losing the 2026 price increase (margins, volume assumptions) and whether the client wants to pursue a commercial resolution or accept the loss.

## Issue 4 – General Liability Cap Is Inadequate Relative to Commercial Value

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 12.2.

**Explanation.** The cap is "Service Fees paid under the Statement of Work in the 12 months before the claim"—approximately $462,000. The annual product flow under this agreement is $6.2 million (minimum purchase commitment). The cap is roughly 7.5% of the annual product value.

While § 12.3 carves out indemnity obligations, confidentiality breaches, gross negligence/wilful misconduct, and (subject to Issue 2 above) Section 8 breaches, many other claims fall within the cap:

- Pruran's breach of its marketing obligations (§ 9.1)
- Pruran's breach of territorial restrictions (§ 3.4)
- Pruran's failure to meet minimum purchase commitments (though the remedy is limited per Issue 5)
- General negligence short of gross negligence
- Breach of the SOW's operational obligations that don't rise to the level of a Temperature Excursion or Handling Failure

**Why it matters.** Fouveirt's primary commercial relationship in a four-state territory is protected by a liability cap tied to a warehousing fee, not to the value of the distribution arrangement. If Pruran's underperformance causes Fouveirt to lose customers or market share, Fouveirt's recovery is capped at ~$462,000.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend § 12.2 to use a more appropriate benchmark. Options:

> (a) "the total value of Products purchased by Distributor in the 12 months before the claim" (i.e., ~$6.2M); or
> (b) "two times the Service Fees paid under the Statement of Work in the 12 months before the claim" (i.e., ~$924,000); or
> (c) a fixed dollar amount negotiated to reflect the Parties' risk allocation.

At minimum, the cap should be decoupled from the Service Fee and tied to the product flow.

## Issue 5 – Minimum Purchase Commitment Remedies Are Limited and Do Not Include a Financial Make-Up

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 5.3; Order Form § 4.

**Explanation.** If Pruran fails to meet the $6.2M minimum in any Contract Year, Fouveirt's "sole remedies" are:

(a) terminate exclusivity (converting to non-exclusive), or
(b) terminate the Agreement entirely.

There is no right to a shortfall payment, liquidated damages, or the difference between the minimum and actual purchases.

**Why it matters.**

- If Pruran purchases $4M against a $6.2M minimum, Fouveirt's only options are to (a) open the territory to other distributors (which may dilute the relationship and create channel conflict) or (b) terminate (which is nuclear and may not be commercially desirable mid-term).
- Fouveirt has no financial remedy for the lost volume. The "sole remedies" language forecloses any claim for damages based on the shortfall.
- This weakens Fouveirt's incentive to monitor Pruran's purchasing and reduces Pruran's downside risk for under-ordering.

**Recommended fix.**

Add a shortfall payment provision. Proposed language for § 5.3:

> If Distributor fails to meet the minimum annual purchase commitment in any Contract Year, Distributor shall pay Supplier, within thirty (30) days after the end of that Contract Year, an amount equal to the difference between the minimum annual purchase commitment and the actual net purchases for that Contract Year, calculated at the prices in effect under Section 6.1. This payment is in addition to, and not in lieu of, Supplier's rights under clauses (a) and (b) above.

Alternatively, if a cash make-up is not commercially viable, at minimum remove the word "sole" to preserve Fouveirt's common-law rights for damages.

## Issue 6 – Fouveirt Is Not Named as Additional Insured on Pruran's Recall and Contamination Policy

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 15.

**Explanation.** Section 15(b) requires Distributor to carry "product contamination and recall insurance of at least $5,000,000." Section 15's final paragraph provides: "Each Party shall name the other as an additional insured on its **general liability policy** and shall provide certificates of insurance on request."

The additional-insured requirement is limited to the general liability policy. It does not extend to the product contamination and recall insurance.

**Why it matters.**

- If a recall occurs, Fouveirt (as the manufacturer and brand owner) will be the primary target of consumer claims, regulatory action, and customer chargebacks.
- Pruran's $5M recall/contamination policy is the most relevant coverage for this scenario, but Fouveirt is not an additional insured on it. Fouveirt would need to rely on Pruran's indemnity obligation (§ 11.2) and its own insurance.
- In a dispute over whether the recall was "caused by Distributor's breach of Section 8," Fouveirt would have to litigate to recover from Pruran, rather than claiming directly under Pruran's recall policy.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend § 15 to add:

> Distributor shall name Supplier as an additional insured on its product contamination and recall insurance policy, or shall provide Supplier with a loss-payee endorsement to the extent permitted by the policy.

At minimum, require Pruran to provide a certificate of the recall/contamination policy (not just the CGL) on request.

## Issue 7 – Service Fee Is Fixed With No Adjustment Mechanism

**Where:** SOW § 4.5.

**Explanation.** "The Service Fee is fixed through February 28, 2027. Any later change to the Service Fee requires a written amendment signed by both Parties."

Unlike product prices (which have the § 6.4 annual adjustment mechanism), the Service Fee has no built-in escalation. After February 28, 2027, any change requires bilateral agreement.

**Why it matters.**

- If inflation, labor costs, or energy costs rise, Pruran may seek a fee increase. Fouveirt has no contractual mechanism to resist or negotiate from a position of structural advantage.
- Conversely, if Fouveirt's volume decreases (e.g., due to the pricing issue in Issue 3), the fixed fee becomes a larger proportion of product value, increasing Fouveirt's effective cost per unit distributed.
- The 9,000-pallet-position threshold (SOW § 4.1) could be exceeded, triggering additional charges at $14.50/pallet-position/month (Annex 1), but the base fee itself cannot be adjusted downward.

**Recommended fix.**

Add a mutual adjustment mechanism. Proposed language:

> The Service Fee shall be reviewed annually at the same time as the price adjustment notice under Section 6.4 of the Agreement. Either Party may propose a change by written notice delivered at least sixty (60) days before the applicable Contract Year anniversary. If the Parties cannot agree, the Service Fee shall remain unchanged for that Contract Year.

This gives Fouveirt a structured opportunity to negotiate rather than being subject to an open-ended demand.

## Issue 8 – Supplier-Owned Inventory Reimbursed at "Standard Cost"

**Where:** SLS § 5.3; SOW § 3.6 (House Account and Consignment Handling).

**Explanation.** SOW § 3.6 contemplates that Fouveirt will consign inventory to Pruran's facilities for House Account fulfillment and Recall handling. SLS § 5.3 provides that if that Supplier-owned Inventory is damaged or destroyed by a Temperature Excursion or Handling Failure, "reimbursement is at Supplier's **standard cost**."

**Why it matters.**

- Standard cost (typically a historical or normalized manufacturing cost) is almost always lower than replacement cost or market value. If Fouveirt has invested in a new formulation or a limited-run product, standard cost may significantly undervalue the loss.
- This provision applies specifically to consigned inventory (House Account stock, recall staging), which is Fouveirt's property sitting in Pruran's warehouse. Fouveirt is paying Pruran $38,500/month to store it, and if Pruran damages it, Fouveirt recovers less than what it cost to make.
- Combined with the SLS § 5.4 cap (Issue 2), Fouveirt's total recovery for loss of consigned inventory is doubly limited.

**Recommended fix.**

Amend SLS § 5.3:

> For Supplier-owned Inventory, reimbursement shall be at the greater of (a) Supplier's standard cost plus a reasonable margin, or (b) the replacement cost of the affected Products as of the date of loss.

## Issue 9 – Forecast-Based Excusal for On-Time Shipment (SL-3)

**Where:** SLS § 4.2(b); Agreement § 5.2 (Forecasts are "non-binding").

**Explanation.** SLS § 4.2(b) excuses a failure to meet SL-3 (On-Time Shipment) "to the extent it is caused by … a Customer order that exceeds Distributor's most recent forecast by more than twenty percent (20%)."

Agreement § 5.2 makes forecasts "non-binding." Pruran provides a rolling six-month forecast by the 5th business day of each month.

**Why it matters.**

- Because forecasts are non-binding, Pruran has no penalty for providing an inaccurate (low) forecast. If Pruran's forecast is 10% below actual demand, any order spike above that low forecast triggers the 20% threshold and excuses late shipments.
- This creates a perverse incentive: the lower Pruran's forecast, the easier it is to excuse missed on-time shipments.
- Fouveirt's customers (pet specialty, grocery, mass-merchandise retail) expect reliable delivery. Chronic late shipments driven by this excuse could damage Fouveirt's brand with end customers, even though the failure is technically "excused."
- The service credit for SL-3 is only 1.5% of the monthly Service Fee (~$577), providing minimal financial deterrence.

**Recommended fix.**

Tighten the excusal. Proposed language:

> A failure to meet SL-3 is excused only to the extent it is caused by a Customer order that exceeds Distributor's most recent forecast by more than twenty percent (20%) **and** Distributor has used commercially reasonable efforts to meet the order within the stated timeframe, including by expediting carrier arrangements.

Alternatively, require forecasts to be "good faith" estimates and give Fouveirt the right to challenge a forecast that is manifestly unreasonable.

## Issue 10 – Recall Handling Labor Charged to Fouveirt When Recall Is Not Caused by Pruran's Breach

**Where:** SOW § 4.2(c); SOW Annex 1; Agreement § 8.6.

**Explanation.** SOW § 4.2(c) provides that "Recall handling labor, to the extent the Recall is not caused by Distributor's breach of Section 8 of the Agreement" is an additional charge at $46.00 per labor hour (Annex 1).

Agreement § 8.6 provides that "Supplier shall bear all other Recall costs" (i.e., those not caused by Distributor's breach of Section 8).

**Why it matters.**

- If a recall is triggered by a manufacturing defect at Fouveirt's Lancaster plant (the most common scenario for a food manufacturer), Fouveirt bears all recall costs under § 8.6 **and** pays Pruran $46/hour for the labor of executing the recall at Pruran's facilities (placing lots on hold, generating distribution reports, receiving and segregating returned product).
- A multi-lot recall across two facilities could involve hundreds of labor hours. At $46/hour, this could add $10,000–$50,000+ to an already expensive recall event.
- The rate is not negotiated against a market benchmark; it is set unilaterally in Annex 1.

**Recommended fix.**

This is a lower-priority item, but we should:

- Confirm that the $46/hour rate is reasonable compared to Pruran's actual labor costs (warehouse associate wages + overhead). If it is inflated, negotiate it down.
- Add a cap on recall handling labor charges per event (e.g., not to exceed $25,000 per recall without prior written agreement).
- Clarify that "Recall handling labor" does not include management-level time, travel, or overtime premiums.
