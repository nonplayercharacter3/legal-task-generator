## Issue 1 – Order Form's "Products" Definition Swallows the Veterinary Diet Exclusion

**Where:** Order Form § 1; Agreement § 1.1 ("Products" definition); Agreement § 3.1; Recital B; Agreement § 2.3 (Order of Precedence).

**The problem.** The Agreement defines "Products" as "dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A, **excluding Veterinary Diet Products**" (Agreement § 1.1). The Order Form defines "Products" as "all pet food manufactured by Supplier and sold under the Fouveirt brand. This includes the dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A to the Agreement, **including Veterinary Diet Products**, and Supplier's freeze-dried dog and cat treats, as Supplier's product range is updated from time to time" (Order Form § 1).

Under the order of precedence (Agreement § 2.3), the Order Form prevails over the Agreement body. The broader Order Form definition therefore controls. Because § 3.1 grants Distributor **exclusive** distribution rights for "Products" in the Territory, and § 3.1(b) prohibits Supplier from "sell[ing] Products directly to Customers in the Territory," the practical effect is that Distributor holds exclusivity over Veterinary Diet Products in New York, New Jersey, Connecticut and Pennsylvania.

This directly contradicts Recital B, which states that Supplier "sells its Veterinary Diet Products through its own veterinary clinic channel, directly to licensed veterinary practices and their affiliated pharmacies." If a veterinary practice in the Territory qualifies as a "Customer" (defined in Agreement § 1.1 as "any retail, wholesale or e-commerce account in the Territory that buys Products from Distributor for resale"), Supplier's existing vet channel is blocked. Even if a veterinary practice is not a "Customer" (because it buys from Supplier, not Distributor), § 3.1(b) still prohibits Supplier from selling Products directly to Customers in the Territory, and the exclusivity in § 3.1(a) prevents appointing any other distributor.

**Why it matters.** Fouveirt's veterinary diet line ("Fouveirt VetCare") appears to be a distinct and potentially high-margin channel. Losing exclusivity protection over that line in four states could strand existing veterinary accounts or force Fouveirt to route them through Pruran, which is a pet-specialty/grocery/mass-merchandise distributor, not a veterinary channel partner.

**Recommended fix.** Amend Order Form § 1 to conform to the Agreement's definition, or add an express carve-out:

> *"Products" means the dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A to the Agreement, excluding Veterinary Diet Products. For the avoidance of doubt, Veterinary Diet Products are not subject to the exclusive appointment in Section 3.1 of the Agreement and Supplier may continue to sell Veterinary Diet Products directly through its veterinary clinic channel in the Territory.*

**Client confirmation needed.** We should ask the client whether the inclusion of Veterinary Diet Products in the Order Form was a drafting error or a deliberate commercial decision. If deliberate, the exclusivity grant needs to be re-scoped to protect the vet channel (e.g., by excluding "licensed veterinary practices and their affiliated pharmacies" from the definition of "Customer").

## Issue 2 – Service Level Schedule § 5.4 Caps Temperature Excursion and Handling Failure Liability at ~$462,000

**Where:** Service Level Schedule § 5.4; Agreement § 12.2 (Cap); Agreement § 12.3(d) (Exclusion from cap); Agreement § 8.6 (Recall costs); Agreement § 2.3 (Order of Precedence).

**The problem.** Agreement § 12.3(d) provides that the liability cap in § 12.2 does **not** apply to "Losses arising from Distributor's breach of Section 8 (Product Handling and Food Safety), including Recall and contamination costs and Customer chargebacks." This was clearly negotiated to protect Fouveirt from bearing the full cost of a food-safety failure caused by Pruran.

However, SLS § 5.4 states: "Distributor's aggregate liability for Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures, **including any indemnity or recall costs**, is subject to Section 12.2 of the Agreement, **notwithstanding Section 12.3** of the Agreement."

Because the SLS has higher precedence than the Agreement body (Agreement § 2.3), SLS § 5.4 effectively carves Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures back under the cap. The cap is "Service Fees paid under the Statement of Work in the 12 months before the claim" (Agreement § 12.2). At the current Service Fee of $38,500/month (SOW § 4.1), the cap is **$462,000**.

**Why it matters.** A temperature excursion in the canned product zone (e.g., a refrigeration failure overnight at the Secaucus or Allentown facility) could render thousands of cases unsaleable, trigger a recall, and generate consumer/animal illness claims. Customer chargebacks from national retail accounts could easily exceed $462,000. Under SLS § 5.4, Fouveirt's recovery from Pruran for those losses is capped at $462,000 regardless of actual damage. The $5,000,000 product contamination and recall insurance Pruran is required to carry (Agreement § 15(b)) protects against third-party claims but does not increase Fouveirt's direct contractual recovery.

**Recommended fix.** Delete SLS § 5.4 in its entirety, or amend it to preserve the § 12.3(d) exclusion for recall costs and third-party claims while capping only first-party product loss:

> *Distributor's aggregate liability for product loss resulting from a Temperature Excursion or Handling Failure (excluding Recall costs, Customer chargebacks, and third-party claims) is subject to Section 12.2 of the Agreement. For the avoidance of doubt, Section 12.3(d) of the Agreement applies in full to all Losses arising from Distributor's breach of Section 8 of the Agreement.*

**Client confirmation needed.** We should ask whether SLS § 5.4 was a negotiated concession Fouveirt made in exchange for something else (e.g., the fixed Service Fee through February 2027, or the addition of the Secondary Facility). If it was a deliberate trade-off, the client should understand the quantified exposure before we raise it with Pruran.

## Issue 3 – 2026 Price Adjustment Notice Was Untimely; Prices Likely Locked for the 2026 Contract Year

**Where:** Agreement § 6.4; SOW § 1.2; SOW § 4.5.

**The problem.** Section 6.4 requires Supplier to deliver price adjustment notice "at least 60 days before each anniversary of the Effective Date." The Effective Date is March 1, 2025. The anniversary for the Contract Year beginning March 1, 2026 is March 1, 2026. Sixty days before March 1, 2026 is **January 1, 2026**. Supplier delivered the 2026 Price Adjustment Notice on **January 20, 2026** — twenty days late.

Section 6.4 is unambiguous: "If Supplier does not deliver a notice within that period, prices for the following Contract Year remain unchanged." Pruran rejected the notice in writing on January 27, 2026 (SOW § 1.2). On the face of the contract, Pruran's rejection is correct.

The SOW was signed "without prejudice" to either party's position (SOW § 1.2), meaning the dispute is formally unresolved, but the contractual language gives Fouveirt little leverage. The Service Fee is fixed through February 28, 2027 (SOW § 4.5), so Fouveirt is paying an increasing Service Fee on a flat product price for at least another twelve months.

**Why it matters.** If Fouveirt's input costs (grain, protein, packaging, freight) have risen, it is absorbing the margin compression on a $6.2M annual purchase commitment with no contractual right to pass through cost increases until the next anniversary (March 1, 2027), by which time it must give notice by January 1, 2027.

**Recommended fix.**

- *Short-term:* Negotiate a side letter or amendment to SOW § 1.2 that resolves the price dispute. Options include (a) Fouveirt concedes the 2026 year at current prices in exchange for Pruran agreeing to a mid-year adjustment mechanism (e.g., a CPI or input-cost trigger), or (b) the parties agree the notice was effective despite the 20-day delay, with Pruran waiving its objection.
- *Long-term:* Amend Agreement § 6.4 to add a cure period or a longer notice window. Proposed language:

> *Supplier may adjust prices once per Contract Year by written notice delivered at least sixty (60) days before the applicable anniversary. If Supplier fails to deliver timely notice, Supplier may deliver a late notice up to fifteen (15) days after the anniversary, in which case the adjustment takes effect on the first day of the following month rather than on the anniversary.*

**Client confirmation needed.** We need to know (a) what the proposed 2026 price increase was and what cost drivers prompted it, and (b) whether the client is prepared to concede the 2026 year in exchange for a structural fix, or whether it wants to litigate the timeliness question (which, on the plain language, looks weak).

## Issue 4 – Open-Ended Product Scope in Order Form Automatically Expands Exclusivity

**Where:** Order Form § 1 ("as Supplier's product range is updated from time to time"); Agreement § 3.1.

**The problem.** Even setting aside the Veterinary Diet issue (Issue 1), the Order Form's definition of Products includes "Supplier's freeze-dried dog and cat treats, **as Supplier's product range is updated from time to time**." Combined with the Order Form's precedence over the Agreement, this means any new product Fouveirt launches under the Fouveirt brand automatically falls within the exclusive distribution grant. Fouveirt could find itself unable to launch a new product line in the four-state Territory without routing it through Pruran, or unable to sell a new product directly to a national account that is not on the House Account list.

**Why it matters.** This is a strategic constraint on Fouveirt's product development and channel strategy for the duration of the Term (through at least February 28, 2028, plus any renewal).

**Recommended fix.** Amend Order Form § 1 to require mutual agreement before new products are added:

> *"Products" means the dry and canned dog and cat food listed in Exhibit A to the Agreement, and such additional products as the Parties agree in writing to add by amendment to Exhibit A. New products do not become "Products" under this Agreement unless and until added to Exhibit A by written amendment signed by both Parties.*

## Issue 5 – Supplier-Owned Consigned Inventory Reimbursed at Standard Cost Only

**Where:** SLS § 5.3; SOW § 3.6; SOW § 2.2 ("Inventory" definition).

**The problem.** SOW § 3.6 allows Supplier to consign inventory to the Facilities for House Account fulfillment and Recall handling. SLS § 5.3 provides that if Supplier-owned Inventory is damaged, destroyed or rendered unsaleable by a Temperature Excursion or Handling Failure, "reimbursement is at Supplier's **standard cost**." Standard cost is typically well below the invoice price or retail value. If Fouveirt consigns finished goods that carry a 40–60% gross margin, a loss event reimburses only the cost of goods, not the lost profit.

Compounding this, SLS § 5.4 (Issue 2) caps even that standard-cost reimbursement at $462,000 for Temperature Excursions and Handling Failures.

**Why it matters.** Fouveirt bears the margin loss on consigned inventory that is destroyed through Pruran's fault, and even the cost recovery is capped.

**Recommended fix.** Amend SLS § 5.3 to reimburse at invoice price (or, for consigned goods, at the price that would have been charged to the House Account):

> *For Supplier-owned Inventory, reimbursement shall be at the invoice price that would have applied had the Product been sold to the applicable House Account or Customer, less any estimated selling expenses.*

And address the cap issue per Issue 2.

## Issue 6 – Liability Cap Tied to Service Fees Is Disproportionately Low

**Where:** Agreement § 12.2; SOW § 4.1.

**The problem.** The general liability cap is "Service Fees paid under the Statement of Work in the 12 months before the claim" (Agreement § 12.2). At $38,500/month, that is $462,000. The annual product purchase commitment is $6,200,000 (Order Form § 4.1). The cap is roughly 7.5% of annual product flow. For a non-food-safety breach (e.g., a chronic failure to meet order accuracy that costs Fouveirt a major retail account), $462,000 may not compensate the actual loss.

**Why it matters.** While § 12.3 carves out several categories from the cap (indemnity, confidentiality, gross negligence, and — subject to Issue 2 — Section 8 breaches), the residual cap for other breaches (e.g., failure to maintain adequate sales force under § 9.1, breach of territorial restrictions under § 3.4) is low.

**Recommended fix.** If the client wants a higher general cap, propose tying it to a percentage of annual purchases:

> *Each Party's aggregate liability arising out of or relating to this Agreement shall not exceed the lesser of (a) the total net invoiced price of Products purchased by Distributor in the twelve (12) months before the claim, or (b) $2,000,000.*

This is a negotiating ask; Pruran will likely resist. But it is worth understanding the current cap before we go back to them.

## Issue 7 – No Express Risk-of-Loss Allocation for Supplier-Owned Consigned Inventory

**Where:** SOW § 3.6; SOW § 2.2; Agreement § 7.2.

**The problem.** Agreement § 7.2 passes title and risk of loss to Distributor "on delivery under Section 7.1" — but that applies to Products sold to Distributor. For Supplier-owned consigned inventory under SOW § 3.6, the SOW says it is "held as Supplier's property" but does not state when (or whether) risk of loss transfers to Distributor. SLS § 5.3 implies Distributor reimburses for losses caused by Temperature Excursions or Handling Failures, but does not address losses from causes outside those categories (e.g., fire, flood, theft, or a third-party warehouse incident not attributable to a "Handling Failure" as defined).

**Why it matters.** If a fire at the Allentown facility destroys consigned inventory, and the fire is not caused by a Temperature Excursion or Handling Failure, it is unclear whether Distributor bears the loss or whether Fouveirt does. Fouveirt's own property insurance may cover it, but if it does not (or if there is a deductible), Fouveirt could be out of pocket.

**Recommended fix.** Add to SOW § 3.6:

> *Risk of loss for Supplier-owned Inventory transfers to Distributor upon receipt at a Facility and reverts to Supplier upon release to Supplier or its designee. Distributor shall be responsible for loss or damage to Supplier-owned Inventory while in its care, custody or control, except to the extent caused by Supplier's negligence or a force majeure event under Section 16.6 of the Agreement.*

## Issue 8 – House Account List Is Static and Excludes Future National Accounts

**Where:** Agreement § 3.3; Exhibit B; Order Form § 3.2.

**The problem.** The House Account carve-out (which permits Supplier to sell directly and excludes those sales from the minimum purchase commitment) covers only three named companies: Chewzy Online Pet Supply, PetHarbor Superstores, and Brightmart Wholesale Club (Exhibit B). If Fouveirt onboards a new national account (e.g., a new e-commerce platform or a regional chain that goes national), that account is not a House Account, and sales to it in the Territory would violate § 3.1(b) unless routed through Pruran.

**Why it matters.** This is a growing constraint as Fouveirt's business evolves. Each new national account requires an amendment to Exhibit B, giving Pruran a veto (since amendments require both parties' signature under § 16.7).

**Recommended fix.** Amend Exhibit B or § 3.3 to include a mechanism for adding House Accounts:

> *Supplier may add accounts to Exhibit B by written notice to Distributor, provided the account is a national or multi-regional chain with at least ten (10) locations in the Territory. Such additions are effective upon notice and do not require Distributor's consent, so long as the total volume sold to added House Accounts does not exceed [X]% of Distributor's minimum annual purchase commitment in any Contract Year.*

Alternatively, if the client prefers a simpler approach, negotiate a standing right to add up to two (2) House Accounts per Contract Year by notice.

## Issue 9 – SOW "Without Prejudice" Language Leaves Price Dispute in Limbo

**Where:** SOW § 1.2.

**The problem.** The SOW was executed "without prejudice to either Party's position on the 2026 Price Adjustment Notice." This means both parties are bound by the new SOW (including the $38,500/month Service Fee and the Secondary Facility) while the underlying price dispute remains formally open. Pruran could argue that Fouveirt's continued performance under the SOW (paying Service Fees, shipping Products) constitutes an implied waiver of its price adjustment claim, or at minimum creates an equitable estoppel argument.

**Why it matters.** The longer the dispute sits unresolved, the weaker Fouveirt's position becomes. Pruran is performing (warehousing, shipping) and invoicing Service Fees without deduction, which supports its argument that the parties are operating as if the 2026 prices are the original prices.

**Recommended fix.** Resolve the price dispute (see Issue 3) before the next quarterly business review. If Fouveirt concedes the 2026 year, get Pruran's agreement in writing that the concession does not set a precedent for future years and that the § 6.4 notice deadline remains 60 days (or is amended per the proposed fix in Issue 3).

## Issue 10 – Email Thread Creates Implicit Standing Consent for Stretch Wrap Tagging

**Where:** Email thread, Message 3 (Marisol Feng, February 20, 2025); Agreement § 9.3.

**The problem.** Agreement § 9.3 prohibits Distributor from "repackage[ing], relabel[ing] or alter[ing] any Product or its packaging without Supplier's prior written consent." In the email thread, Marisol stated that Pruran's WMS location tag on stretch wrap "fits within Section 9.3 of the Agreement, and no consent is needed for it." While a stretch wrap tag is arguably not "packaging" (it is a temporary logistics label that comes off when the pallet is broken down), Marisol's statement can be read as a standing written consent or waiver for this practice.

**Why it matters.** This is low-risk in practice, but if a dispute ever arises about label integrity (e.g., a regulatory inspection questions whether the pallet was altered in transit), Pruran will point to the email as Supplier's authorization. It also sets a precedent that informal email confirmations can modify contractual requirements.

**Recommended fix.** No immediate action required, but in the next amendment or side letter, add a clarifying sentence to § 9.3:

> *For the avoidance of doubt, Distributor's application of internal WMS location tags to stretch wrap on pallets, which are removed before case-level picking, does not constitute relabeling or alteration under this Section 9.3.*

This converts the implicit consent into an express, bounded one.

## Issue 11 – Minimum Purchase Commitment "Sole Remedies" Language Limits Fouveirt's Recourse for Shortfall

**Where:** Agreement § 5.3; Order Form § 4.

**The problem.** Section 5.3 states that if Distributor fails to meet the $6.2M minimum, Supplier's remedies are "as its **sole remedies** for that shortfall": (a) convert to non-exclusive, or (b) terminate. Fouveirt cannot claim damages for lost profits attributable to the shortfall (e.g., if Pruran under-purchases and Fouveirt's plant runs below capacity, the idle capacity cost is not recoverable).

**Why it matters.** This is a standard feature of distribution agreements and likely was negotiated. However, combined with the low liability cap (Issue 6), Fouveirt's financial protection if Pruran chronically under-purchases but does not formally "fail" the minimum (e.g., purchases $5.9M against a $6.2M commitment in a year where Fouveirt's costs have risen) is limited.

**Recommended fix.** This is harder to fix without Pruran's agreement. If the client wants stronger protection, propose:

> *If Distributor's purchases in any Contract Year are less than ninety percent (90%) of the minimum annual purchase commitment, Distributor shall pay Supplier the difference between actual purchases and ninety percent of the minimum as a capacity reservation fee, in addition to Supplier's rights under this Section 5.3.*

This is an aggressive ask and may not be accepted, but it is worth understanding the current gap.
