## Executive Summary

I have reviewed all five documents in the matter folder. Two issues are critical and should be raised with Seller before we respond on the deal documents: (1) the survival period for general representations in the SPA is approximately 90 days, a dramatic reduction from the 18 months contemplated in the LOI; and (2) the consent of Northvale Compute LLC — the customer representing ~41% of Company revenue — is not a condition to Closing, creating a material post-closing revenue risk. ## Issue 1 (Critical): Survival Period for General Representations Is ~90 Days, Not 18 Months

**Where:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Survival Date"): "the date that is sixty (60) days after delivery of the Closing Statement."
- SPA § 2.4(a): Closing Statement is due "within 30 days after the Closing Date."
- SPA § 9.1: General reps survive "until the Survival Date."
- SPA § 9.6: Claims must be noticed "before the Survival Date."
- LOI § 4.1: "The general representations and warranties of Seller will survive Closing for **18 months** after Closing."

**Why it matters:** On the SPA's own timeline, the Survival Date falls no later than 90 days after Closing (30 days for the Closing Statement + 60 days). The LOI contemplated 18 months (~547 days). This is a reduction of roughly 83% in the window during which Voutuv can discover and assert claims for breaches of Seller's general representations — including financial statements (§ 4.5), material contracts (§ 4.6), product warranty (§ 4.8), environmental matters (§ 4.9), intellectual property (§ 4.10), and litigation/compliance (§ 4.11). For a manufacturing business with product warranties, environmental obligations, and complex IP, 90 days is an extremely short period to surface post-closing issues. Many warranty claims, environmental liabilities, and contract disputes will not manifest within 90 days.

**Recommendation:** Restore the survival period to 18 months from Closing, consistent with the LOI. Proposed language for SPA § 1.1:

> "**Survival Date**" means the date that is eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date.

And conforming edits to § 9.1 and § 9.6. If Seller resists 18 months, a fallback of 12 months is the minimum I would recommend, with the Fundamental Representations retaining their statute-of-limitations-plus-60-days tail (which is already in the SPA and consistent with the LOI).

## Issue 2 (Critical): Northvale Compute LLC Consent Is Not a Condition to Closing

**Where:**
- SPA Schedule 4.6, item 1: Identifies the Thermal Systems Master Supply Agreement with Northvale Compute LLC as a Material Contract. States: "The agreement requires Northvale Compute LLC's prior written consent to any change of control of the Company. If that consent is not obtained, Northvale Compute LLC may terminate the agreement on 30 days' notice." Also notes this contract represented "approximately 41% of the Company's revenue for fiscal year 2025."
- SPA Schedule 7.2(c) (Required Consents): Lists only the landlord consent for the Austin, Texas facility. The Northvale consent is **not** listed.
- SPA § 7.2(c): Buyer's closing condition requires delivery of "each of the consents listed in Schedule 7.2(c)."
- SPA § 6.5: "Obtaining any such consent, other than the consents listed in Schedule 7.2(c), shall not be a condition to Closing."

**Why it matters:** The SPA structure means Voutuv can be compelled to close (or choose to close) without having obtained Northvale's consent. Post-closing, Northvale could terminate the Master Supply Agreement on 30 days' notice, eliminating ~41% of the Company's revenue. The purchase price of $214.5 million presumably reflects the value of that revenue stream. There is no indemnification carve-out or specific protection for this risk; it would fall under the general rep in § 4.6 (Material Contracts) and be subject to the short survival period (Issue 1), the deductible, and the 10% cap.

**Recommendation:** Add the Northvale consent to Schedule 7.2(c) so that its delivery becomes a condition to Buyer's obligation to close. Alternatively, if Seller believes the consent is a formality, require Seller to deliver the consent (or evidence of Northvale's written acknowledgment that it will not terminate) as a closing deliverable under § 3.2(a). Proposed addition to Schedule 7.2(c):

> 2. Consent of Northvale Compute LLC to the change of control of the Company resulting from the sale of the Shares, pursuant to the Thermal Systems Master Supply Agreement identified in Schedule 4.6, item 1.

**Fact needed from client:** Has the Northvale consent request already been made? What is the status and expected timeline? If Northvale has signaled it may use the change of control as leverage to renegotiate pricing, that changes the risk calculus.

## Issue 3 (High): Bill of Sale Schedule A Is Entirely Placeholder

**Where:**
- Bill of Sale, Schedule A, Parts 1 and 2: Every entry for manufacturer/model, serial number, current location, patent/application number, and copyright registration number is marked [●].

**Why it matters:** The Transferred Test Assets are a material component of the deal (Recital C; SPA § 6.4; SPA § 4.10). If the specific assets are not identified before closing, Voutuv cannot verify that it is receiving what it paid for, cannot confirm the IP is validly owned by the Transferor, and cannot assess whether the equipment is in working condition. The SPA makes delivery of the Bill of Sale and transfer of the assets a closing condition (§ 7.2(f) via § 3.2(a)(iii)), but a Bill of Sale with an empty schedule provides no meaningful protection.

**Recommendation:** Require Seller to populate Schedule A in full before the SPA is finalized (or as a condition to Closing). At minimum, the test equipment should be identified by manufacturer, model, and serial number, and the IP should be identified by registration/application number or, for unregistered IP, by specific description. Consider adding a representation in the Bill of Sale that the assets listed in Schedule A are complete and accurate.

**Fact needed from client:** Has technical due diligence identified the specific test equipment and IP to be transferred? If so, that list should be the basis for Schedule A.

## Issue 4 (High): TSA Service Fees Are Blank

**Where:**
- TSA, Schedule A: Monthly Service Fee for ERP, Payroll, and Field-Service Dispatch are all $[●].

**Why it matters:** The TSA is a closing deliverable (SPA § 3.2(a)(ii)) and a condition to Buyer's obligation to close (SPA § 7.2(f)). Voutuv will be contractually obligated to pay Service Fees for up to 9 months (plus a 3-month extension) but the fees are not stated. The LOI (§ 5.2) provides that pricing will be "at Seller's cost, without mark-up," and the TSA (§ 5.1) implements that, but "cost" is defined functionally (allocated personnel, pass-throughs, system/license allocation) and will be determined by Seller's accounting. Without stated fees, Voutuv has no benchmark to assess whether invoices are reasonable, and the dispute mechanism (§ 5.3) will be the only protection.

**Recommendation:** Require Seller to populate the monthly fees in Schedule A before signing, based on the prior 12 months of actual cost allocation. If Seller resists stating fixed fees, add a cap or a most-favored-cost provision, and require Seller to deliver a cost allocation methodology as an exhibit to the TSA.

## Issue 5 (High): TSA Lacks Service Level Remedies

**Where:**
- TSA, Schedule A, "Service Levels" paragraph: Requires Provider to meet pre-Closing response times for Field-Service Dispatch and to report monthly on performance. No remedy is specified for failure to meet those levels.
- TSA § 4.2 (Standard of Performance): General standard but no specific SLA metrics or consequences.

**Why it matters:** The Company services cooling systems for hyperscale data centers, where customer contracts likely impose their own SLAs (response times, uptime). If the dispatch platform fails to meet response times during the transition, the Company could breach its customer contracts, incurring liquidated damages or termination rights. The TSA provides no service credits, no termination right for chronic SLA failure, and no specific indemnity for customer claims caused by dispatch failures. The general liability cap (§ 9.3) is limited to total Service Fees paid and payable, which may be modest.

**Recommendation:** Add a service credit mechanism (e.g., 5% of the monthly Field-Service Dispatch fee for each month in which the response-time target is missed by more than 10%) and a termination right if Provider fails to meet the service level for three consecutive months. Alternatively, expand the indemnity in § 9.4 to cover third-party claims (i.e., customer claims) caused by Provider's failure to meet the stated service levels, not just gross negligence or willful misconduct.

## Issue 6 (Moderate): Ostrander Freight Agreement — Renewal Timing and Termination Risk

**Where:**
- Ostrander Freight Services Agreement § 4.1–4.2: Initial Term is 3 years from March 1, 2024 (i.e., through approximately February 28, 2027). Auto-renews for 1-year periods. Either party may prevent renewal by notice at least 90 days before the end of the then-current term.
- Ostrander Freight Services Agreement § 11.1: Either party may terminate for convenience on 90 days' notice.
- Ostrander Freight Services Agreement § 5.4: Carrier may increase line-haul rates once per year by up to 4%.
- SPA Schedule 4.6, item 3: Lists the Ostrander agreement as a Material Contract.

**Why it matters:** The Ostrander agreement is a Material Contract that Voutuv inherits at closing. Three points warrant attention:

1. **Renewal decision timing.** The 90-day non-renewal notice for the Initial Term is due approximately December 1, 2026 — likely before or shortly after the expected Closing. If the Company (under Seller's control pre-closing) fails to give non-renewal notice, the agreement auto-renews for another year, locking Voutuv into the relationship (and the 4% annual escalator) for at least 12 more months.
2. **Convenience termination.** Ostrander can terminate on 90 days' notice at any time. If Ostrander terminates shortly after closing, the Company would need to find replacement freight capacity for shipping cooling systems to data center sites, which could disrupt operations.
3. **Rate escalator.** The 4% annual increase is a cost risk, though modest relative to the overall deal.

The assignment clause (§ 12.3) is favorable: a change in ownership or control is expressly not an assignment and does not require consent. So there is no consent issue.

**Recommendation:** (a) Confirm with the client whether the Company has given or plans to give non-renewal notice before the December 2026 deadline. If the client wants the flexibility to switch carriers post-closing, ensure notice is given pre-closing (as a condition or covenant in the SPA). (b) Consider adding a covenant in the SPA requiring Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to maintain the Ostrander relationship through the Closing, or to give non-renewal notice if the client prefers to transition to a different carrier. (c) The 4% escalator is acceptable but should be noted in the client's post-closing budget.

**Fact needed from client:** Does Voutuv intend to continue with Ostrander post-closing or is a carrier transition part of the integration plan? Has the Company already given or received any notice regarding the Ostrander agreement's renewal?

## Issue 7 (Moderate): Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction (TSA in Texas, SPA in Delaware)

**Where:**
- SPA § 11.9: Delaware governing law.
- SPA § 11.10: Exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware (Chancery or Superior Court).
- TSA § 12.2: Texas governing law; exclusive jurisdiction in Harris County, Texas. Expressly states that SPA §§ 11.9 and 11.10 "do not apply to this Agreement" and that this is "expressly [provided] otherwise for purposes of Section 11.4 of the Purchase Agreement."

**Why it matters:** The TSA carves itself out of the SPA's governing law and jurisdiction provisions. This means that a dispute about transition services (e.g., a claim that Provider failed to perform the ERP or dispatch services) will be litigated in Texas under Texas law, while a dispute about the SPA (e.g., an indemnification claim for a breached representation) will be in Delaware under Delaware law. If a single factual scenario gives rise to both types of claims (e.g., a dispatch failure causes a customer breach, which is also evidence of a pre-closing representation breach), Voutuv could face parallel proceedings in two jurisdictions. The TSA's express override of SPA § 11.4 (order of precedence) makes this intentional, not an oversight.

**Recommendation:** Seek to conform the TSA to Delaware governing law and jurisdiction, consistent with the SPA and the Bill of Sale (which uses Delaware law per Bill of Sale § 8.3). If Seller insists on Texas (perhaps because the services are performed from Texas or the relevant personnel are in Texas), at minimum add a provision that permits Voutuv to bring any TSA claim in the Delaware courts if it is ancillary to or arises in connection with a claim under the SPA.

## Issue 8 (Moderate): MAE Definition — Broad Industry Exclusion

**Where:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Material Adverse Effect"): Excludes "conditions generally affecting the data center cooling industry."

**Why it matters:** The carve-out for industry-wide conditions is broader than a typical "general economic conditions" exclusion. If the data center cooling sector experiences a downturn (e.g., a pause in hyperscale capex), the Company's revenue and earnings could decline significantly, but Voutuv would not be able to walk away under the MAE condition (§ 7.2(e)) or to claim a breach of the bring-down of representations (§ 7.2(a)) if the decline is characterized as "generally affecting the industry." The disproportionate-effect carve-back ("except to the extent such effects disproportionately affect the Company") provides some protection, but it is a high bar — Voutuv would need to show the Company is hit harder than its peers.

**Recommendation:** Narrow the industry exclusion to "conditions generally affecting the data center cooling industry that do not disproportionately affect the Company relative to its peers," or delete the industry-specific exclusion and rely on the general economic conditions exclusion plus the disproportionate-effect carve-back. At minimum, confirm through due diligence that the Company's customer base (particularly Northvale at 41%) is not so concentrated that an industry-wide slowdown would hit it disproportionately, which would actually help Voutuv under the carve-back.

## Issue 9 (Moderate): Bill of Sale Contains No Independent Representations or Warranties

**Where:**
- Bill of Sale: No section contains reps or warranties from Transferor regarding the condition of the Test Equipment, the validity or ownership of the Transferred IP, or the absence of claims or encumbrances.
- Bill of Sale § 7.3: "Nothing in this Bill of Sale expands, limits or otherwise modifies any representation, warranty, covenant or agreement of Seller in the Purchase Agreement."

**Why it matters:** The Bill of Sale is a bare conveyance. Voutuv's protection regarding the Transferred Test Assets flows entirely through the SPA — specifically § 4.10 (IP) and § 4.6 (Material Contracts, to the extent any vendor agreements relate to the test equipment). Those reps are subject to the short survival period (Issue 1), the deductible, and the cap. If a defect in the test equipment or a gap in the IP chain of title is discovered after the Survival Date, Voutuv has no claim under the Bill of Sale itself. The Transferor (a Seller subsidiary) is not a party to the SPA and has no direct indemnification obligation to Voutuv.

**Recommendation:** Add a short article of representations to the Bill of Sale in which Transferor represents that: (a) it is the sole and exclusive owner of the Transferred Assets; (b) the Transferred Assets are free and clear of all Encumbrances; (c) the Test Equipment is in good working order and condition; and (d) to its knowledge, the Transferred IP does not infringe the rights of any third party. Make Voutuv an express third-party beneficiary of those reps (it already is under § 8.2, but that should be extended to cover the new reps). Alternatively, if Seller resists adding reps to the Bill of Sale, ensure the SPA survival period is extended (Issue 1) so that Voutuv has adequate time to discover and claim for any defects through the SPA indemnification.

## Issue 10 (Moderate): Adjustment Escrow May Be Inadequate

**Where:**
- SPA § 2.2(a): Adjustment Escrow Amount is $2,000,000.
- SPA § 1.1: Target Working Capital is $18,750,000.
- SPA § 2.4(d): If final Closing Working Capital is below the estimate, the shortfall is paid from the escrow and "to the extent the Adjustment Escrow Amount is insufficient, by Seller."

**Why it matters:** The $2 million escrow is ~10.7% of Target Working Capital. If the Company's actual working capital at closing is materially below target (e.g., due to unusual payables timing, inventory write-downs, or receivable issues), the escrow may be exhausted before the full adjustment is made. While § 2.4(d) provides that Seller must cover the shortfall, this converts the adjustment into an unsecured claim against Seller post-closing. If Seller has distributed the remaining purchase price, collection could be difficult.

**Recommendation:** Assess the expected range of working capital variance based on financial due diligence. If the potential shortfall could exceed $2 million, increase the escrow or add a holdback from the purchase price. Alternatively, require Seller to maintain a minimum net worth or liquidity covenant post-closing to support the indemnification and adjustment obligations.

**Fact needed from client:** What is the range of working capital variance identified in financial due diligence? Is $2 million sufficient to cover the downside case?
