## Issue 1 – Non-Competition Covenant Is Far Broader Than the LOI and Would Encompass Seller's Retained Businesses

**Where:** SPA §6.4 (Non-Competition) vs. LOI ¶8.

**What the LOI provided:** A three-year non-compete limited to "the development or publishing of tactical role-playing games for console and PC only," with express carve-outs for (i) the Bikas Pocket mobile casual game publishing business, (ii) Seller's continued ownership, development and licensing of the Cinderloom engine, and (iii) passive ownership of less than 5% of publicly traded securities.

**What the SPA provides:** A three-year non-compete covering "any Competing Business anywhere in the world," where "Competing Business" is defined by cross-reference to §1.1 of the Key Employee Agreement as "the development, publishing or distribution of any video game in any genre, on any platform." The only carve-out is passive ownership of less than 5% of publicly traded securities. There is no carve-out for Bikas Pocket, no carve-out for the Cinderloom engine, and no genre or platform limitation.

**Why it matters:** As drafted, Bikas would be in breach of §6.4 simply by continuing to operate its existing mobile casual game publishing business (Bikas Pocket) or by continuing to develop and license the Cinderloom engine to third parties. Both are core, retained, revenue-generating businesses that the LOI expressly preserved. The cross-reference to the Key Employee Agreement's definition is particularly inappropriate: that definition was negotiated to restrict a single employee (Vasquez-Ilunga) from competing with his employer, not to foreclose an entire parent company's business lines worldwide.

**Recommendation:** Restore the LOI formulation. The non-compete should be limited to tactical role-playing games on console and PC, with express carve-outs for (a) the Seller Retained Business (Bikas Pocket) and (b) Seller's ownership, development, and licensing of the Cinderloom engine. Delete the cross-reference to the Key Employee Agreement and define "Competing Business" directly in the SPA with the appropriate scope.

## Issue 2 – Kestrel Motion License Terminates Automatically at Closing; SPA Does Not Address This

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement §7.3(d) (Change of Control); SPA (silence); SPA §4.8 (IP representation); SPA §4.10 (Material Contracts representation); SPA Schedule 4.10, item 1.

**The problem:** The Key Employee Agreement defines "Change of Control" in §1.1 to include "the sale, transfer or other disposition, in one transaction or a series of related transactions, of a majority of the outstanding equity securities of the Company." The sale of 100% of the Shares to Douvend is squarely within that definition. Section 7.3(d) provides that the Kestrel Motion License "will terminate automatically upon a Change of Control of the Company, unless before the Change of Control Executive has consented in writing to the continuation of the Kestrel Motion License following that Change of Control."

The SPA contains no closing condition, covenant, or deliverable requiring Vasquez-Ilunga's written consent to the continuation of the Kestrel Motion License. The Retained Engine License (Exhibit A) and Transition Services Agreement (Exhibit B) are not yet attached, so we cannot confirm whether either addresses Kestrel Motion.

**Why it matters:** Kestrel Motion is the procedural animation toolkit used in the Company's games, including Ashen Vanguard (see Key Employee Agreement, Recital B; §7.3(a)). If the license terminates at Closing:

- The Company loses its right to use Kestrel Motion in Ashen Vanguard and any other titles, potentially impairing the game's development, maintenance, and monetization during the three-year Earnout Period.
- Seller's representation in SPA §4.8 ("The Company owns, or has a valid right to use, all Intellectual Property used in the conduct of the Business") would be false as of the Closing Date, giving Buyer a walk-right under §7.2(a) and a post-Closing indemnification claim under §9.2(a).
- The Key Employee Agreement is listed as a Material Contract in Schedule 4.10, so the automatic termination could also implicate the §4.10 representation that each Material Contract is "valid and in full force and effect."
- The earnout (up to $30 million) depends on Ashen Vanguard's performance; if Kestrel Motion is unavailable, that performance is at risk.

**Additional complication:** SPA §6.1 (Conduct of Business) prohibits the Company from amending any Material Contract without Buyer's prior written consent. If obtaining Vasquez-Ilunga's consent requires an amendment to the Key Employee Agreement (rather than a standalone consent letter), Buyer's consent would be needed before Closing.

**Recommendation:**
1. Obtain Vasquez-Ilunga's written consent to the continuation of the Kestrel Motion License post-Change of Control before or as a condition to Closing. Add this as a Seller closing deliverable under §3.2 or as a condition to Buyer's obligations under §7.2.
2. Confirm with the client whether Vasquez-Ilunga is amenable to providing such consent and what, if anything, he expects in exchange (e.g., continued employment terms, equity, or a buyout of the license).
3. If a standalone consent is not sufficient and an amendment is required, coordinate with Buyer under §6.1 to obtain consent to the amendment pre-Closing.
4. Ensure the Retained Engine License (Exhibit A) or a separate ancillary agreement expressly addresses Kestrel Motion to avoid any ambiguity.

**Fact needed from client:** Has Vasquez-Ilunga been approached about the Change of Control consent? What are his expectations? Is his continued employment post-Closing assured (the email thread suggests yes, but there is no contractual commitment in the SPA)?

## Issue 3 – Buyer's Indemnification Cap Does Not Exclude Earnout Obligations or Engine License Breaches, Contrary to the LOI

**Where:** SPA §9.5(b) (Buyer's Cap); SPA §1.1 (definition of "Excluded Buyer Obligations"); LOI ¶6.

**What the LOI provided:** Buyer's liability cap would be the same as Seller's ($8.4 million), "except that the following will be excluded from Buyer's liability cap: fraud, Earnout payment obligations, and breaches of the Retained Engine License."

**What the SPA provides:** "Excluded Buyer Obligations" is defined in §1.1 as only "any Losses arising from fraud by Buyer." Section 9.5(b) caps Buyer's aggregate liability under §9.3 at the Cap ($8.4 million), except for Excluded Buyer Obligations (i.e., fraud only). Earnout payment obligations and breaches of the Retained Engine License (or the related restrictions in §6.7) are NOT excluded from the cap.

**Why it matters:**
- **Earnout:** The maximum earnout is $30 million. If Buyer fails to pay an earnout installment (or underpays via manipulation of Net Revenue), Seller's recovery under the indemnification provision is capped at $8.4 million. The LOI specifically carved earnout obligations out of the cap to ensure full recovery.
- **Cinderloom Engine:** The restrictions in §6.7 (no reverse engineering, no competitive use, no sublicensing) protect Seller's most valuable retained asset. If Buyer breaches §6.7 and the resulting harm exceeds $8.4 million, Seller's recovery is capped. The LOI excluded "breaches of the Retained Engine License" from the cap for this reason.

**Recommendation:** Amend the definition of "Excluded Buyer Obligations" in §1.1 to include (a) fraud, (b) Buyer's obligations under Section 2.5 (Earnout), and (c) Buyer's obligations under Sections 6.6 and 6.7 (Retained Engine License and restrictions on use of Cinderloom). This restores the LOI bargain.

## Issue 4 – Seller's IP Indemnification Is Uncapped, Perpetual, and Broader Than the LOI Contemplated

**Where:** SPA §9.2(d); SPA §1.1 (definition of "Company IP"); LOI ¶5.

**What the SPA provides:** Seller indemnifies Buyer against "any third-party claim alleging that any Company IP, or the development, publishing or exploitation of any game title of the Company, infringes, misappropriates or otherwise violates any Intellectual Property of any Person, whether known or unknown, and whether arising before or after the Closing." This obligation applies "without regard to the Cap, the Basket or the survival periods in Section 9.1."

**Why it matters:**
- **Uncapped and perpetual:** Unlike all other Seller indemnification obligations (which are capped at $8.4 million and survive 18 months), the IP indemnity has no monetary limit and no time limit. A single successful infringement claim could expose Seller to unlimited liability indefinitely.
- **"Company IP" is overbroad:** The definition in §1.1 includes "all Intellectual Property owned or purported to be owned by, or **licensed to**, the Company." This would encompass the Kestrel Motion toolkit (licensed from Vasquez-Ilunga) and the Cinderloom engine (licensed from Seller under the Retained Engine License). If a third party asserts that the use of Cinderloom in Ashen Vanguard infringes its rights, Seller would indemnify Buyer under §9.2(d) without cap or time limit—even though the claim would really be against the engine Seller retained and licensed.
- **Post-Closing claims:** The indemnity covers claims "arising … after the Closing." Seller has no control over the Company's post-Closing development activities, yet it bears unlimited liability for IP claims arising from them.
- **LOI deviation:** LOI ¶5 contemplated "customary" indemnification with a cap and basket. It did not provide for an uncapped, perpetual IP indemnity.

**Recommendation:**
1. Subject the IP indemnity to the Cap (or at minimum a separate, higher cap tied to the Base Purchase Price).
2. Limit survival to a reasonable period (e.g., the applicable statute of limitations, consistent with the treatment of Fundamental Representations in §9.1).
3. Narrow the scope to IP owned by the Company (delete "licensed to" from the relevant portion, or at minimum exclude the Cinderloom engine and Kestrel Motion from the indemnity).
4. Add a carve-out so that claims arising from the use of the Cinderloom engine are governed by the Retained Engine License (where Seller's obligations as licensor would be defined) rather than by this uncapped SPA indemnity.

## Issue 5 – Buyer May Assign the SPA to Any Designee Without Seller's Consent and Be Fully Released

**Where:** SPA §11.4 (Assignment).

**What the SPA provides:** "Buyer may, without Seller's consent, assign this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, in whole or in part, to any Buyer Designee. Upon any such assignment and the Buyer Designee's written assumption of the assigned obligations, Buyer will be relieved of its obligations under this Agreement and the applicable Ancillary Agreements."

**Why it matters:**
- Seller has no approval right over who the ultimate buyer is. Buyer could assign to a financially weaker entity, a competitor of Bikas Pocket, or an entity with adverse IP positions.
- Once assigned, Buyer is fully released. If the designee later fails to pay the earnout or breaches the Cinderloom restrictions, Seller's counterparty is the designee, not Buyer.
- The LOI did not address assignment. This is a buyer-favorable addition.
- Combined with Issue 3 (earnout not excluded from Buyer's cap), if the designee's creditworthiness is inferior, Seller's practical recovery on a $30 million earnout is further diminished.

**Recommendation:** Either (a) require Seller's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) to any assignment, or (b) provide that Buyer remains jointly and severally liable with any designee for all obligations under the SPA (particularly the earnout and the Cinderloom restrictions), or (c) at minimum require the designee to be a "sophisticated" entity with net worth or creditworthiness no less than Buyer's at the time of assignment.

## Issue 6 – Earnout Acceleration Does Not Cover a Stock Sale of the Company

**Where:** SPA §2.5(f) (Acceleration).

**What the SPA provides:** Unpaid earnout accelerates only if "Buyer sells all or substantially all of the assets of the Company relating to Ashen Vanguard."

**Why it matters:** If Buyer sells the *stock* of the Company (rather than its assets) to a third party during the Earnout Period, the acceleration trigger is not met. The new stockholder would step into the Company's shoes, but the earnout obligation sits with Buyer (or its assignee under §11.4) under the SPA. If Buyer has already assigned and been released (see Issue 5), or if the new owner has different incentives regarding Ashen Vanguard, Seller's $30 million earnout is at risk with no acceleration remedy.

**Recommendation:** Expand §2.5(f) to accelerate the earnout upon any Change of Control of the Company (using the same definition as in the Key Employee Agreement, or a tailored definition), or upon any sale of all or substantially all of the Company's equity securities, in addition to the existing asset-sale trigger.

## Issue 12 – "Company IP" Definition Sweeps in Licensed Third-Party IP

**Where:** SPA §1.1 (definition of "Company IP"); SPA §9.2(d) (IP indemnity); Key Employee Agreement §7.3 (Kestrel Motion License).

**What the SPA provides:** "Company IP" means "all Intellectual Property owned or purported to be owned by, or **licensed to**, the Company, including all Intellectual Property embodied in Ashen Vanguard and the Company's other game titles."

**Why it matters:** As noted in Issue 4, this definition captures the Kestrel Motion toolkit (licensed from Vasquez-Ilunga) and the Cinderloom engine (licensed from Seller). When combined with the uncapped, perpetual IP indemnity in §9.2(d), Seller could be on the hook for third-party claims relating to IP it does not own and did not develop. For example, if Vasquez-Ilunga's Kestrel Motion were alleged to infringe a third party's patent, Seller would indemnify Buyer under §9.2(d) without cap or time limit, even though the IP is Vasquez-Ilunga's and the warranty of non-infringement is his (Key Employee Agreement §7.3(f)).

**Recommendation:** In conjunction with the fix to Issue 4, narrow the "Company IP" definition for purposes of §9.2(d) to IP *owned* by the Company (or for which Seller has specifically warranted title), and exclude IP licensed from third parties (including Kestrel Motion) and IP licensed *by* Seller (Cinderloom).
