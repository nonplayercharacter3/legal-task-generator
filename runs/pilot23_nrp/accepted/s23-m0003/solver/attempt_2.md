## Issue 1: Non-Competition Covenant Is Dramatically Broader Than the LOI and Would Prohibit Seller's Retained Business

**Where:** SPA §6.4; compare LOI ¶8; KEE §1.1 (definition of "Competing Business").

**Analysis:**

The LOI ¶8 agreed that Seller's non-compete would be limited to "the development or publishing of tactical role-playing games for console and PC only," with express carve-outs for (a) the Bikas Pocket mobile casual game publishing business and (b) Seller's "continued ownership, development and licensing of the Cinderloom engine."

The SPA §6.4 instead provides that for three years post-Closing, Seller shall not "directly or indirectly, own, manage, operate or engage in any Competing Business anywhere in the world." It then defines "Competing Business" by importing the definition from KEE §1.1: "the development, publishing or distribution of any video game in any genre, on any platform."

This creates three compounding problems:

- **Genre and platform scope:** The restriction covers *any* video game in *any* genre on *any* platform, not just tactical role-playing games on console and PC.
- **No Bikas Pocket carve-out:** Bikas Pocket is a mobile casual game publishing business (SPA Recital D). Under the SPA's definition, publishing mobile casual games is a "Competing Business." The SPA as drafted would prohibit Bikas from operating the very business it is retaining as part of the deal structure.
- **No Cinderloom carve-out:** The SPA Recital C states that Seller retains the Cinderloom engine and licenses it to the Company. Developing, improving, or licensing the Cinderloom engine to third parties for use in video games would likely constitute "development" or "publishing" of a video game under the KEE definition. The SPA contains no exception for this.
- **Geographic scope:** "Anywhere in the world" replaces the LOI's implicit platform limitation.
- **Activity scope:** "Own, manage, operate or engage in" is broader than the LOI's "engage in the development or publishing of."

**Why it matters:** This is the single most damaging deviation in the draft. As written, it would prevent Bikas from operating its retained mobile business and from continuing to develop and license its own game engine—both of which are foundational to the deal structure agreed in the LOI. Accepting this provision would effectively force Bikas to abandon or restructure its retained operations.

**Recommendation:** Restore the LOI formulation. Propose that §6.4 be limited to "the development or publishing of tactical role-playing games for console and PC," with express carve-outs for (a) the Seller Retained Business (Bikas Pocket) and (b) Seller's ownership, development, and licensing of the Cinderloom engine. Remove the cross-reference to KEE §1.1. Limit geography to the markets in which the Company operates (or at minimum, North America and Europe).

## Issue 2: Buyer's Indemnification Cap Does Not Exclude Earnout Obligations or Retained Engine License Breaches

**Where:** SPA §9.5(b) and §1.1 (definition of "Excluded Buyer Obligations"); compare LOI ¶6.

**Analysis:**

LOI ¶6 states: "Buyer's aggregate indemnification liability will be subject to the same cap that applies to Seller's general indemnification obligations, **except that the following will be excluded from Buyer's liability cap: fraud, Earnout payment obligations, and breaches of the Retained Engine License**."

The SPA §9.5(b) provides: "Buyer's aggregate liability under Section 9.3 shall not exceed the Cap [$8,400,000], except for Losses arising out of Excluded Buyer Obligations."

"Excluded Buyer Obligations" is defined in SPA §1.1 as "any Losses arising from fraud by Buyer." Only fraud is excluded. Earnout payment obligations and Retained Engine License breaches are **not** excluded from the cap.

Additionally, SPA §9.5(a) subjects Buyer's indemnification to the same $840,000 Basket as Seller's, which the LOI did not provide for.

**Why it matters:** The Maximum Earnout Amount is $30,000,000. If Buyer fails to pay a $10,000,000 Earnout Payment, Seller's recovery under the SPA as drafted would be limited to $8,400,000 minus the $840,000 Basket = $7,560,000. Seller would be out of pocket by at least $2.44M on a single missed payment. Similarly, if Buyer breaches the Cinderloom engine restrictions (SPA §6.7), causing Seller to lose the value of its retained engine through misuse or disclosure, Seller's recovery is capped at $7.56M regardless of actual damages.

**Recommendation:** Amend §1.1 to define "Excluded Buyer Obligations" to include (a) fraud, (b) Buyer's obligations under Section 2.5 (Earnout), and (c) Buyer's obligations under Sections 6.6 and 6.7 (Retained Engine License and restrictions on Cinderloom). Delete the Basket in §9.5(a) or make it inapplicable to Excluded Buyer Obligations.

## Issue 3: Kestrel Motion License Terminates Automatically on Change of Control Unless Vasquez-Ilunga Consents

**Where:** KEE §7.3(d) and §1.1 (definition of "Change of Control"); SPA §4.8; SPA §1.1 (definition of "Company IP").

**Analysis:**

KEE §7.3(d) provides: "The Kestrel Motion License will terminate automatically upon a Change of Control of the Company, **unless before the Change of Control Executive has consented in writing** to the continuation of the Kestrel Motion License following that Change of Control."

"Change of Control" (KEE §1.1) includes "the sale, transfer or other disposition, in one transaction or a series of related transactions, of a majority of the outstanding equity securities of the Company." The SPA is a sale of 100% of the Company's shares—unambiguously a Change of Control.

KEE §7.3(e) provides that on termination, "the Company shall cease all use and distribution of Kestrel Motion, including in any Company Game, and shall return or destroy all copies of Kestrel Motion in its possession."

Kestrel Motion is embedded in Ashen Vanguard (KEE §7.3(a) licenses it for use in "Company Games," defined as Ashen Vanguard and all other games). If the license terminates at Closing:

- The Company must cease all use of Kestrel Motion in Ashen Vanguard.
- This could impair the game's ability to receive updates, patches, ports, or new content.
- The SPA's IP representation (§4.8) states the Company "owns, or has a valid right to use, all Intellectual Property used in the conduct of the Business." If Kestrel Motion is used in Ashen Vanguard and the license terminates, this representation is false at Closing.
- The earnout (SPA §2.5) depends on Ashen Vanguard's Net Revenue over three years. Impairment of the game could reduce revenue and the earnout.

**The SPA does not mention Kestrel Motion at all.** It is not listed in the Disclosure Schedule excerpts, not addressed in the Retained Engine License (which covers only Cinderloom), and not identified as a closing condition or deliverable.

**Why it matters:** This is a potential deal-breaker if not addressed. Without Vasquez-Ilunga's written consent before Closing, the Company loses its right to use Kestrel Motion in Ashen Vanguard, the IP representation is breached at Closing, and the earnout is at risk.

**Recommendation:**
- **Immediately** confirm with the client whether Vasquez-Ilunga has been approached about providing written consent under KEE §7.3(d). If not, this should be a priority action item before signing.
- Add Kestrel Motion to the Disclosure Schedule (Schedule 4.8 or a new schedule) with a specific disclosure regarding the Change of Control termination provision.
- Consider adding a condition to Closing (in Article 7) that Vasquez-Ilunga's written consent to continuation of the Kestrel Motion License has been delivered.
- Alternatively, negotiate an amendment to the KEE (with Vasquez-Ilunga's agreement) that removes or modifies the automatic termination on Change of Control, or that deems consent given as part of the transaction.
- Ensure the SPA's IP representation (§4.8) is qualified in the Disclosure Schedule to the extent Kestrel Motion is subject to this termination right.

**Client question:** Has Vasquez-Ilunga been informed of the Change of Control termination provision? Is he amenable to providing written consent? Does the client have a relationship with him that facilitates this?

## Issue 4: Uncapped, Unlimited-Survival IP Indemnification

**Where:** SPA §9.2(d); compare LOI ¶5.

**Analysis:**

SPA §9.2(d) requires Seller to indemnify Buyer against "any third-party claim alleging that any Company IP, or the development, publishing or exploitation of any game title of the Company, infringes, misappropriates or otherwise violates any Intellectual Property of any Person, whether known or unknown, and whether arising before or after the Closing." Critically, the section states: "Seller's obligations under this Section 9.2(d) apply **without regard to the Cap, the Basket or the survival periods in Section 9.1**."

LOI ¶5 addressed only "general representations and warranties" (18-month survival, 10% cap, 1% basket) and "fundamental representations" (capped at Base Purchase Price). It did not contemplate a separate, uncapped, perpetual IP indemnity.

**Why it matters:** This creates unlimited, perpetual financial exposure for Bikas. A single IP infringement claim against Ashen Vanguard (or any other Company game) could result in damages far exceeding the $8.4M Cap, and the claim could arise years after Closing. The "whether arising before or after the Closing" language means even post-Closing development activities by Buyer that give rise to IP claims would be indemnified by Seller.

**Recommendation:**
- Cap the IP indemnity at the Base Purchase Price ($84M) or at least at a multiple of the general Cap (e.g., 2x or 3x = $16.8M–$25.2M).
- Limit survival to a defined period (e.g., the applicable statute of limitations for IP claims, or 3–5 years post-Closing).
- Delete "whether arising before or after the Closing" or limit to claims arising from IP owned or used by the Company as of the Closing Date.
- Carve out claims arising from Buyer's post-Closing modifications, combinations with other technology, or use outside the scope of the Business.
- If the client is comfortable with an uncapped IP indemnity (which is not unusual in gaming M&A given the IP-heavy nature of the business), at minimum negotiate the survival period and the "before or after" language.

**Client question:** Does the client have any known or potential IP exposure in Ashen Vanguard or other Company titles? Has due diligence surfaced any IP concerns? This will inform how aggressively we should negotiate this point.

## Issue 5: Earnout Protections Are Insufficient

**Where:** SPA §2.5(e) and (f); compare LOI ¶3.

**Analysis:**

Several gaps in the earnout protection:

**(a) No obligation to maintain the development team or continue development.** SPA §2.5(e) requires Buyer to (i) act in good faith, (ii) keep Ashen Vanguard commercially available on existing platforms, and (iii) maintain books and records. It does **not** require Buyer to continue developing Ashen Vanguard, maintain the studio team, or retain Vasquez-Ilunga. Buyer could let the studio wind down, stop all development, and simply keep the existing game on the stores. Revenue would likely decline over time, reducing the earnout, but this would not technically violate §2.5(e) unless the "primary purpose" was to avoid the earnout—a high bar to prove.

**(b) Acceleration only for asset sales, not stock sales.** SPA §2.5(f) accelerates the unpaid earnout if "Buyer sells all or substantially all of the assets of the Company relating to Ashen Vanguard." If Buyer sells the **stock** of the Company (i.e., sells Emberline Forge Studios to a third party), the acceleration is not triggered. The earnout obligation remains with Buyer under the SPA, but the asset generating the revenue is now owned by someone else. Combined with §11.4 (see Issue 6), this creates collection risk.

**(c) "Net Revenue" definition is ambiguous.** SPA §2.5(b) defines Net Revenue as revenue "determined in accordance with GAAP consistently applied." Consistently applied to what? The Company's historical practice? If Buyer changes its revenue recognition methodology post-Closing (e.g., different treatment of in-game monetization, DLC, or seasonal passes), Net Revenue could be affected. The LOI left the "calculation methodology" to the definitive agreement, so this is a new term that needs scrutiny.

**Why it matters:** The earnout represents up to $30M (36% of total consideration). These gaps create meaningful risk that the earnout will be lower than expected or difficult to collect.

**Recommendation:**
- Add to §2.5(e): Buyer shall use commercially reasonable efforts to continue active development and support of Ashen Vanguard during the Earnout Period, and shall not materially reduce the personnel dedicated to Ashen Vanguard below the level in place at Closing without Seller's consent.
- Expand §2.5(f) to cover a sale of the Company's stock (or a Change of Control of the Company) in addition to an asset sale.
- Clarify "consistently applied" to mean "consistently with the Company's historical revenue recognition practices as reflected in the financial statements delivered under Section 4.5," or specify the particular GAAP guidance to be applied.
- Consider adding a requirement that Buyer provide Seller with quarterly (not just annual) revenue reporting during the Earnout Period for transparency.

## Issue 6: Buyer May Assign the SPA (Including Earnout Obligations) Without Seller's Consent

**Where:** SPA §11.4.

**Analysis:**

SPA §11.4 provides: "Buyer may, without Seller's consent, assign this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, in whole or in part, to any Buyer Designee. Upon any such assignment and the Buyer Designee's written assumption of the assigned obligations, Buyer will be relieved of its obligations under this Agreement and the applicable Ancillary Agreements."

There is no requirement that the Buyer Designee have comparable financial capacity, no right of Seller to approve or consent, and no continued liability of Buyer as a guarantor. The "Sufficiency of Funds" representation in §5.3 applies to Buyer as originally named, not to a designee.

**Why it matters:** If Buyer assigns the SPA to a shell entity or a financially weaker affiliate and then walks away, Seller's earnout and indemnification claims would be against an entity with no assets. The $30M earnout and any indemnification recovery become uncollectible.

**Recommendation:**
- Require Seller's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment by Buyer.
- Alternatively, require that any Buyer Designee (a) have net worth or creditworthiness no less than Buyer's at the time of assignment, (b) provide a financial statement or credit support, and (c) that Buyer remain jointly and severally liable (or provide a parent guarantee) for at least the Earnout Period.
- At minimum, delete the language that "Buyer will be relieved of its obligations" upon assignment.

## Issue 7: No Protection for Seller Regarding Vasquez-Ilunga's Post-Closing Employment

**Where:** SPA generally; KEE §5.3 (Good Reason); LOI ¶7; email thread, Message 2, item 4.

**Analysis:**

The LOI ¶7 states that Buyer "considers the continued involvement of the Company's studio head, Teodor Vasquez-Ilunga, important to the Transaction." The email thread confirms that Douvend will request resignations from all officers "other than the Studio Head," implying Vasquez-Ilunga remains in place.

However, the SPA contains:
- No covenant requiring Buyer to retain Vasquez-Ilunga.
- No condition to Closing that he remains employed.
- No protection against his resignation for Good Reason post-Closing.

Under KEE §5.3, "Good Reason" includes "a material reduction in his duties, authority or responsibilities as Studio Head." If Buyer restructures the studio post-Closing and reduces his role, he can resign for Good Reason and collect 12 months' salary plus pro rata bonus plus COBRA (KEE §5.5). His departure would likely impair Ashen Vanguard's development and the earnout.

**Why it matters:** The earnout (up to $30M) depends on Ashen Vanguard's performance, which depends heavily on the studio team and its leader. The loss of Vasquez-Ilunga would be a material negative for the earnout, yet Seller has no contractual protection against it.

**Recommendation:**
- Add a covenant in the SPA requiring Buyer to use commercially reasonable efforts to retain Vasquez-Ilunga in his role as Studio Head for a specified period (e.g., the first two Earnout Years).
- Alternatively, add a condition to Closing that Vasquez-Ilunga has executed a reaffirmation or amendment of the KEE acknowledging the Change of Control and confirming his continued employment.
- Coordinate with the KEE consent required under Issue 3—ideally, the same written consent from Vasquez-Ilunga should address both the Kestrel Motion License continuation and his post-Closing role.

**Client question:** What is the client's understanding of Vasquez-Ilunga's attitude toward the transaction? Is he supportive? Does he have any concerns about his post-Closing role or compensation that might motivate a Good Reason resignation?

## Issue 8: SPA References KEE Definition Without Addressing the KEE's Change of Control Consequences

**Where:** SPA §6.4 (importing KEE §1.1 definition); KEE §7.3(d).

**Analysis:**

The SPA imports the KEE's definition of "Competing Business" into Seller's non-compete (§6.4) but does not address the fact that the KEE itself contains a Change of Control provision that will be triggered by the SPA transaction. This is internally inconsistent: the SPA relies on the KEE for a definition while the transaction contemplated by the SPA will alter the KEE's operation (terminating the Kestrel Motion License).

This is related to Issue 3 but is also a drafting issue. The SPA should either (a) not import definitions from the KEE, or (b) explicitly address the KEE's Change of Control consequences as part of the transaction.

**Recommendation:** As part of the fix for Issue 1, remove the cross-reference to KEE §1.1. As part of the fix for Issue 3, add the KEE consent as a closing deliverable or condition.

## Issue 11: Buyer's Indemnification for Post-Closing Operations Is Subject to the Cap

**Where:** SPA §9.3(c) and §9.5(b).

**Analysis:**

SPA §9.3(c) provides that Buyer indemnifies Seller for "the operation of the Company after the Closing." This is a broad protection for Seller (e.g., if Buyer's post-Closing operations create liabilities that affect Seller, such as environmental, employment, or regulatory matters). However, under §9.5(b), this indemnification is subject to the $8.4M Cap (minus the $840K Basket). For a company with ongoing operations, $7.56M of net coverage may be insufficient.

**Why it matters:** Less critical than Issues 1–4, but the cap on this indemnity could leave Seller exposed if Buyer's post-Closing operations generate significant liabilities that somehow affect Seller (e.g., through the Transition Services Agreement or the Retained Engine License).

**Recommendation:** Consider whether the Cap should not apply to §9.3(c), or whether a higher cap (e.g., the Base Purchase Price) is appropriate for post-Closing operation claims. At minimum, ensure the Transition Services Agreement and Retained Engine License have their own indemnification provisions that are not subject to the SPA cap.

## Issue 12: Earnout Thresholds and Structure — Confirm Client Expectations

**Where:** SPA §2.5(b); LOI ¶3.

**Analysis:**

The LOI left "revenue thresholds, calculation methodology, reporting, audit rights and dispute resolution to be set out in the Definitive Agreement." The SPA sets:
- Three annual measurement periods (Earnout Years).
- $40M Net Revenue threshold for full $10M payment.
- $30M Net Revenue floor for partial payment (sliding scale between $30M and $40M).
- Maximum aggregate earnout of $30M.

These terms are not unreasonable on their face, but we should confirm they align with the client's expectations and the financial projections that supported the deal valuation.

**Client question:** Do the $30M/$40M Net Revenue thresholds align with the client's financial model and expectations for Ashen Vanguard? Is the client comfortable with the all-or-nothing structure (no payment below $30M) versus a sliding scale that starts at a lower threshold?

## Issue 13: Governing Law and Jurisdiction — Minor Note

**Where:** SPA §11.6–11.7 (New York law, Manhattan jurisdiction); KEE §9.8 (Delaware law, Delaware jurisdiction).

**Analysis:**

The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan. The KEE is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware. This means disputes involving the KEE (e.g., the Kestrel Motion License termination, Vasquez-Ilunga's employment) will be in Delaware, while disputes under the SPA will be in New York. If a single factual scenario implicates both agreements (e.g., Vasquez-Ilunga's departure affecting both the KEE and the SPA's earnout), there could be forum complications.

**Why it matters:** Low severity, but worth being aware of for litigation strategy if disputes arise.

**Recommendation:** No change needed, but flag for the litigation team if issues arise.

## Issue 14: Precedence Clause Confirms SPA Controls Over LOI

**Where:** SPA §11.9.

**Analysis:**

"The SPA prevails over any Ancillary Agreement and the Letter of Intent." This is standard, but it means that once the SPA is signed, all the LOI terms that the SPA deviates from (Issues 1, 2, 4, etc.) are superseded. There is no fallback to the LOI. This underscores the importance of negotiating the SPA terms before signing.

**Recommendation:** No change to the clause itself, but it reinforces that every deviation from the LOI must be addressed in the SPA markup.
