## Issue 1: Assignment to a Direct Competitor Without Consent

**Where:** EDA § 17.2; Stepisk Notice Letter §§ 1–3.

**Explanation.** Section 17.2 permits Supplier to assign the Agreement "in whole or in part, to any person … without the consent of Distributor, upon written notice to Distributor." The October 19 notice complies with the delivery requirements of § 19.1 (overnight courier and email to notices@taisundevents.com). The assignment is effective December 1, 2026.

The assignee, Orlenne Event Group LLC, is described in the notice (¶ 2.2) as operating "an event furniture rental business" that "serves venues, caterers, planners and corporate customers in Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware" — i.e., the identical Territory and customer base defined in EDA § 3.1 and Recital B. After the Assignment Date, Orlenne steps into Stepisk's shoes as "Supplier" and becomes Taisund's sole contractual counterparty for product supply, refurbishment, warehousing, and logistics, while simultaneously remaining a competitor for the same end-customers.

Stepisk itself does not exit the picture entirely: it continues to manufacture and will supply products to Orlenne for fulfillment (Notice Letter ¶ 3.3). But Taisund loses its direct contractual relationship with the manufacturer. All of Taisund's rights — pricing protections, warranty, indemnification, service levels, termination triggers — run against Orlenne, a rental company, not a furniture manufacturer.

**Why it matters.** This is the single most consequential development. Taisund's entire business model under the Agreement depends on exclusive access to Stepisk products in the Territory. That access is now controlled by a competitor with every incentive to prioritize its own rental fleet over Taisund's supply.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- Taisund cannot prevent the assignment. It should, however, respond in writing before December 1 to (a) confirm receipt, (b) request a copy of Orlenne's insurance certificates under EDA § 11.3, and (c) reserve all rights.
- **[DECISION NEEDED]** Taisund should decide whether to open a negotiation with Orlenne before the Assignment Date to seek contractual protections: e.g., a firewall provision barring Orlenne from using Taisund's Confidential Information (particularly customer delivery schedules and locations) for its own rental solicitation; a commitment that Orlenne will maintain the Supplier Warehouse and delivery operations at current service levels for a transition period; or a most-favored-customer guarantee that Orlenne will not supply Stepisk products to itself or any other rental operator in the Territory at a price or on terms more favorable than Taisund's.
- If Orlenne will not negotiate, Taisund should begin contingency planning for alternative supply (see Issue 9).

## Issue 2: Transfer of Taisund's Confidential Information to a Competitor

**Where:** EDA § 14.1; Notice Letter § 4.1; SLS § 7.2.

**Explanation.** Section 14.1 defines "Confidential Information" to include "pricing, Forecasts, Purchase Orders, Customer identities, Customer delivery schedules and locations, and business plans." The notice letter (¶ 4.1) confirms that Stepisk will deliver to Orlenne, on or before December 1:

- (b) Distributor's Forecasts and open Purchase Orders;
- (d) Depot schedules, delivery notes and **Customer delivery schedules and locations**;
- (e) Service Level performance reports and supporting records.

Orlenne will be bound by § 14 as successor (Notice Letter ¶ 4.2), and the confidentiality obligation runs for five years post-termination (§ 14.4). But the practical protection is materially weakened: Orlenne is a rental operator in the same Territory. With Taisund's customer delivery schedules and locations, Orlenne knows exactly which events Taisund is servicing, when, and where — intelligence it can use to solicit those same customers for Orlenne's own (non-Stepisk) rental inventory.

**Why it matters.** Customer delivery schedules and locations are Taisund's most operationally sensitive data. In the rental business, knowing that a particular venue is hosting a 500-guest event on a specific date allows a competitor to call that venue the week before and offer a competing package.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[DECISION NEEDED]** Taisund should consider whether to request, as a condition of its acquiescence in the assignment, that Orlenne agree to a narrower data-transfer protocol: e.g., that Customer delivery schedules and locations be transferred in aggregated or redacted form, or that Orlenne's access to that data be limited to personnel who do not work in Orlenne's rental sales function.
- Absent a negotiated firewall, Taisund should immediately review its own internal data-security practices to ensure that no Taisund-side systems or personnel inadvertently expose additional customer data beyond what is contractually required to be shared.
- Taisund should calendar the five-year confidentiality tail (§ 14.4) and monitor Orlenne's rental activity in the Territory for any pattern suggesting use of Taisund's customer intelligence. A breach of § 14 is excluded from the liability cap (§ 12.2) and from the consequential damages waiver (§ 12.3), so Taisund has a meaningful remedy if misuse occurs — but proving causation will be difficult.

## Issue 3: Asymmetric Assignment Rights Lock Taisund In

**Where:** EDA §§ 17.1, 17.2.

**Explanation.** Section 17.1 prohibits Distributor from assigning "without the prior written consent of Supplier." Section 17.2 allows Supplier to assign to "any person" without consent. The asymmetry is absolute: Taisund cannot transfer its distribution rights to a successor (e.g., in a sale of Taisund's business) without Orlenne's approval, while Orlenne could assign the Agreement to yet another party tomorrow with mere notice.

**Why it matters.** If Taisund wishes to sell or restructure, it needs Orlenne's consent — and Orlenne is a competitor with no incentive to facilitate Taisund's exit to a third party who might become a stronger competitor. This entrenches Taisund in a relationship with a competitor on unfavorable structural terms.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- In any negotiation with Orlenne (see Issue 1), Taisund should seek to amend § 17.1 to permit assignment to an Affiliate or in connection with a sale of all or substantially all of Taisund's assets, subject to the assignee assuming the Agreement's obligations.
- At minimum, Taisund should be aware that this clause constrains its own M&A flexibility for the duration of the Term (through at least March 2029, potentially longer with auto-renewal under § 4.2).

## Issue 4: Overbroad Distributor Indemnity Covers Product Defects

**Where:** EDA §§ 10.1, 10.2, 8.1, 8.3, 12.2.

**Explanation.** Section 10.1 requires Taisund to indemnify Supplier against "any and all claims … arising out of or relating to the distribution, rental, setup or use of the Products by Distributor, any Customer or any other person, **regardless of whether caused by a defect in the Products**." The italicized language is critical: even if a Stepisk chair has a manufacturing defect that causes a customer's injury, Taisund must defend and indemnify the Supplier.

Section 10.2 (Supplier's indemnity to Distributor) covers only IP infringement. There is no Supplier indemnity for product-defect personal injury or property damage claims.

Taisund's sole recourse for a defective product is the warranty in § 8.3: repair, replace, or refund the price. This does not cover third-party bodily injury, medical costs, or lost business from a product recall.

Section 12.2 excludes § 10 obligations from the liability cap, meaning Taisund's indemnity exposure is uncapped.

**Why it matters.** In the event-furniture rental business, the primary product risk is personal injury (a table collapses, a chair breaks under load). Taisund, as the party that places the product in the customer's hands, will be the first defendant. Under § 10.1, Taisund must then indemnify the Supplier (now Orlenne) even if the injury was caused by a manufacturing defect. Taisund's insurance (EDA § 11.2: $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate CGL) may be insufficient for a serious injury claim, and the excess is Taisund's.

The risk is amplified by the assignment: Orlenne, as a competitor, has every incentive to assert the indemnity broadly and every incentive to resist Taisund's warranty claims (since Orlenne is not the manufacturer and may pass the cost to Stepisk or absorb it).

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[DECISION NEEDED]** Taisund should review its CGL and products liability insurance coverage with its broker to confirm that (a) the policy responds to claims arising from defective products that Taisund rents, and (b) the limits are adequate given the uncapped indemnity in § 10.1. If the current $1M/$2M limits are the contractual minimum (§ 11.2), Taisund should consider purchasing additional coverage.
- In any renegotiation with Orlenne, Taisund should seek to narrow § 10.1 to exclude claims "to the extent caused by a defect in design, materials or workmanship of the Products" — i.e., carve out manufacturer-defect claims from Taisund's indemnity and route them through the warranty or a new Supplier defect-indemnity.
- As a practical matter, Taisund should ensure its rental agreements with Customers include robust hold-harmless and insurance requirements so that end-customers bear their own share of risk.

## Issue 5: Split Governing Law and Forum (Delaware vs. New York)

**Where:** EDA §§ 18.1–18.2; SOW § 9.1; EDA § 2.3.

**Explanation.** The EDA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Wilmington, Delaware (§§ 18.1–18.2). The SOW is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County (§ 9.1). The Service Level Schedule contains no independent governing-law clause.

Under the order of precedence in EDA § 2.3, the SOW prevails over the SLS, which prevails over the EDA. The SOW's New York clause applies to "any claim, controversy or dispute arising out of or relating to this SOW or the Services." Because the SLS governs the measurement and crediting of service levels for "the Services" described in the SOW, there is a strong argument that SLS disputes are also governed by New York law and must be brought in New York.

**Why it matters.** Taisund (a Pennsylvania LLC) now faces a bifurcated dispute-resolution landscape:

- Claims about pricing, exclusivity, termination, indemnification, or the distribution relationship → Delaware law, Wilmington forum.
- Claims about delivery performance, refurbishment quality, warehousing, service credits, or SOW fees → New York law, New York County forum.

A single operational failure (e.g., a late delivery that causes Taisund to miss a customer event) could implicate both the SLS (service credit) and the EDA (potential termination under § 15.3 or breach under § 15.1). The parties could end up litigating related but distinct claims in two different forums under two different bodies of law.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- In any renegotiation, seek to conform the SOW's governing law and forum to the EDA (Delaware / Wilmington) to create a single dispute-resolution track.
- If no renegotiation occurs, Taisund should ensure its internal escalation protocol (§ 18.3: senior executive meeting within 15 business days) is used for all disputes before filing in either forum, to preserve the relationship and avoid parallel proceedings.
- Taisund should brief its outside Delaware and New York counsel on the split so that any future claim is routed correctly from the outset.

## Issue 6: Order of Precedence Gives SOW (Now Controlled by Competitor) Supremacy

**Where:** EDA § 2.3.

**Explanation.** Section 2.3 provides: "Statement of Work, then Service Level Schedule, then Distribution Agreement." If the SOW and the EDA conflict, the SOW controls.

After the assignment, Orlenne controls the SOW (as the party that performed it and can propose amendments under SOW § 7.3). While any amendment requires Taisund's signature (SOW § 10.4), the existing SOW terms already override the EDA where they conflict. For example:

- The SOW's fee schedule (§ 6.1) and fee-adjustment right (§ 6.3) operate alongside the EDA's price-adjustment right (§ 6.1). If the two adjustment mechanisms produce inconsistent results for the same product, the SOW controls.
- The SOW's transition provision (§ 8.3) governs what happens to inventory on termination, potentially overriding the EDA's sell-off period (§ 16.3) to the extent of any conflict.

**Why it matters.** The document that a competitor now controls has the highest precedence. This structural feature, combined with the assignment, means Orlenne holds the interpretive high ground in any ambiguity between the SOW and the EDA.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- Taisund should conduct a gap analysis comparing the SOW and EDA for any actual or potential conflicts (pricing, delivery, termination, inventory ownership) and document them. If conflicts exist, they should be raised with Orlenne before the Assignment Date.
- In any renegotiation, seek to reverse the order of precedence so the EDA (the master agreement) controls over the SOW, or at minimum to add a provision that the EDA's protective terms (exclusivity, MFN, warranty, indemnification) cannot be overridden by the SOW.

## Issue 7: Orlenne's Capability to Meet Service Levels

**Where:** SLS §§ 2–6; SOW §§ 3–5; Notice Letter ¶¶ 3.3, 5.2.

**Explanation.** The Service Level Schedule imposes demanding operational standards: 48-hour replacement of damaged units (§ 2.2), 97% on-time delivery of POs (§ 3.1), 98% completion of scheduled runs (§ 3.2), 7–12 business day refurbishment turnaround (§ 4.2), 99.5% inventory accuracy (§ 5.2), and 95% buffer availability (§ 5.3). These were designed for a manufacturer with its own warehouse, refurbishment shop, and delivery fleet (Stepisk at Bridgeport, NJ).

Orlenne is a rental operator. The notice letter states that Stepisk will "continue to manufacture" and "supply [products] to Orlenne for fulfillment" (¶ 3.3), and that Stepisk will "coordinate with Orlenne on the handover of the Supplier Warehouse and delivery operations" (¶ 5.2). But the contractual obligations under the SOW and SLS now run to Orlenne. If Stepisk's handover to Orlenne is delayed, incomplete, or if the Stepisk–Orlenne supply relationship sours, Orlenne is contractually responsible for the service-level failures.

**Why it matters.** If Orlenne cannot meet the service levels, Taisund's remedies are:

- Service credits (capped at 20% of Monthly Services Fees per month, SLS § 8.2) — a modest financial offset.
- Termination under EDA § 15.3 if the same service level is missed in three or more months in a rolling 12-month period — but termination means losing the exclusive supply relationship and the Stepisk product line.
- Termination for material breach under EDA § 15.1 (30-day cure) — a high bar.

The practical risk is a period of degraded service during the transition (December 2026 – early 2027) that erodes Taisund's customer relationships without giving Taisund a clean exit trigger until the § 15.3 threshold is met.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[DECISION NEEDED]** Taisund should request from Orlenne, before December 1, a written transition plan confirming: (a) that the Bridgeport warehouse, refurbishment shop, and delivery fleet will continue operating without interruption; (b) the identity of the operations lead who will replace Tobias Renwald; and (c) the service-desk staffing arrangement for Priority 1 inquiries (SLS § 6.2).
- Taisund should begin tracking service-level performance from the Assignment Date with heightened scrutiny. If failures appear in December or January, Taisund should document them meticulously (per SLS § 7.2 record-keeping) to preserve the § 15.3 termination trigger.
- Taisund should confirm whether Orlenne has contracted with Stepisk for a back-to-back supply agreement and, if so, whether that agreement contains service-level commitments that flow down to Taisund's benefit.

## Issue 9: No Exit Right Triggered by the Assignment Itself

**Where:** EDA §§ 15.1, 15.2, 15.3, 17.2; § 4.2.

**Explanation.** The Agreement provides three termination triggers: material breach with 30-day cure (§ 15.1), insolvency (§ 15.2), and repeated service failures (§ 15.3). The assignment is expressly permitted by § 17.2 and is not a breach. Taisund has no right to terminate simply because the counterparty is now a competitor.

The Initial Term runs to March 1, 2029. Auto-renewal under § 4.2 requires 180 days' non-renewal notice (i.e., by approximately September 3, 2028). Taisund is locked in for at least two more years absent a termination trigger.

**Why it matters.** Taisund is contractually committed to a competitor through at least March 2029 (and potentially beyond via auto-renewal). Its leverage is limited to performance-based termination (§ 15.3) or negotiating a consensual exit.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[DECISION NEEDED]** Taisund should decide on its end-state: (a) accept the Orlenne relationship and negotiate protections (Issues 1–2), (b) plan to non-renew at the end of the Initial Term (notice by ~September 2028), or (c) seek a consensual early termination with Orlenne in exchange for a transition period and sell-off rights (§ 16.3).
- If Taisund pursues option (b), it should begin qualifying alternative product suppliers now so that it is not dependent on Stepisk/Orlenne at the point of exit. The force-majeure substitute-product right (§ 20.2) is limited to 60+ day supply disruptions and is not a general right to source elsewhere.
- Taisund should note that § 16.3 provides a 12-month sell-off period post-termination, which would allow it to continue renting existing Stepisk inventory. It should confirm that this right survives an assignment (it should, as it is a Distributor right under the EDA).

## Issue 10: Insurance Verification for Orlenne

**Where:** EDA §§ 11.1, 11.3; Notice Letter (silent on insurance).

**Explanation.** Section 11.1 requires Supplier to maintain CGL insurance with products and completed operations coverage at not less than $2M per occurrence / $5M aggregate, during the Term and for three years afterward. The notice letter does not address whether Orlenne currently carries (or will carry) insurance at these limits. Orlenne is a rental company, not a manufacturer; its existing insurance program may not include products/completed-operations coverage at the required levels.

**Why it matters.** If Orlenne is underinsured and a product-defect injury claim exceeds its policy limits, Taisund's indemnity obligation under § 10.1 (see Issue 4) becomes the primary funding source. Taisund's own insurance (§ 11.2: $1M/$2M CGL) may be exhausted.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[DECISION NEEDED / ACTION]** Taisund should demand, before December 1, a certificate of insurance from Orlenne confirming compliance with § 11.1. If Orlenne cannot evidence the required coverage, Taisund should (a) require Orlenne to obtain it as a condition of the assignment taking effect, or (b) treat the failure as a potential material breach under § 15.1 (though this is a strong argument only if the insurance obligation is deemed material — which it likely is given the products-liability context).
- Taisund should also confirm that its own insurance carrier has been notified of the change in counterparty and that the carrier does not require updated certificates or policy endorsements.

## Issue 12: Most-Favored-Pricing Clause – Ambiguity Post-Assignment

**Where:** EDA § 6.2; Notice Letter ¶¶ 2.2, 3.3.

**Explanation.** Section 6.2 provides: "Supplier shall not sell any Product to any other distributor or rental operator in the United States at a net price lower than the price then charged to Distributor for comparable volumes."

After the assignment, Orlenne is "Supplier." But Orlenne is also "a rental operator in the United States" (Notice Letter ¶ 2.2). If Orlenne purchases Stepisk products from Stepisk for its own rental fleet at a price lower than what Orlenne charges Taisund, is that a § 6.2 violation? The clause restricts the Supplier from selling to "any other … rental operator." Orlenne is not selling to itself; it is purchasing from Stepisk. The clause does not on its face address the Supplier's own procurement costs.

However, if Orlenne passes through a lower cost basis to Taisund's detriment (e.g., by raising Taisund's prices while using lower-cost inventory for its own rental fleet), the MFN clause may provide a basis for challenge — but the analysis is fact-intensive and the clause is ambiguous as applied.

**Why it matters.** Pricing is the economic foundation of Taisund's rental margins. If Orlenne can acquire Stepisk products cheaper than Taisund's contract price and use them in its own competing rental business, Taisund is at a structural cost disadvantage.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- **[FACTS NEEDED]** We would need to confirm: (a) the terms of Orlenne's supply arrangement with Stepisk (price per unit, volume commitments); and (b) whether Orlenne's rental fleet includes Stepisk products. If Orlenne is buying Stepisk products for its own fleet at a lower price than Taisund's Exhibit A price, Taisund has a strong § 6.2 argument.
- In any renegotiation, seek to clarify § 6.2 to state that the Supplier (Orlenne) shall not acquire Products from the manufacturer at a net cost lower than the price charged to Distributor, or to add a cost-plus transparency provision.

## Issue 13: Deemed-Acceptance of Purchase Orders – Operational Risk

**Where:** EDA § 5.4.

**Explanation.** A Purchase Order not rejected by Supplier within five business days is "deemed accepted." After the assignment, Orlenne must respond to Taisund's POs within that window. If Orlenne's operations team is still standing up its supply-chain function in December 2026, it may miss the five-day deadline, resulting in deemed-accepted POs that Orlenne then cannot or will not fulfill — generating on-time delivery failures (SLS § 3.1) and potentially short-ship claims.

**Why it matters.** Deemed acceptance creates a binding supply obligation. If Orlenne accepts (by silence) a PO it cannot fill, Taisund is entitled to the products and to service credits for late delivery. But Taisund's real-world need is the furniture, not the credit.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- Taisund should confirm with Orlenne that its PO-acceptance/rejection workflow is operational before December 1.
- Taisund's purchasing team should calendar the five-business-day deadline for each PO and follow up if no response is received, to create a record of deemed acceptance.

## Issue 14: Payment Transition and Dispute Mechanics

**Where:** EDA § 6.4; Notice Letter ¶ 5.3.

**Explanation.** The notice letter states that invoices for pre-Assignment-Date performance remain payable to Stepisk, and post-Assignment-Date invoices will be issued by Orlenne. Payment is due within 30 days of a correct invoice (§ 6.4). Taisund may withhold disputed amounts in good faith.

The risk is a gap or overlap in invoicing around December 1. If Stepisk invoices for services performed in late November but delivered in early December, or if Orlenne issues its first invoice for a period that straddles the Assignment Date, the allocation of payment responsibility could be unclear.

**Why it matters.** A payment dispute at the transition could be used by Orlenne to leverage other negotiations or to create a record of "Distributor breach" (non-payment) that Orlenne could later assert as a material breach under § 15.1.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- Taisund's AP team should reconcile all open invoices with Stepisk before December 1 and confirm in writing which invoices are Stepisk's final.
- Request Orlenne's first invoice template and payment instructions before the Assignment Date (the notice says they will follow "under separate cover" — ¶ 5.3). Do not pay Orlenne until instructions are received and verified.

## Issue 15: SOW Governing Law – New York – Affects SLS Disputes

**Where:** SOW § 9.1; SLS § 7.3; EDA § 2.3.

**Explanation.** SLS § 7.3 provides that unresolved service-level disputes are "handled under Section 18.3 of the Agreement" (the escalation provision). Section 18.3 is in the EDA, which is governed by Delaware law. But the underlying service-level dispute arises from the SOW/SLS, which (per the order of precedence and the SOW's own § 9.1) is governed by New York law. The interaction is unclear: does the escalation procedure follow Delaware law (because it's in the EDA) while the substantive service-level question follows New York law (because it's in the SOW)?

**Why it matters.** This is a lower-stakes issue than the others, but it could create procedural confusion if a service-level dispute escalates. The parties might disagree about which law governs the interpretation of the service-level measurement rules.

**Recommended fix / next steps.**

- Note for the file. If a service-level dispute arises, Taisund should raise the governing-law question early in the escalation process (§ 18.3) to avoid a threshold litigation over which law applies.
- In any renegotiation, conform all Contract Documents to a single governing law and forum.
