## Executive Summary

Stepisk Furnishings Manufacturing, Inc. has notified Taisund that it will assign the entire Exclusive Distribution Agreement (including the SOW and Service Level Schedule) to Orlenne Event Group LLC effective December 1, 2026. Orlenne is a direct competitor of Taisund in the same three-state Territory. The assignment is contractually permitted under Section 17.2 of the Agreement, but it creates a fundamental structural conflict with the exclusivity provisions, exposes Taisund's customer data to a rival, and places enforcement of key commercial terms in the hands of a competitor. Taisund has approximately six weeks before the Assignment Date to respond. Several issues require urgent client decision.

## Issue 1 – Assignment to a Direct Competitor Undermines the Exclusive Distribution Structure

**Where:** Stepisk Notice Letter §§ 2.2, 3.1–3.3; Agreement §§ 3.1, 3.2, 17.2; SOW § 1.1.

**Explanation:**

Orlenne is described in the Notice as operating "an event furniture rental business" serving "venues, caterers, planners and corporate customers in Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware" (Notice § 2.2). That is the identical customer base and geographic Territory in which Taisund operates as the exclusive distributor and rental operator (Agreement § 3.1).

After December 1, 2026, Orlenne becomes "Supplier" under the Contract Documents (Notice § 3.2). This creates an irreconcilable conflict with Agreement § 3.2, which provides that during the Term, "Supplier shall not, directly or through any Affiliate or third party … (a) sell, rent or otherwise supply Products to any person for use, resale or rental in the Territory other than through Distributor, or (b) appoint any other distributor, dealer or rental operator for the Products in the Territory." Orlenne is already a rental operator in the Territory. It cannot simultaneously be the "Supplier" bound by § 3.2 and an active competitor renting event furniture in PA, NJ, and DE.

Furthermore, the Notice states that Stepisk will continue to manufacture the Products and supply them to Orlenne for fulfillment (Notice § 3.3). Orlenne is a rental company, not a manufacturer. The actual supply chain becomes Stepisk → Orlenne → Taisund, adding an intermediary with no manufacturing capability and a direct competitive interest in Taisund's customer relationships.

**Why it matters:** The entire commercial rationale for Taisund's agreement is exclusivity in the Territory. If Orlenne is both the "Supplier" and a competitor renting in the same market, Taisund's exclusivity is illusory. Taisund is effectively locked into a supply relationship with its rival.

**Recommended fix:**

- Taisund should immediately send a written response to Stepisk (and copy Orlenne) raising the § 3.2 conflict and demanding clarification of how Orlenne will comply with the Supplier Restrictions while continuing its own rental operations in the Territory.
- Taisund should negotiate, as a condition of accepting the assignment: (a) a contractual firewall prohibiting Orlenne from using Taisund's Confidential Information (especially Customer identities and delivery schedules) for its own rental operations in the Territory; (b) a carve-out or amendment to § 3.2 that addresses Orlenne's pre-existing rental business; and (c) a commitment that Orlenne will not actively solicit Taisund's existing Customers.
- If Stepisk/Orlenne refuse to address the § 3.2 conflict, Taisund should consider whether the assignment is ineffective to the extent it places Orlenne in immediate breach, or whether it constitutes a material breach of the Agreement giving Taisund a termination right under § 15.1.
- **DECISION NEEDED FROM TAISUND BEFORE DECEMBER 1, 2026.**

**Facts to confirm with client:** Whether Orlenne is an Affiliate of Stepisk under Agreement § 1.1 (i.e., whether Stepisk owns >50% of Orlenne or vice versa). The Notice refers to a "broader commercial arrangement" (Notice § 2.2) but does not disclose the ownership relationship. If Orlenne is a Stepisk Affiliate, the § 3.2 prohibition on acting "through any Affiliate" is directly implicated and the conflict is even more acute.

## Issue 2 – Taisund's Customer Data Will Be Transferred to a Competitor

**Where:** Notice § 4.1(a)–(e); Agreement § 14.1, 14.2, 14.4; SOW § 10.2.

**Explanation:**

The Notice provides that on or before the Assignment Date, Stepisk will deliver to Orlenne:

- "Distributor's Forecasts and open Purchase Orders" (Notice § 4.1(b));
- "Depot schedules, delivery notes and Customer delivery schedules and locations provided in connection with expedited and scheduled deliveries" (Notice § 4.1(d));
- "the current buffer plan and weekly inventory reports" (Notice § 4.1(c)); and
- "Service Level performance reports and supporting records" (Notice § 4.1(e)).

Agreement § 14.1 expressly defines Confidential Information to include "Customer identities, Customer delivery schedules and locations, and business plans." Taisund's Forecasts (delivered monthly under § 5.3) reveal product demand by Depot and by month. Delivery notes and Customer delivery schedules reveal which venues and corporate clients Taisund serves and when.

While the Notice states that Orlenne "will be bound by" § 14 as Supplier's successor (Notice § 4.2), and while § 12.2 excludes breach of § 14 from the liability cap (providing uncapped damages for breach), the practical reality is that a direct competitor will possess Taisund's most sensitive commercial intelligence. The five-year post-termination confidentiality tail (§ 14.4) is of limited comfort if Orlenne uses the data during the Term to poach Taisund's Customers.

**Why it matters:** Customer relationships are Taisund's core asset. A competitor with knowledge of Taisund's Customer base, delivery schedules, and demand patterns can target those Customers with competing offers, undercut pricing, or time its own service to overlap.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a data-use restriction as part of the assignment acceptance: Orlenne may use Taisund's Confidential Information solely to perform its obligations as "Supplier" under the Contract Documents and not for its own rental or distribution business in the Territory.
- Consider whether Taisund should restrict the scope of Forecasts and delivery information it provides going forward, to the minimum necessary for supply planning.
- Document all Confidential Information already transferred to Stepisk so Taisund can assess what Orlenne will receive.
- **DECISION NEEDED FROM TAISUND: Whether to condition acceptance of the assignment on a data-use restriction, or to seek injunctive relief if Orlenne uses the data competitively.**

**Facts to confirm with client:** What specific Customer data has Taisund already provided to Stepisk under the Agreement (Forecasts, delivery notes, expedited delivery requests identifying Customer venues)? Taisund should inventory this before December 1.

## Issue 3 – Asymmetric Assignment Rights Leave Taisund Locked In

**Where:** Agreement §§ 17.1, 17.2.

**Explanation:**

Section 17.1 prohibits Taisund from assigning the Agreement "whether voluntarily, by operation of law or otherwise" without Stepisk's (now Orlenne's) prior written consent. Any purported assignment is void. Section 17.2 permits Supplier to assign to "any person" without Distributor's consent, upon written notice.

This asymmetry means Taisund cannot sell its business, merge with another company, or restructure without the consent of the very party that just became its competitor. Meanwhile, Stepisk was free to assign to Orlenne with no input from Taisund beyond receiving notice.

**Why it matters:** Taisund's exit options are severely constrained. If the relationship with Orlenne becomes untenable, Taisund cannot simply transfer its distribution rights to a more acceptable party. It is captive to Orlenne's terms for the duration of the Term (at least through March 2, 2029, subject to renewal).

**Recommended fix:**

- In any negotiation regarding the assignment, seek a mutual assignment provision or at minimum a right for Taisund to assign to a non-competitor with reasonable notice.
- If no amendment is achievable, ensure Taisund understands that its only exit routes are: (a) termination for Orlenne's material breach under § 15.1 (30-day cure), (b) termination for repeated service failures under § 15.3, or (c) non-renewal at the end of the then-current term (180 days' notice under § 4.2).
- **Note:** The Initial Term runs through March 2, 2029. The first non-renewal notice deadline would be September 4, 2028 (180 days before March 2, 2029). Taisund should calendar this date.

## Issue 4 – Indemnification Structure Places Product Liability Risk on Taisund Even for Defective Products

**Where:** Agreement §§ 10.1, 10.2, 12.2, 12.3.

**Explanation:**

Section 10.1 requires Taisund to defend and indemnify Stepisk/Orlenne against "any and all claims … arising out of or relating to the distribution, rental, setup or use of the Products by Distributor, any Customer or any other person, **regardless of whether caused by a defect in the Products**." This is a broad, one-way product liability indemnity.

Section 10.2 limits Supplier's indemnity to Taisund to third-party IP infringement claims only. Supplier does not indemnify Taisund for personal injury or property damage caused by defective Products.

Section 12.2 excludes indemnification obligations from the liability cap, meaning Taisund's exposure under § 10.1 is uncapped.

Taisund's protection for defective products is the 36-month warranty in § 8.1 (repair, replace, or refund at Taisund's election under § 8.3) and the right to reject non-conforming products under § 7.3. But if a defective chair injures a Customer and the Customer sues both Taisund and the manufacturer, Taisund must indemnify the manufacturer even though the defect caused the injury. Taisund's recourse is a warranty claim against the manufacturer, which is subject to the 36-month period and the practical difficulty of enforcing against a party that may be a competitor (Orlenne) or a non-manufacturer (Orlenne, with Stepisk as the actual manufacturer behind the scenes).

**Why it matters:** This is a significant and potentially uncapped financial exposure. In the event furniture rental business, a single serious injury claim could exceed Taisund's insurance limits (CGL: $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate under § 11.2). The indemnity runs to the manufacturer/supplier, so Taisund would bear the defense cost and any judgment exceeding its insurance, then seek recovery under the warranty—a process that is slow and uncertain, especially against a competitor.

**Recommended fix:**

- In any renegotiation or amendment connected to the assignment, seek to narrow § 10.1 to exclude claims "to the extent caused by a defect in design, materials or workmanship of the Products" (i.e., align it with the warranty).
- Confirm that Taisund's CGL insurance includes products-completed operations coverage adequate for the rental fleet, and that the policy does not contain a subrogation waiver that would prevent recovery from the manufacturer.
- **Facts to confirm with client:** What are Taisund's current CGL limits and does the policy cover the full rental fleet? Has Taisund made any claims or received any injury reports to date?

## Issue 5 – Split Governing Law and Forum Creates Litigation Complexity

**Where:** Agreement §§ 18.1, 18.2; SOW § 9.1; Agreement § 2.3 (order of precedence).

**Explanation:**

The Agreement is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in Wilmington, Delaware (Agreement §§ 18.1–18.2). The SOW is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County, New York (SOW § 9.1). The order of precedence (Agreement § 2.3) gives the SOW priority over the Agreement.

Because the SOW governs all refurbishment, warehousing, and delivery services—and the Service Level Schedule is incorporated into the SOW framework—most operational disputes (missed deliveries, late refurbishment, inventory inaccuracies, service credits) will be New York matters. Commercial disputes (pricing, minimum purchase, termination for breach of the distribution agreement, warranty claims on products) will be Delaware matters.

A single factual scenario could implicate both regimes. For example, if a late delivery (SOW/New York) causes Taisund to miss a Customer event and Taisund claims the minimum purchase should be reduced (Agreement/Delaware), the parties could end up in two forums under two bodies of law.

**Why it matters:** Taisund is a Pennsylvania company. Neither Delaware nor New York is home territory. The split increases legal costs, creates strategic complications, and could lead to inconsistent outcomes. It also complicates the escalation procedure in Agreement § 18.3, which applies to disputes "arising out of or relating to this Agreement" but may not clearly cover SOW-governed disputes.

**Recommended fix:**

- Seek an amendment conforming the SOW's governing law and forum to the Agreement (Delaware/Wilmington). This is a reasonable ask in the context of the assignment negotiation.
- If the split cannot be resolved, ensure Taisund's outside counsel arrangement covers both Delaware and New York litigation, and that the operations team understands which forum applies to which type of dispute.

## Issue 6 – Minimum Purchase Commitment Enforceable by a Competitor

**Where:** Agreement § 5.2; Notice § 3.2.

**Explanation:**

Taisund must purchase at least $1,200,000 in Products per contract year. If Taisund misses the minimum in two consecutive contract years, "Supplier's sole remedy is to convert the appointment in Section 3.1 to a non-exclusive appointment on 90 days' written notice" (§ 5.2).

After the assignment, Orlenne—a direct competitor—holds this remedy. If the transition to Orlenne causes service disruptions that reduce Taisund's rental revenue and its ability to meet the purchase minimum, Orlenne could convert the appointment to non-exclusive, destroying Taisund's competitive position in the Territory.

Contract Year 1 runs from March 2, 2026 through March 1, 2027. Taisund is approximately seven months into this period.

**Why it matters:** A competitor enforcing a minimum purchase commitment is a perverse incentive structure. Orlenne benefits from Taisund buying more product (increased revenue for Orlenne as "Supplier") while simultaneously competing against Taisund for the same end customers.

**Recommended fix:**

- **URGENT: Confirm with Taisund whether it is on track to meet the $1.2M minimum for Contract Year 1 (by March 1, 2027).** If Taisund is at risk, this should be raised in the assignment negotiation—seek a tolling or adjustment for the transition period.
- In any amendment, seek to make the non-exclusive conversion remedy subject to a good-faith negotiation period or to exclude periods of Supplier-caused service disruption from the calculation.

**Facts to confirm with client:** Taisund's year-to-date purchase volume against the $1.2M minimum for Contract Year 1. Any service disruptions to date that may have affected Taisund's ability to sell or rent.

**Explanation:**

The Notice provides that Stepisk will continue performing until December 1, 2026, and that "Supplier will coordinate with Orlenne on the handover of the Supplier Warehouse and delivery operations" (Notice § 5.2). However:

## Issue 8 – Warranty Enforcement Post-Assignment Is Unclear

**Where:** Agreement §§ 8.1, 8.3; Notice § 3.2–3.3; SOW § 3.4.

**Explanation:**

The product warranty runs 36 months from delivery (Agreement § 8.1). Products delivered between March 2026 and December 1, 2026 will have warranties extending to March 2029 or later. The Notice states that Orlenne assumes "Supplier's obligations accruing from and after the Assignment Date" (Notice § 3.2).

It is unclear whether a warranty claim on a product delivered in June 2026 that manifests a defect in January 2027 "accrues" before or after the Assignment Date. If the obligation accrues when the defect is discovered (January 2027), Orlenne is responsible. If it accrues when the product was delivered (June 2026), Stepisk retains the obligation—but Stepisk is no longer a party to the Agreement.

Practically, Orlenne is a rental company, not a manufacturer. Its ability to repair or replace defective Products (Agreement § 8.3) depends entirely on Stepisk's continued cooperation as the manufacturer (Notice § 3.3). If Stepisk and Orlenne's "broader commercial arrangement" breaks down, Taisund's warranty rights may be unenforceable.

**Recommended fix:**

- Seek a clarification or amendment confirming that Stepisk remains liable for warranty obligations on Products it manufactured, regardless of the assignment, or that Orlenne's warranty obligations are backed by a direct supply commitment from Stepisk.
- Ensure the SOW § 3.4 provision (no fees for warranty items) is expressly preserved post-assignment.

## Issue 9 – Service Credit Cap Is Low Relative to Taisund's Total Spend

**Where:** SLS §§ 8.1, 8.2; SOW § 6.1.

**Explanation:**

Service Credits are calculated as a percentage of "Monthly Services Fees" (SLS § 1.2(d)), which means the fees invoiced under SOW § 6 (delivery runs, warehousing, refurbishment). The total cap is 20% of Monthly Services Fees per Measurement Period (SLS § 8.2).

Based on the SOW fee schedule, estimated monthly SOW fees are roughly:
- Scheduled delivery runs: (3 runs/wk × 4 wks × $285) + (3 runs/wk × 4 wks × $285) + (2 runs/wk × 4 wks × $360) + (2 runs/wk × 4 wks × $360) = $3,420 + $3,420 + $2,880 + $2,880 = ~$12,600/month
- Warehousing: depends on pallet positions (at $22/pallet/month)—could be $2,000–$5,000/month
- Refurbishment: variable, perhaps $3,000–$8,000/month

Total Monthly Services Fees likely in the range of $18,000–$25,000/month. A 20% cap yields maximum credits of roughly $3,600–$5,000/month.

Taisund's total annual spend under the relationship (products + SOW fees) is likely $1.5M–$2M+. The service credit remedy is therefore a small fraction of the total commercial value at stake if service levels are chronically missed.

**Why it matters:** If Orlenne (or Stepisk performing for Orlenne) consistently underperforms, the financial remedy via service credits is modest. Taisund's real protection is the termination right in § 15.3 (three or more months of the same service level failure in a rolling 12-month period), but termination may not be practical if Stepisk is the sole manufacturer.

**Recommended fix:**

- In any amendment, seek to increase the service credit cap or to calculate credits as a percentage of total monthly spend (products + services) rather than SOW fees only.
- Track service level performance meticulously from the first monthly report. Document all failures to preserve the § 15.3 termination right.

## Issue 10 – Order of Precedence Favors SOW Over Main Agreement

**Where:** Agreement § 2.3.

**Explanation:**

The order of precedence is: SOW → Service Level Schedule → Distribution Agreement. This means that if the SOW and the Agreement are inconsistent on any point, the SOW controls. This is unusual; most agreement structures give the master agreement priority.

The SOW already departs from the Agreement on governing law (New York vs. Delaware) and forum. If future amendments or operational documents create further inconsistencies, the SOW will prevail.

**Why it matters:** In a dispute, the opposing party could argue that an SOW provision overrides a more favorable Agreement provision. Taisund should be aware that the operational documents (SOW, SLS) are the primary source of rights regarding service performance.

**Recommended fix:**

- In any renegotiation, seek to reverse the order of precedence so the Agreement controls over the SOW, or at minimum add a provision that the SOW shall not be construed to modify the Agreement's allocation of risk (indemnification, limitation of liability, warranty).

## Issue 11 – Most Favored Pricing Clause Is Circulated by the Assignment

**Where:** Agreement § 6.2; Notice § 3.2.

**Explanation:**

Section 6.2 provides that "Supplier shall not sell any Product to any other distributor or rental operator in the United States at a net price lower than the price then charged to Distributor for comparable volumes." After the assignment, Orlenne is both the "Supplier" and a "rental operator in the United States." The clause would prohibit Orlenne from selling Products to itself (or its own rental operations) at a lower price than Taisund pays. While this is somewhat meaningless on its face (Orlenne buying from Stepisk at a wholesale price is not the same as "selling to a rental operator"), the clause creates interpretive ambiguity and could be raised by Orlenne to argue that Taisund's prices must match whatever Orlenne pays Stepisk.

**Why it matters:** Low immediate risk, but the clause could be weaponized in a pricing dispute. If Orlenne pays Stepisk $40/unit for chiavari chairs and Taisund pays $48/unit, Orlenne could argue the MFP clause requires Taisund's price to be reduced to $40.

**Recommended fix:**

- Seek clarification that § 6.2 applies only to sales by Supplier to third-party distributors or rental operators, and not to Supplier's own purchase of Products from its manufacturer (Stepisk).

## Issue 12 – Termination Right Under § 15.3 May Be of Limited Practical Value

**Where:** Agreement § 15.3; SLS § 10.2.

**Explanation:**

Taisund may terminate if Supplier fails to meet the same service level in three or more months in any rolling 12-month period. However, termination would end Taisund's right to purchase the Products at all. Since Stepisk appears to be the sole manufacturer of the Products (Recital A; the Products are specific "Stepisk Furnishings" items), termination would leave Taisund without its core product line. Taisund would then need to find an alternative manufacturer, retrain staff, and re-equip its fleet—a process that could take 12–24 months.

**Why it matters:** The termination right is a theoretical lever but may not be a practical exit. Taisund should not count on it as its primary protection.

**Recommended fix:**

- Use the § 15.3 right as negotiating leverage (the threat of termination) rather than as a planned exit.
- In the interim, begin informal market research on alternative event furniture manufacturers to understand Taisund's fallback options.
