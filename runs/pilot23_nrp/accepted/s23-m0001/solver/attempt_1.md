## Summary

Stepisk has given written notice that it will assign the entire Exclusive Distribution Agreement, the Statement of Work, and the Service Level Schedule to Orlenne Event Group LLC effective **December 1, 2026**. Orlenne is, by Stepisk's own description, an event furniture rental operator already serving customers in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware—the same Territory in which Taisund holds an exclusive appointment. The assignment is contractually permitted under Section 17.2 of the Agreement, but its practical effect is to place Taisund's exclusive supplier in direct competition with Taisund for the same customers, while transferring Taisund's confidential customer data to that competitor. Taisund has approximately **six weeks** to decide how to respond.

The issues below are ordered by importance to the client.

## Issue 1 – Assignment to a Direct Competitor Destroys the Value of the Exclusive Appointment

**Where:** Stepisk Notice Letter §§ 1–3; Agreement § 3.1 (Exclusive Appointment), § 3.2 (Supplier Restrictions), § 17.2 (Assignment by Supplier).

**Explanation.** Section 3.1 appoints Taisund as Stepisk's *exclusive* distributor and rental operator for the Products in PA, NJ, and DE. Section 3.2 prohibits Supplier from selling, renting, or supplying Products in the Territory through anyone other than Taisund, and from appointing any other distributor, dealer, or rental operator in the Territory. The entire commercial rationale for Taisund's investment in Depots, fleet, and staff is this exclusivity.

The Notice Letter states that Orlenne "operates an event furniture rental business" and "serves venues, caterers, planners and corporate customers in Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware" (§ 2.2). From December 1, Orlenne will simultaneously be (a) the contractual "Supplier" owed exclusivity protections to Taisund, and (b) a rental operator competing for the same customers in the same Territory. Section 3.2's restriction binds "Supplier"—so technically Orlenne cannot *appoint another* distributor or sell Products *to* third parties in the Territory. But nothing in Section 3.2 prevents Orlenne from using the Products in *its own* rental business, which it is already doing.

Section 17.2 permits Supplier to assign "to any person … without the consent of Distributor, upon written notice to Distributor." The notice has been given. On its face, the assignment is valid.

**Why it matters.** This is the single most consequential development in the matter. If Taisund does nothing, it will be locked into a three-year exclusive distribution relationship (through March 2, 2029, plus potential renewal terms) with a direct competitor as its counterparty. Taisund's customers will see Orlenne marketing the same Stepisk products, potentially at lower effective prices (see Issue 5), and Taisund's competitive moat will be gone.

**Recommended fix / options for Taisund:**

1. **Negotiate before December 1.** Taisund has leverage: Orlenne cannot operate the logistics network without Taisund's Depots, Forecasts, and cooperation. Taisund should open a dialogue with both Stepisk and Orlenne to negotiate:
   - A firewall provision prohibiting Orlenne from using Taisund's Confidential Information (customer lists, delivery schedules, pricing) in its own rental operations;
   - A commitment that Orlenne will not actively solicit Taisund's existing Customers for a defined period (e.g., 12–24 months);
   - Enhanced service levels or price concessions to compensate for the loss of exclusivity value;
   - A mutual non-compete or market-sharing arrangement.

2. **Assert that the assignment frustrates the purpose of the contract.** Under Delaware law (governing per § 18.1), the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing prohibits a party from acting in a way that destroys the value of the other party's contractual rights. Taisund could argue that assigning to a known competitor in the Territory, while technically permitted by § 17.2, breaches the covenant because it nullifies the exclusivity that was the bargained-for consideration. This is a strong but not certain argument; § 17.2 is broad and unambiguous.

3. **Reserve the right to terminate under § 15.1** if post-assignment conduct constitutes a material breach (e.g., Orlenne soliciting Taisund's customers, failing to refer inquiries under § 3.3, or degrading service levels).

4. **If no satisfactory resolution is reached, consider terminating** under § 15.1 (if a breach is identified) or allowing the relationship to run its course while protecting Taisund's position (see Issue 10 on the § 15.3 fallback).

**⚠️ URGENT – Decision needed by early November 2026.** Taisund should decide whether to negotiate, litigate, or accept the assignment. The Assignment Date is December 1, 2026.

**Facts to confirm with client:**
- Does Taisund know Orlenne as a competitor? Which specific customers overlap?
- What is Orlenne's approximate market share or fleet size in the Territory?
- Has Taisund had any prior dealings with Orlenne?
- Is Taisund willing to walk away from the Stepisk product line if necessary, or is it strategically committed?

## Issue 2 – Transfer of Taisund's Confidential Information (Including Customer Data) to a Competitor

**Where:** Agreement § 14.1 (definition of Confidential Information includes "Customer identities, Customer delivery schedules and locations"); Notice Letter § 4.1(a)–(e) (records to be transferred to Orlenne); SOW § 10.2 (inventory reports, buffer plans, Depot schedules are Confidential Information).

**Explanation.** The Notice Letter confirms that on or before December 1, Stepisk will deliver to Orlenne:
- Taisund's Forecasts and open Purchase Orders (§ 4.1(b));
- The current buffer plan and weekly inventory reports (§ 4.1(c));
- **Depot schedules, delivery notes, and Customer delivery schedules and locations** (§ 4.1(d));
- Service Level performance reports and supporting records (§ 4.1(e)).

All of these are Taisund's Confidential Information under § 14.1. While the Notice Letter states that Orlenne "will be bound by [Section 14] as Supplier's successor" (§ 4.2), the practical reality is that a direct competitor will possess Taisund's customer delivery schedules, event locations, and demand forecasts. Orlenne can use this information to time its own deliveries, bid on the same events, and optimize its inventory against Taisund's.

Section 14.2 permits disclosure to "employees, contractors and advisors who need to know it"—a broad category for a competitor.

**Why it matters.** Customer delivery schedules and event locations are among the most commercially sensitive data in the event rental business. They reveal Taisund's revenue pipeline, customer concentration, and operational patterns. In the hands of a competitor, this data has immediate tactical value.

**Recommended fix:**
- As part of any negotiation (see Issue 1), require that Orlenne's access to Taisund's Confidential Information be limited to what is strictly necessary for supply-chain operations (e.g., Product codes, quantities, Depot addresses) and exclude customer-level data (Customer names, event dates, event locations, delivery schedules tied to specific Customers).
- Require a separate data-processing or information-barrier agreement between Taisund and Orlenne.
- If no agreement is reached, Taisund should consider whether the transfer of this data to a competitor constitutes a material breach of § 14 (arguably yes, if Orlenne uses it for its own competitive purposes) and whether that provides a termination trigger under § 15.1.

**⚠️ URGENT – Address before December 1, 2026.**

## Issue 3 – Conflicting Governing Law and Forum Clauses

**Where:** Agreement § 18.1–18.2 (Delaware law; exclusive jurisdiction in Wilmington, DE); SOW § 9.1 (New York law; exclusive jurisdiction in New York County, NY); Agreement § 2.3 (Order of Precedence: SOW > SLS > EDA).

**Explanation.** The SOW contains its own governing law and forum clause selecting New York law and New York County courts. The Agreement selects Delaware law and Wilmington courts. Under § 2.3, the SOW prevails over the Agreement in the event of conflict. The Service Level Schedule has no independent governing law clause.

This means:
- Disputes about the core distribution relationship (exclusivity, pricing, termination, assignment) are governed by Delaware law in Delaware.
- Disputes about the operational services (refurbishment, warehousing, delivery, fees) are governed by New York law in New York.
- Disputes about Service Levels are ambiguous—the SLS is incorporated into the Agreement (§ 11.1) but the SOW (which prevails) has its own NY clause.

**Why it matters.** For Taisund (a Pennsylvania company), neither forum is home. But the split creates:
- Increased litigation cost and complexity (two potential forums, two bodies of law);
- Risk of inconsistent rulings on overlapping issues (e.g., a service-level failure that also constitutes a material breach of the Agreement);
- Strategic disadvantage if Taisund needs to bring a termination claim (Delaware) that is intertwined with service-level failures (New York).

In the context of the Orlenne assignment, this is particularly acute: Taisund may want to challenge the assignment in Delaware (under the Agreement) while also asserting service-level claims in New York (under the SOW).

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation or amendment, conform the SOW's governing law and forum to the Agreement (Delaware / Wilmington).
- If no amendment is achievable, be aware of the split in litigation planning. Document which claims arise under which document.

**Facts to confirm:** Whether the SLS was intended to follow the EDA's Delaware clause or the SOW's New York clause. The SLS is silent, and § 2.3's precedence rule (SOW over SLS over EDA) suggests the SOW's NY clause controls for SLS-related disputes, but this is not certain.

## Issue 4 – Broad One-Sided Indemnification by Taisund for Product Defects

**Where:** Agreement § 10.1 (Distributor Indemnification).

**Explanation.** Section 10.1 requires Taisund to defend and indemnify Supplier against claims "arising out of or relating to the distribution, rental, setup or use of the Products by Distributor, any Customer or any other person, **regardless of whether caused by a defect in the Products**." This means if a Stepisk chair collapses at a wedding and a guest is injured, Taisund must indemnify the Supplier even if the chair was defectively manufactured.

Section 10.2 (Supplier's indemnity) is limited to IP infringement claims only.

The liability cap in § 12.1 does not protect Taisund here because § 12.2 expressly excludes § 10 obligations from the cap.

**Why it matters.** Post-assignment, Taisund's indemnity runs to Orlenne—a competitor with potentially less financial substance than Stepisk (a manufacturing corporation vs. a rental LLC). But more importantly, the clause is commercially unusual: most distribution agreements allocate product-defect liability to the manufacturer/supplier. Taisund is bearing manufacturing risk it cannot control.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation, seek to amend § 10.1 to exclude claims "to the extent caused by a defect in design, materials or workmanship of the Products" (i.e., carve out what § 8.1 warranty covers).
- At minimum, confirm that Taisund's product liability insurance (beyond the CGL required by § 11.2) covers this exposure. The required limits in § 11.2 ($1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate) may be insufficient for a serious product-liability claim.

**Facts to confirm with client:** What product liability insurance does Taisund currently carry, and at what limits? Has Taisund had any product-related injury claims to date?

## Issue 5 – Most Favored Pricing Clause Nullified by Orlenne's Dual Role

**Where:** Agreement § 6.2 (Most Favored Pricing); Notice Letter §§ 2.2, 3.3.

**Explanation.** Section 6.2 provides: "Supplier shall not sell any Product to any other distributor or rental operator in the United States at a net price lower than the price then charged to Distributor for comparable volumes."

Post-assignment, Orlenne is both the "Supplier" and a "rental operator." The clause prevents Orlenne from selling Products to *other* distributors or rental operators at a lower price. But Orlenne's *own* rental operations are not "selling to another distributor or rental operator"—they are Orlenne's internal use. Orlenne can buy Products from Stepisk at a wholesale price (per § 3.3 of the Notice Letter: "Supplier will supply them to Orlenne for fulfillment under the Agreement") and rent them to its own customers at a price that undercuts Taisund's all-in cost (Exhibit A price + SOW fees + Taisund's own overhead).

**Why it matters.** Taisund pays the Exhibit A net prices plus SOW service fees (refurbishment, warehousing, delivery runs, expedited deliveries). Orlenne, as the new Supplier, presumably receives Products from Stepisk at a lower internal transfer price and does not pay itself the SOW fees. Orlenne's cost base is structurally lower, allowing it to rent the same products at prices Taisund cannot match without destroying its margins.

**Recommended fix:**
- Negotiate a price protection provision: Orlenne's rental rates for Stepisk Products in the Territory shall not be less than Taisund's rental rates for the same Products, or Taisund's all-in cost shall be reduced to account for Orlenne's lower procurement cost.
- Alternatively, require that Orlenne pay Taisund the SOW fees for any Products Orlenne sources from the Supplier Warehouse for its own use (leveling the playing field).
- If no price protection is achievable, factor Orlenne's cost advantage into Taisund's go/no-go decision on the relationship.

## Issue 6 – Section 3.3 Customer Referral Obligation Is Operationally Impossible Post-Assignment

**Where:** Agreement § 3.3; Notice Letter § 2.2.

**Explanation.** Section 3.3 requires Supplier to "refer to Distributor all inquiries it receives for Products for delivery or use in the Territory." Post-assignment, Orlenne must refer inquiries to Taisund. But Orlenne is already serving those customers. An inquiry from a venue that Orlenne already rents to is not a new lead—it is Orlenne's existing business. Requiring Orlenne to refer its own customers to Taisund is commercially absurd and will either (a) not be followed, creating a breach claim, or (b) be followed in a way that harms Orlenne's existing relationships, creating friction.

**Why it matters.** This provision was designed to prevent Supplier from bypassing the exclusive distributor. Post-assignment, it becomes a source of ongoing disputes. Taisund cannot realistically enforce it without damaging the working relationship it needs for supply-chain continuity.

**Recommended fix:**
- In renegotiation, replace § 3.3 with a provision that Orlenne shall refer to Taisund only *new* inquiries (i.e., from customers that Orlenne does not already serve).
- If no amendment is reached, be prepared for this to be a low-enforcement provision that Taisund should not build its strategy around.

## Issue 7 – Insurance Verification for Orlenne

**Where:** Agreement § 11.1 (Supplier insurance: $2M per occurrence / $5M aggregate CGL with products/completed operations, during Term + 3 years); § 11.3 (certificates on request).

**Explanation.** From December 1, Orlenne must maintain the insurance required of "Supplier" under § 11.1. This includes products and completed operations coverage—a critical layer given that Orlenne will be the entity responsible for manufacturing-quality products in the market.

**Why it matters.** If Orlenne is a smaller rental business, it may not currently carry products/completed operations coverage at the required limits. A gap in insurance would leave Taisund exposed if a product fails and Orlenne cannot satisfy a judgment.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Request Orlenne's certificate of insurance before December 1, 2026**, pursuant to § 11.3. Confirm that it includes products and completed operations coverage at not less than $2M/$5M.
- If Orlenne cannot provide adequate coverage, this is a material issue that should be raised before the Assignment Date and could support a claim that the assignment is not being effected in accordance with the Agreement's requirements.

**⚠️ Action needed before December 1, 2026.**

## Issue 8 – Asymmetric Assignment Rights (Structural Issue)

**Where:** Agreement § 17.1 (Distributor may not assign without Supplier's consent); § 17.2 (Supplier may assign to any person without consent).

**Explanation.** The assignment rights are entirely one-way. Taisund cannot assign, sell, or merge without Stepisk/Orlenne's consent. Stepisk/Orlenne can assign to anyone, including a competitor, with mere notice.

**Why it matters.** This asymmetry is what enabled the Orlenne assignment. Even if Taisund prevails on the good-faith argument (Issue 1), the structural imbalance means the Supplier side can unilaterally change its identity at any time. If Taisund ever wants to restructure (e.g., merge with another rental company, sell a division), it needs the Supplier's consent.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any amendment, add a restriction on § 17.2: Supplier may not assign to an entity that is a direct competitor of Distributor in the Territory without Distributor's prior written consent.
- Alternatively, add a provision that if Supplier assigns to a competitor, Distributor has the right to terminate on 60 days' notice with a transition period.

## Issue 9 – Minimum Purchase Commitment Risk Post-Assignment

**Where:** Agreement § 5.2 ($1,200,000 minimum per contract year; remedy for two consecutive shortfalls is conversion to non-exclusive).

**Explanation.** If Orlenne undercuts Taisund on price (Issue 5) or diverts shared customers, Taisund's revenue may decline, making it harder to meet the $1.2M annual minimum. The remedy for missing the minimum in two consecutive years is conversion to non-exclusive—not termination—so Taisund would lose exclusivity but remain bound to the purchase commitment structure.

**Why it matters.** The minimum is a fixed dollar amount that does not adjust for market share loss caused by the Supplier's own competitive activities. If Orlenne's competition causes Taisund to fall below the minimum, Taisund would be penalized (loss of exclusivity) for a consequence of the assignment.

**Recommended fix:**
- In renegotiation, seek to either (a) reduce the minimum proportionally if Orlenne is competing in the Territory, or (b) provide that shortfall caused by Supplier/Orlenne's competitive activity does not count toward the two-consecutive-year trigger.
- Track Taisund's purchases against the minimum closely. If Taisund is at risk of missing the minimum in contract year 1 (March 2026–March 2027) or year 2, this becomes urgent.

**Facts to confirm:** What is Taisund's year-to-date purchase volume against the $1.2M minimum for the current contract year?

## Issue 10 – Service Level Termination Right as a Fallback

**Where:** Agreement § 15.3 (termination if Supplier fails the same service level in 3+ months in any rolling 12-month period); SLS § 10.2 (failures count toward § 15.3); SLS § 8 (service credits).

**Explanation.** If Orlenne's service performance deteriorates post-assignment (e.g., because it is diverting logistics resources to its own rental operations), Taisund can terminate under § 15.3 after three months of failure on the same service level in a rolling 12-month period. This is a no-fault termination right—no cure period required.

**Why it matters.** This is Taisund's cleanest exit path if the relationship with Orlenne does not work. It does not require proving a "material breach" (which would be harder given § 17.2's express permission to assign). It simply requires documenting service-level failures.

**Recommended fix:**
- Ensure Taisund's operations team is rigorously documenting all service-level failures from day one of the Orlenne transition.
- Track failures by service level and by month. If the same level (e.g., Replacement Time under SLS § 2.5) is missed in three months within a rolling 12-month window, Taisund can terminate on 30 days' notice.
- Note that § 16.3 gives Taisund a 12-month sell-off period post-termination, during which it can continue renting existing inventory and using Stepisk/Orlenne trademarks.

## Issue 11 – Stepisk Remains in the Supply Chain but Is Not a Contractual Party

**Where:** Notice Letter § 3.3 ("Supplier will continue to manufacture the Products. From the Assignment Date, Supplier will supply them to Orlenne for fulfillment under the Agreement").

**Explanation.** Post-assignment, Stepisk still manufactures the Products but sells them to Orlenne, not to Taisund. Taisund's contractual counterparty for all supply, refurbishment, and logistics obligations is Orlenne. If Stepisk delays manufacturing or ships defective products to Orlenne, Taisund has no direct contractual claim against Stepisk—only against Orlenne.

**Why it matters.** This adds a layer of risk. Orlenne's ability to meet service levels depends on Stepisk's manufacturing performance, but Orlenne remains liable to Taisund for any failure. If there is a dispute, Taisund must go after Orlenne, which then must go after Stepisk. This is a weaker position than dealing directly with the manufacturer.

**Recommended fix:**
- Seek a tripartite acknowledgment or side letter from Stepisk confirming its manufacturing commitments to Orlenne for the benefit of Taisund, or at minimum confirming that Stepisk will honor the Specifications and warranty obligations (§ 8) for Products it manufactures post-assignment.
- Confirm that Stepisk's product warranty (§ 8.1, 36 months) continues to run from delivery to Taisund's Depot, regardless of the intermediate sale to Orlenne.

## Issue 12 – SOW Transition Obligations May Be Inadequate

**Where:** SOW § 8.3 (on termination, Supplier must complete in-progress refurbishment and deliver all inventory to Depots within 30 days).

**Explanation.** If Taisund terminates (under § 15.1, § 15.3, or by mutual agreement), Orlenne must return Taisund's inventory from the Supplier Warehouse within 30 days. However, the Supplier Warehouse is at 300 Commodore Distribution Way, Bridgeport, NJ (SOW § 4.1). Post-assignment, this warehouse may be operated by or for Orlenne. Taisund's buffer inventory and any products in refurbishment are physically in Orlenne's control.

**Why it matters.** In a contested termination, Taisund's inventory (title passed to Taisund under § 7.2 upon delivery to Depot, and products at the Supplier Warehouse are held by Supplier as bailee under SOW § 4.4) could be at risk. A 30-day transition in a contentious scenario may not be sufficient.

**Recommended fix:**
- Before the Assignment Date, confirm the status of all Taisund inventory at the Supplier Warehouse (physical count under SOW § 4.5, which Taisund is entitled to on 48 hours' notice).
- In any termination scenario, demand immediate delivery of inventory rather than waiting the full 30 days, and be prepared to seek injunctive relief under § 18.4 (which bypasses the escalation requirement) if Orlenne withholds inventory.

## Conclusion

The Orlenne assignment is a fundamental change to the commercial relationship that Taisund did not anticipate when it negotiated the exclusive appointment. While the assignment is technically permitted by § 17.2, its effect—placing a direct competitor in the Supplier seat, transferring Taisund's customer data to that competitor, and nullifying the price protections—goes to the heart of what Taisund bargained for. Taisund's strongest position is to use the six-week window before December 1 to negotiate meaningful protections (information barriers, non-solicitation, price parity, or a right to terminate). If negotiation fails, Taisund should be prepared to (a) challenge the assignment under the implied covenant of good faith, (b) document service-level failures for a § 15.3 exit, or (c) accept the assignment while protecting its downside through the sell-off period (§ 16.3) and the 12-month transition right (SOW § 8.3).

The governing law conflict (Issue 3) and the broad indemnity (Issue 4) are secondary but should be addressed in any amendment.
