## ISSUES REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION

### 1. Kestrune Engine License Consent Missing from Required Consents

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Item 3 (consent restriction disclosed); Schedule 4.5, Items 1–2 (only Halvern and Ostrava listed); APA § 4.3 (No Conflicts representation); APA § 7.2(d) (closing condition tied to Schedule 4.5).

**Why it matters:** Schedule 4.9, Item 3 expressly states that Section 15.3 of the Kestrune Engine License prohibits assignment without Kestrune's prior written consent and that "any assignment made without that consent is void." The Kestrune Engine License is the engine on which every Emberfall Saga title and the unreleased sequel are developed (Schedule 4.9, Item 3). Yet Kestrune's consent is absent from Schedule 4.5 (Required Consents). Because APA § 7.2(d) conditions closing only on consents "set forth on Schedule 4.5," the buyer could close without Kestrune's consent and find the engine license assignment void. This would strip the buyer of the right to use the engine for existing titles and the sequel.

**Recommendation:** Add Kestrune's consent to Schedule 4.5 as a Required Consent before signing the final agreement or before closing. Alternatively, make it a specific closing condition in § 7.2. Confirm whether Kestrune has been notified and whether consent is likely. If Kestrune can withhold consent, the buyer needs a walk-right or a price adjustment.

### 2. Side Letter Eliminates Excluded Liabilities Carve-Out from the Indemnity Cap

**Where:** Side Letter § 2.1 (restated APA § 9.5(b)); compare original APA § 9.5(b) (page 5); APA § 9.5(a) (Cap = $4,800,000; threshold = $240,000); APA § 9.2(c) (indemnity for Excluded Liabilities).

**Why it matters:** Original § 9.5(b) provided that the Cap and threshold do not apply to three categories: (i) Fraud, (ii) Fundamental Representations, and (iii) "Seller's obligations in respect of Excluded Liabilities pursuant to Section 9.2(c)." The Side Letter's restated § 9.5(b) deletes clause (iii). The practical effect: the Pre-Closing Player Litigation (a putative nationwide consumer class action over randomized in-game purchases, per Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.4, Item 1) is an Excluded Liability. Under the original provision, the buyer could recover unlimited amounts from Seller for that litigation. Under the Side Letter, recovery for Excluded Liabilities is now capped at $4,800,000 in the aggregate with all other § 9.2 claims. A nationwide class action seeking restitution, statutory damages, and attorneys' fees could easily exceed that cap, leaving the buyer bearing the excess.

**Recommendation:** This is a material economic concession to Seller. Before agreeing to the Side Letter, the client should either (a) restore clause (iii) so Excluded Liabilities remain outside the Cap, or (b) negotiate a separate, higher cap (or no cap) specifically for the Pre-Closing Player Litigation, or (c) require Seller to maintain a dedicated escrow or letter of credit for that litigation. At minimum, the client should model the worst-case class action exposure and confirm the $4.8M cap is adequate.

### 3. Escrow Release Date (6 Months) Is Inconsistent with General Representation Survival (18 Months)

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 4.2 (Release Date = 6 months post-Closing); APA § 9.1 (General Representations survive 18 months); APA § 9.6 (escrow is "sole and exclusive source of recovery" for General Representation breaches, except Fraud).

**Why it matters:** APA § 9.6 makes the Indemnity Escrow Funds the sole and exclusive remedy for breaches of General Representations. The Escrow Agreement releases all remaining funds to Seller at 6 months (less any Pending Claim Amount). After the Release Date, no escrow funds remain. For the 12-month window between month 6 and month 18, if a General Representation breach is discovered, the buyer has no recourse: the survival period has not expired, but the exclusive source of recovery (the escrow) has been emptied. This effectively truncates the buyer's practical protection for General Representations from 18 months to 6 months.

**Recommendation:** Extend the Release Date in Escrow Agreement § 4.2 to 18 months post-Closing to align with the survival period. Alternatively, amend APA § 9.6 to permit direct recovery from Seller for General Representation breaches discovered after the Release Date. The first option is cleaner and preserves the escrow's security function.

### 4. Buyer's Objection to Supplement No. 1 Was Likely Timely-Barred

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Part 3 (Supplement No. 1, delivered October 2, 2026); APA § 6.3(b) (5 Business Days to object); Side Letter § 1.2(c)–(d) (Buyer objected October 16; Seller contends deemed acceptance).

**Why it matters:** Supplement No. 1 was delivered by email on Friday, October 2, 2026. Counting five Business Days (per APA § 1.1, excluding weekends and New York bank holidays): Monday October 5 (1), Tuesday October 6 (2), Wednesday October 7 (3), Thursday October 8 (4), Friday October 9 (5). The objection deadline was October 9. Buyer's objection was delivered October 16—seven Business Days late. Under the plain text of § 6.3(b), the Supplement is "deemed accepted" and "treated as having cured any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty disclosed in it."

If deemed accepted, the Orvenna Sound Collective claim (Supplement No. 1, § 4.2) cures the § 4.12(a) title representation and the § 4.12(c) non-infringement representation. The buyer would lose indemnification rights for the Orvenna claim under Article IX.

**Recommendation:** This needs immediate strategic assessment. Options include: (a) argue that the email delivery date is not the "receipt" date under § 11.2 (notices are effective "when sent by email with confirmation of transmission"—arguably the receipt date is when the recipient actually receives it, not when it is sent); (b) seek a negotiated waiver of the deemed-acceptance provision from Seller in exchange for other concessions; or (c) treat the Orvenna claim as a known risk and adjust the purchase price or escrow accordingly. The Side Letter's § 3 (Reservation of Rights) preserves the dispute but does not resolve it.

### 5. Orvenna Sound Collective Claim – Soundtrack IP at Risk

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Supplement No. 1, §§ 4.2–4.4; Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Item 8 (Hallorin Bridge score is "work made for hire"); IP Assignment Agreement, Exhibit A, Part 2 (only two copyright registrations listed—no separate score registration).

**Why it matters:** Orvenna Sound Collective claims copyright ownership in the main theme and in-game combat suite based on an alleged 2019 co-writing arrangement with Hallorin Bridge members. The demand letter (received September 28, 2026) gives 30 days to comply before seeking injunctive relief—meaning the deadline falls around October 28, 2026. The IP Assignment Agreement's Exhibit A does not list any separate copyright registration for the soundtrack. If the score is not independently registered, the buyer's chain of title to the soundtrack may be incomplete or disputed. Injunctive relief against distribution of the affected tracks would directly impair the value of the franchise.

**Recommendation:** (a) Confirm whether the 30-day deadline has passed or will pass before closing, and whether Orvenna has filed for injunctive relief. (b) Require Seller to produce the Hallorin Bridge agreement and any co-writing documentation to assess the claim's merit. (c) If the claim has substance, negotiate a specific indemnity from Seller (outside the Cap) or a purchase price reduction. (d) Ensure the Assignment and Assumption Agreement (Exhibit B to the APA) expressly covers all unregistered copyrights in the soundtrack, including any works of Hallorin Bridge.

### 6. Key Employee Non-Compete Likely Unenforceable Under California Law

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 6.1 (24-month non-compete, Territory = US, Canada, UK, EU); § 9.1 (governing law = California; venue = Los Angeles); APA § 6.10 (requires non-compete as part of Key Employee Agreement); Daibaind Member Consent § 1.6 (acknowledges covenants as condition of transaction).

**Why it matters:** California Business & Professions Code § 16600 voids every contract that restrains a person from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business, with narrow exceptions that do not apply here. The non-compete in § 6.1—prohibiting Fenwright from working in premium action RPG or fantasy adventure games for 24 months across four jurisdictions—is almost certainly unenforceable under California law. The choice-of-law clause (§ 9.1) selects California, which compounds the problem. The non-solicitation covenants (§§ 6.2–6.3) may survive under California law (see *AMN Healthcare v. Aya Healthcare*, 21 Cal. App. 5th 114 (2017)), but the non-compete does not.

The deal structure depends on this covenant: APA § 6.10 makes it a condition, the Member Consent treats it as a key protection, and the $450,000 retention bonus + $1.2M equity grant are presumably priced in part on the assumption that Fenwright will be locked in.

**Recommendation:** (a) Change the governing law and venue in the Key Employee Agreement to Texas (where Fenwright works and the Business is located). Texas law permits enforceable non-competes under Bus. & Com. Code § 15.50 if ancillary to an otherwise enforceable agreement and reasonable in scope. (b) Alternatively, if California law must govern (e.g., for 409A or other reasons), replace the non-compete with a robust non-solicitation of employees and business partners, a confidentiality obligation, and a garden-leave or clawback provision. (c) Confirm with employment counsel that the revised covenants are enforceable in the chosen jurisdiction.

### 7. IP Assignment Agreement Covers Only Registered IP

**Where:** IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1(b) (Assigned Copyrights = "copyright registrations and applications... set forth on Exhibit A"); Exhibit A, Part 2 (only two registrations listed); compare APA § 2.1(b)(iii) (Purchased IP includes "all registered and unregistered copyrights").

**Why it matters:** The IP Assignment Agreement is the dedicated instrument for IP transfer, but its scope is limited to registered or applied-for IP on Exhibit A. Unregistered copyrights—source code, art assets, the soundtrack, the unreleased sequel, domain names, social media accounts—are not captured by this agreement. They would need to be transferred via the Bill of Sale (Exhibit A) or the Assignment and Assumption Agreement (Exhibit B), neither of which is in the folder for review. If those documents do not expressly cover unregistered IP, there is a gap in the transfer mechanism.

**Recommendation:** Review Exhibits A and B (Bill of Sale and Assignment and Assumption Agreement) to confirm they expressly assign all unregistered copyrights, trade secrets, and other IP not on the IP Assignment Agreement's Exhibit A. If they do not, amend them before closing.

If closing occurs in October–December 2026, the 38-person Austin development team (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.10) will have no committed office space for up to three months. ### 9. Fenwright's Dual Role Creates Execution Risk

**Where:** Daibaind Member Consent (Fenwright holds 34% membership interest; § 4.2 authorizes only "commercially reasonable efforts" to cause her to sign); APA § 6.10 (Seller "shall cause" Key Employee to sign); APA § 3.2(d) (Key Employee Agreement is a Seller closing deliverable); APA § 7.2(f) (delivery of § 3.2 items is a closing condition).

**Why it matters:** Fenwright is simultaneously a 34% owner of Seller (entitled to ~$16.3M of the Purchase Price) and the Key Employee whose signed agreement is a closing condition. The Member Consent authorizes only "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain her signature, not an absolute obligation. If she is dissatisfied with the Key Employee Agreement terms (particularly if the non-compete is struck under Issue 6), she could refuse to sign, blocking the closing. Seller would be in breach of § 6.10, but the buyer's remedy would be limited to the termination right in § 8.1(c) (20-day cure period) or a damages claim.

**Recommendation:** (a) Confirm Fenwright has reviewed and is comfortable with the Key Employee Agreement before closing. (b) Consider making her signature a condition to the Member Consent's effectiveness (i.e., the consent is conditional on her executing the Key Employee Agreement). (c) If the non-compete is modified per Issue 6, ensure she re-consents to the revised terms.

### 10. Side Letter Execution Status Unclear

**Where:** Side Letter, signature blocks (Buyer signature line present; Seller block reads "Agreed and accepted as of October 21, 2026" with signature line).

**Why it matters:** The Side Letter amends APA § 9.5(b) (a material economic term). APA § 11.9 requires the amendment to be "signed by Buyer and Seller." If Seller has not yet countersigned, the amendment is not effective, and the original § 9.5(b) (with the Excluded Liabilities carve-out) remains in force. The client should not proceed on the assumption that the Side Letter is agreed.

**Recommendation:** Confirm whether Seller has executed the Side Letter. If not, do not treat the amended § 9.5(b) as binding. If the client wants the original § 9.5(b) preserved (per Issue 2), do not allow the Side Letter to be executed without first negotiating the Excluded Liabilities carve-out back in.

### 11. Escrow Tax Allocation

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 7.1 (Buyer treated as owner of Indemnity Escrow Funds for tax purposes; all earnings allocated to Buyer until disbursed).

**Why it matters:** The buyer will recognize taxable income on escrow earnings (interest on $4.8M in a money market fund) even though the funds are held as security for Seller's indemnification obligations. This is a modest but unnecessary tax cost.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate a change so that earnings are allocated to Seller (the party whose obligations the escrow secures) or split proportionally. Alternatively, accept the cost if it is immaterial (at current rates, approximately $30K–$50K per year on $4.8M).
