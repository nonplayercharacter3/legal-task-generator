### 1. Side Letter Eliminates Uncapped Excluded Liability Indemnity for the Consumer Class Action

**Where:** Side Letter § 2.1 (restating APA § 9.5(b)); APA §§ 2.4(d), 9.2(c), 9.5(b); Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.4, Item 1.

**Issue:** The original APA § 9.5(b) carved three categories out of the $4.8M cap: (i) Fraud, (ii) Fundamental Representations, and (iii) "Seller's obligations in respect of Excluded Liabilities pursuant to Section 9.2(c)." The Side Letter (October 21, 2026) restates § 9.5(b) to retain only the Fraud and Fundamental Representation carve-outs, deleting clause (iii) in its entirety.

The Pre-Closing Player Litigation (*Calloway, et al. v. Daibaind Games, LLC*, Case No. 1:26-cv-00814, W.D. Tex.) is an Excluded Liability under APA § 2.4(d) and is therefore indemnified under APA § 9.2(c). It is a putative nationwide consumer class action alleging violations of the Texas DTPA and other states' consumer protection laws, with claims for restitution, statutory damages, attorneys' fees, and injunctive relief. Under the Side Letter, Seller's indemnification exposure for this matter is now capped at $4.8M—the same amount held in escrow.

**Why it matters:** A multi-state consumer class action with statutory damages per consumer can easily produce a judgment well in excess of $4.8M. The Side Letter converts what was an uncapped indemnity into one limited to the escrow. Because APA § 9.6 makes the escrow the "sole and exclusive source of recovery" for General Representation breaches (and, post-Side-Letter, for Excluded Liability claims as well), Buyer's maximum recovery for the class action is $4.8M regardless of the actual judgment.

**Recommendation:** Before we go back to the other side, we need to determine whether the Side Letter was negotiated with full understanding of its effect on the *Calloway* matter. If not, we should seek to reinstate the Excluded Liability carve-out or, alternatively, negotiate a separate specific indemnity for the Pre-Closing Player Litigation that is not subject to the cap. At minimum, we should confirm with the client that they accepted this trade knowingly.

### 2. Buyer's Objection to Supplement No. 1 Was Likely Untimely—Risk of Deemed Acceptance

**Where:** APA § 6.3(b); Disclosure Schedule, Part 3 (Supplement No. 1, § 4.1); Side Letter, Recital 1.2(c)–(d).

**Issue:** Supplement No. 1 was delivered to Buyer on October 2, 2026 (a Thursday). APA § 6.3(b) gives Buyer five (5) Business Days to object. Counting from October 3 (Friday) through October 9 (Thursday), the objection deadline was October 9, 2026. Buyer's written objection was delivered October 16, 2026—seven Business Days after delivery.

Under § 6.3(b), failure to object within the period results in deemed acceptance, and "the Schedule Supplement is treated as having cured any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty disclosed in it, for purposes of Article IX and of the condition in Section 7.2(a)."

The Side Letter (Recital 1.2(d)) confirms that Seller contends the supplement was deemed accepted and that Buyer disputes that contention. Both parties "reserve all of its rights and positions."

**Why it matters:** Supplement No. 1 discloses the Orvenna Sound Collective claim (alleged copyright ownership in the Emberfall Saga soundtrack, including the main theme and combat suite). If the supplement is deemed accepted, it cures the inaccuracy in APA § 4.12(c) (no infringement claims) and § 4.9 (score is a work made for hire owned by Seller). That means:

- Buyer loses its indemnification right under APA § 9.2(a) for the Orvenna claim (Article IX is expressly excluded by § 6.3(b)).
- The closing condition in § 7.2(a) (reps true and correct) is satisfied despite the inaccuracy.
- Buyer acquires the soundtrack IP subject to a live copyright claim with a 30-day demand for cessation of distribution, and has no contractual recourse against Seller.

**Recommendation:** This is the most time-sensitive issue. We need to (a) confirm the exact delivery date and method of Supplement No. 1 (the supplement says email to General Counsel with a copy by overnight courier—§ 11.2 provides that email notice is effective "when sent by email with confirmation of transmission," so the five-day clock likely ran from October 2); (b) assess whether there is any argument that the objection period had not yet expired (e.g., if the overnight courier copy arrived later and that controls); and (c) prepare a fallback position. If the objection is truly late, we should negotiate a specific indemnity for the Orvenna matter or a price adjustment, and consider whether to make the resolution of the Orvenna claim a condition to closing. We should also evaluate whether to pursue the dispute or accept it and address the risk commercially.

### 3. Kestrune Engine License Anti-Assignment Clause—Consent Missing from Schedule 4.5

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.9, Item 3; APA §§ 4.5, 7.2(d), 2.1(c); Kestrune Engine License § 15.3 (as described in the Disclosure Schedule).

**Issue:** Schedule 4.9, Item 3 states that the Kestrune Engine License "covers the Kestrune proprietary game engine, runtime and editor tools, on which each Emberfall Saga title and the unreleased sequel build are developed." Section 15.3 of that license provides that Seller "may not assign or transfer the license, or any rights under it, without the prior written consent of Kestrune," and that "[a]ny assignment made without that consent is void." The restriction expressly applies to "any assignment by operation of law, merger or change of control, or in connection with a sale of all or substantially all of the assets of the licensed business."

The Kestrune Engine License is an Assigned Contract (Schedule 2.1(c), Item 3). However, Kestrune's consent is NOT listed on Schedule 4.5 (Required Consents), which lists only Halvern Console Network and Ostrava Entertainment Systems.

**Why it matters:**

- Because Kestrune's consent is not on Schedule 4.5, it is not a closing condition under APA § 7.2(d). Buyer could close without it.
- But the license provides that assignment without consent is *void*. If the assignment of the Kestrune Engine License is void, Buyer does not obtain the engine license. The engine underlies all released titles and the unreleased sequel.
- The APA § 4.3(c) representation (no conflicts with Assigned Contracts) is qualified by "except as set forth on Schedule 4.5"—so the Kestrune restriction is not disclosed as an exception, meaning Seller's rep may be inaccurate. But this is a General Rep, subject to the cap and the escrow-as-sole-remedy provision.
- Practically, if the license is void, Buyer's entire product line (including the sequel) loses its engine license. This goes to the heart of the asset's value.

**Recommendation:** We must add Kestrune's consent to Schedule 4.5 as a Required Consent and make it a condition to closing under § 7.2(d). We should also request that Seller confirm in writing whether it has already sought or received Kestrune's consent. If Kestrune has not been approached, we need to understand the likelihood and timing of obtaining consent before we can be comfortable with the deal. This is a potential deal-breaker if consent is refused.

### 4. Escrow Release at Six Months vs. Eighteen-Month Survival—Structural Gap in Buyer's Remedies

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 4.2 (Release Date = 6 months post-Closing); APA § 9.1 (General Reps survive 18 months); APA § 9.6 (escrow is "sole and exclusive source of recovery" for General Rep breaches).

**Issue:** The Escrow Agreement releases all remaining Indemnity Escrow Funds to Seller six months after the Closing Date, less any Pending Claim Amount. APA § 9.6 provides that the escrow is the "sole and exclusive source of recovery" for Buyer's Losses under § 9.2(a) arising from General Representation breaches (except Fraud). General Representations survive for 18 months under § 9.1.

This creates a 12-month gap (months 7 through 18) during which:
- A General Rep may be breached (the rep survives).
- Buyer may discover the breach.
- But the escrow has already been released to Seller.
- And § 9.6 bars Buyer from recovering directly from Seller because the escrow was the "sole and exclusive source."

**Why it matters:** Many of the most valuable reps for a game studio acquisition are General Reps: IP non-infringement (§ 4.12(c)), open-source compliance (§ 4.12(f)), employee IP assignments (§ 4.12(d)), platform compliance (§ 4.13), financial information accuracy (§ 4.4), and compliance with laws including data privacy and loot-box regulations (§ 4.8). Issues under these reps often surface only after the first product cycle post-closing (e.g., a platform holder audit, a user data breach, an open-source contamination discovered during a sequel build). Six months is very short for that to happen.

**Recommendation:** We should negotiate to extend the Release Date to at least 18 months (matching the survival period), or alternatively amend APA § 9.6 to permit direct recovery from Seller for General Rep breaches that are discovered after the escrow is released. A middle ground would be to extend the Release Date to 12 months and add a provision that Buyer may recover directly from Seller for General Rep breaches discovered after the Release Date but within the survival period, up to the remaining cap.

### 5. IP Assignment Agreement Scope Is Narrower Than the APA's Purchased IP Definition

**Where:** IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 and Exhibit A; APA § 2.1(b).

**Issue:** The IP Assignment Agreement defines "Assigned IP" as IP "to the extent registered or applied-for and set forth on Exhibit A." Exhibit A lists four trademark registrations/applications and two copyright registrations. It does not include:

- Unregistered copyrights in source code, art assets, audio, animations, models, design documents, or the unreleased sequel (APA § 2.1(b)(ii)–(v));
- Domain names (emberfallsaga.com, emberfall.gg) (APA § 2.1(b)(vi));
- Social media accounts (APA § 2.1(b)(vi));
- Trade secrets or know-how.

The APA § 2.1(b) is the operative transfer provision and covers all "Purchased IP." The IP Assignment Agreement is the recordation instrument (Recital C). However, if a dispute arises over whether unregistered IP was properly assigned, the narrow language of the IP Assignment Agreement could be cited by Seller to argue that only Exhibit A items were assigned under that instrument.

**Why it matters:** The soundtrack/score issue (see Issue 6 below) illustrates the risk. The score is not a separately registered copyright on Exhibit A. If Orvenna's claim succeeds, the question of whether the score was properly assigned to Buyer becomes critical, and the IP Assignment Agreement's limited scope does not help.

**Recommendation:** Add a provision to the IP Assignment Agreement (or a side certificate) confirming that the assignment in APA § 2.1(b) encompasses all Purchased IP, whether or not registered, and that the IP Assignment Agreement is delivered for recordation purposes only and does not limit the scope of the APA assignment. Alternatively, add a catch-all paragraph to Exhibit A covering all unregistered copyrights, domain names, and social media accounts.

### 6. Orvenna Sound Collective Claim—Soundtrack Ownership at Risk

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, Part 3 (Supplement No. 1, §§ 4.2–4.4); Schedule 4.9, Item 8 (as supplemented); IP Assignment Agreement, Exhibit A, Part 2; APA § 4.12(d).

**Issue:** Orvenna Sound Collective asserts copyright ownership in "certain compositions and sound recordings contained in the Emberfall Saga soundtrack, including the main theme and the in-game combat suite," based on an "alleged 2019 co-writing arrangement with members of Hallorin Bridge." The demand letter (received September 28, 2026) requires cessation of distribution within 30 days and threatens injunctive relief.

The original Schedule 4.9, Item 8 represented that "the score is a work made for hire, and all copyrights in the score are owned by Seller." The IP Assignment Agreement Exhibit A does not list a separate copyright registration for the score. APA § 4.12(d) represents that all contributors "have executed a written agreement presently assigning to Seller that individual's rights."

**Why it matters:** If Orvenna's claim has merit, a portion of the soundtrack—central to the game's identity—may not be validly owned by Seller and therefore not validly assigned to Buyer. The 30-day demand deadline (from September 28) would have expired around October 28, 2026. If Orvenna has filed for injunctive relief, the games could be taken off platforms. This is an existential threat to the franchise.

Additionally, as noted in Issue 2, if Supplement No. 1 is deemed accepted, Buyer loses its indemnification for this matter.

**Recommendation:**
- Determine immediately whether Orvenna has filed suit or a TRO motion (the 30-day deadline has likely passed).
- Request that Seller produce the Hallorin Bridge Music Composition and License Agreement and any underlying co-writing or collaboration agreements from 2019.
- If the objection to Supplement No. 1 is unsustainable (Issue 2), negotiate a specific indemnity for the Orvenna matter, uncapped or with a higher cap, or a purchase price reduction reflecting the risk.
- Consider making resolution or indemnification of the Orvenna claim a condition to closing.

### 7. Key Employee Agreement—California Governing Law and Non-Compete Enforceability

**Where:** Key Employee Agreement § 9.1 (governing law: California; venue: Los Angeles); § 6.1 (non-compete, 24 months, US/Canada/UK/EU); APA § 6.10 (requires non-compete and non-solicit covenants); APA § 11.6 (governing law: Delaware).

**Issue:** The Key Employee Agreement is governed by California law and must be litigated in Los Angeles. It contains a 24-month non-compete covering "premium action role-playing or fantasy adventure video games for console or PC platforms" across four jurisdictions. California has a well-established public policy against post-employment non-compete agreements. The choice of California governing law for an agreement whose central protective purpose is a non-compete creates significant enforceability risk.

The APA (Delaware law) § 6.10 requires the Key Employee Agreement to include these covenants and acknowledges they are "necessary to protect the goodwill of the Business acquired by Buyer." If the covenants are unenforceable under California law, the APA's stated purpose for requiring them is defeated.

**Why it matters:** Fenwright is the Studio Creative Director and a 34% member of Seller. She is the single most important person for the creative continuity of the franchise. If the non-compete is unenforceable, she could leave for a direct competitor after the Closing (or even during employment, if she resigns) without meaningful restriction. The 24-month retention bonus structure (§ 3.3) provides some economic lock-in, but it is back-ended (first installment at year 1, second at year 2) and is forfeited only upon "material breach" of § 6 or § 7 (§ 8.2)—which presupposes those sections are enforceable.

**Recommendation:**
- Discuss with California employment counsel the enforceability of the non-compete as drafted under current California law.
- If unenforceable, consider: (a) changing the governing law to Texas (Fenwright is an Austin resident) or Delaware (matching the APA), subject to enforceability analysis in the chosen jurisdiction; (b) replacing the non-compete with a narrower non-solicitation of players/users or a project-specific non-compete that may survive under California law; or (c) increasing the retention bonus and adding a clawback provision tied to competitive employment, structured as a liquidated damages clause rather than a non-compete.
- Coordinate with the APA: if the Key Employee Agreement covenants are modified, confirm that APA § 6.10 is conformed.

The security deposit is already due. The TI allowance is forfeited if not used by December 31, 2027, creating pressure to proceed with buildout even if the deal is in limbo.

### 9. Escrow Tax Allocation—Buyer Taxed on Earnings

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 7.1.

**Issue:** "Buyer is treated as the owner of the Indemnity Escrow Funds, and all earnings on them are allocated to Buyer until disbursed." The funds are invested in a money market fund (§ 3.1).

**Why it matters:** Buyer will receive a 1099 for interest/earnings on the $4.8M escrow and must include it in income, even though the funds are really Seller's (held as security for Seller's indemnity obligations). If the funds are ultimately disbursed to Seller, Buyer may need to seek a refund or adjustment. This is a minor administrative and economic issue but should be flagged to the client's tax team.

**Recommendation:** Confirm with tax counsel whether a different allocation is achievable (e.g., treating the escrow as a constructive trust for Seller's benefit) or whether Buyer should build in a small tax cost for the earnings.

### 10. APA § 6.10 and Member Consent—Fenwright's Dual Role

**Where:** APA § 6.10; Member Consent §§ 1.6, 4.2; Key Employee Agreement (entire).

**Issue:** Fenwright is simultaneously (a) a 34% member of Seller (receiving a share of the $48M Purchase Price through her membership interest) and (b) the Key Employee who must sign an employment agreement with Buyer as a condition to closing. The Member Consent (§ 1.6) discloses this arrangement, and § 4.2 authorizes Seller to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to cause her to sign.

However, there is no consequence specified if she refuses to sign. APA § 7.2(f) makes delivery of the Key Employee Agreement a condition to Buyer's closing obligation, so Buyer could walk. But Seller has no affirmative obligation to *ensure* she signs—only to use "commercially reasonable efforts."

**Why it matters:** If Fenwright leverages her position (she gets value from the deal as a member regardless of whether she signs the employment agreement) to extract better terms or simply refuse, the deal cannot close. Buyer has already committed to the lease (Issue 8) and may have other time-sensitive obligations.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm that Fenwright has indicated she will sign. If not, consider making her execution a condition on *Seller's* obligation to close (not just Buyer's), with a specific consequence (e.g., Seller's breach).
- The equity grant in the Key Employee Agreement (§ 3.4) is "subject to approval by the Company's Board of Directors." Confirm that Board approval is in hand or scheduled before closing, to avoid a last-minute gap.
