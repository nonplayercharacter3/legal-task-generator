## Summary

I reviewed all four documents in the matter folder. I identified twelve issues affecting Rilux, ranging from a fundamentally one-sided indemnity in the Bill of Sale to a missing closing condition on the Company's most critical supply contract. The three most consequential items are: (1) the Bill of Sale indemnity runs in the wrong direction and is unlimited; (2) the raw milk supply consent is absent from the Required Consents schedule; and (3) the working capital dispute appears time-barred under the SPA's plain terms. Details and recommended fixes follow in order of significance.

## Issue 1 — Bill of Sale Indemnity Runs to Seller, Not Buyer (Critical)

**Document / Section:** Bill of Sale, § 5.1–5.2

**Problem:** Section 5.1 provides that *the Company* (i.e., Rilux's subsidiary) "shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless Seller" for "any and all Liabilities arising out of or relating to the Conveyed Assets or their ownership, operation, maintenance or use," including product contamination, tanker accidents, and regulatory compliance. Section 5.2 makes this indemnity unlimited in amount. The defined term "Liabilities" (§ 1.3) sweeps in claims "whether known or unknown, accrued or unaccrued, absolute or contingent, and whether in contract, tort, strict liability or otherwise," and expressly covers liabilities "whether arising before, on or after the date hereof."

**Why it matters to Rilux:** This inverts the normal allocation of risk in an asset transfer. Rilux is buying the Company, which is receiving equipment from Seller. Yet the Company must indemnify Seller for everything—past and future—related to that equipment. If the HTST pasteurizer (Schedule A, Part I, item 1) has a latent defect that causes a contamination event, or if a bulk milk tanker (Part II) is involved in a pre-closing accident that surfaces post-closing, the Company's (and therefore Rilux's) obligation is to make Seller whole, with no cap. Meanwhile, Seller's only warranty is title (§ 3.1); the assets are otherwise conveyed "AS IS, WHERE IS" (§ 3.2).

**Recommended fix:**

- Strike § 5.1 in its entirety and replace with a seller-side indemnity: *"Seller shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless the Company and its Affiliates from and against any and all Liabilities arising out of or relating to the Conveyed Assets to the extent such Liabilities result from (a) any defect in Seller's title, (b) any condition of the Conveyed Assets existing prior to the Closing Date, or (c) any act or omission of Seller or its Affiliates prior to the Closing Date."*
- If Seller resists a full pre-closing indemnity, negotiate at minimum: (i) a carve-out so the Company's indemnity to Seller applies only to post-Closing liabilities; (ii) a cap tied to the fair market value of the Conveyed Assets; and (iii) a mutual waiver of subrogation to the extent both parties are covered under the same insurance policies.
- Raise this with Glegond's counsel as a condition to Rilux's sign-off on the deal package. As drafted, this section is not acceptable.

## Issue 2 — Raw Milk Supply Consent Missing from Required Consents (Critical)

**Document / Section:** SPA, Schedule 4.5(a) (item 1) vs. Schedule 7.2(d)

**Problem:** Schedule 4.5(a) discloses that the Raw Organic Milk Supply Agreement with Ashbourne Valley Organic Producers Cooperative (term through February 28, 2029) requires the Cooperative's prior written consent for any change of control, including a transfer of a majority of voting stock (§ 14.2 of that agreement). Without consent, the Cooperative may terminate on 60 days' written notice. However, Schedule 7.2(d)—which defines the "Required Consents" that are a condition to Buyer's closing obligation under § 7.2(d)—lists only the landlord's consent (Lamoille Industrial Properties). The Cooperative's consent is absent.

**Why it matters to Rilux:** The raw milk supply is the lifeblood of the Business. The SPA's "Business" definition (§ 1.1) is "the processing, packaging, marketing and sale of organic fluid milk, cream and yogurt products." Without the milk, there is no business. Because the Cooperative's consent is not a Required Consent, its absence does not give Rilux the right to walk away at closing under § 7.2(d). Post-closing, if the Cooperative terminates, Rilux has no specific indemnity trigger under the SPA (the rep in § 4.5(a) only says the contract is "valid, binding and in full force and effect"—it does not represent that consent was obtained or that no termination right exists). The 6-month survival period (§ 8.1) may expire before a 60-day termination notice is even given.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Immediate:** Confirm whether the Cooperative's consent was in fact obtained before or at Closing. If it was, request a copy and add it to the closing file. If it was not, this is an urgent gap.
- **If consent was not obtained:** Raise with Glegond's counsel that the omission from Schedule 7.2(d) appears to be an error given the materiality of the contract and the express consent requirement disclosed in Schedule 4.5(a). Request either (a) delivery of the Cooperative's written consent as a post-closing cure, or (b) a specific indemnity from Seller for losses arising from termination of the Raw Organic Milk Supply Agreement, with survival extended to the earlier of (i) the expiration of the contract term (February 28, 2029) or (ii) 18 months post-Closing.
- **Going forward:** In any amendment or side letter, add the Cooperative's consent to Schedule 7.2(d) for the record, and add a specific rep in § 4.5(a) that all change-of-control consents required under Material Contracts have been obtained or are not required.

## Issue 3 — Working Capital Dispute Likely Time-Barred (High)

**Document / Section:** SPA, § 2.4(a)–(b); IP Assignment Agreement, Recital C

**Problem:** Under § 2.4(a), Seller delivered the Closing Statement on April 30, 2026. Section 2.4(b) requires Buyer to deliver a Notice of Disagreement "within thirty (30) days after delivery of the Closing Statement." Thirty days from April 30 is May 30, 2026. Buyer delivered its Notice on June 12, 2026—13 days late. Seller has asserted (per Recital C of the IP Assignment) that the Notice was untimely and the Closing Statement is final and binding. Buyer disputes this and reserves its rights.

**Why it matters to Rilux:** If the Notice is deemed untimely, § 2.4(b) provides that "the Closing Statement shall be final and binding on the Parties." Rilux would lose the ability to recover any working capital shortfall. Given the $6.2M Target Working Capital and the size of the deal, even a modest disagreement (e.g., $500K–$1M) is material.

**Open questions / facts needed:**

- Was the Closing Statement actually *delivered* on April 30, or was it merely *sent*? If delivery (in the § 11.2 sense—personal delivery, overnight courier, or email with confirmation) occurred later, the 30-day clock may not have started on April 30.
- Did Seller's Closing Statement include "reasonable supporting detail" as required by § 2.3 (by analogy) and § 2.4(a)? If the statement was deficient, there may be an argument that the 30-day period did not begin to run until a conforming statement was delivered.
- Is there any course of dealing or waiver argument? For example, did Seller discuss the disputed items with Buyer between May 30 and June 12 in a manner that could constitute a waiver of the strict deadline?
- What is the dollar amount in dispute? This bears on proportionality and any equitable argument.

**Recommended fix:**

- Instruct deal team to pull the transmission records for the April 30 Closing Statement delivery and all communications between May 30 and June 12.
- Prepare a position paper for the next call with Glegond's counsel arguing: (a) the actual delivery date, (b) any deficiency in the Closing Statement, and/or (c) equitable waiver/tolling based on Seller's conduct.
- If the position is weak, consider whether a negotiated resolution (e.g., independent accountant determination under § 2.4(c) limited to the disputed items) is preferable to litigating the timeliness question. The § 2.4(c) mechanism requires a "timely" Notice of Disagreement as a precondition, so this is not available unless the timeliness issue is resolved in Buyer's favor.
- Flag to the client that this dispute is live and that the IP Assignment's § 8.1–8.2 (reservation of rights / no set-off) confirms neither side has conceded.

## Issue 4 — IP License to Seller Is Transferable and Broadly Scoped (High)

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement, § 4.1, § 1.2 ("Retained Product Lines"), § 4.2

**Problem:** The Company grants Seller a "non-exclusive, **transferable**, royalty-free license" to use SORRELFIELD Marks 1–3 and the core yogurt formulations (plain whole-milk and Greek-style strained) in connection with "Retained Product Lines," defined as "dairy products manufactured, marketed or sold by Seller or its Affiliates as of the Effective Date, **together with reasonable line extensions of those products**."

**Why it matters to Rilux:**

- *Transferability:* Unlike the sublicensing right in § 4.2 (limited to Affiliates and contract manufacturers/co-packers), the underlying license is "transferable." This could permit Seller to assign the license to a non-Affiliate third party, potentially a competitor, without Company consent.
- *Scope of "Retained Product Lines":* The "reasonable line extensions" language is open-ended. If Seller's retained conventional dairy operations include, say, a flavored yogurt line, a "reasonable line extension" could arguably encompass organic flavored yogurts using the licensed SF-C3 or SF-G2 culture blends under the SORRELFIELD mark.
- *Non-compete gap:* SPA § 6.8 restricts Seller from processing or selling "organic fluid milk or organic yogurt" for 3 years. But the IP license permits Seller to use SORRELFIELD marks and yogurt formulations for "Retained Product Lines." If Seller's retained lines include products that are not strictly "organic fluid milk" or "organic yogurt" (e.g., organic cream, organic half-and-half, or conventional yogurt sold under the SORRELFIELD mark), the non-compete may not apply while the IP license does. The two provisions need to be read together, and the IP license appears broader.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend § 4.1 to delete "transferable" or to limit transferability to the same scope as sublicensing in § 4.2 (Affiliates and contract manufacturers/co-packers only, with Company consent for any other transfer).
- Tighten the "Retained Product Lines" definition: replace "reasonable line extensions" with a specific, enumerated list of SKUs or product categories agreed by the Parties, or at minimum add a proviso that line extensions must not include products bearing the SORRELFIELD mark in the organic fluid milk or organic yogurt categories (to align with the SPA non-compete).
- Add a cross-reference in § 4.1: *"Nothing in this Section 4 shall permit Seller to use the Marks in a manner that would violate Section 6.8 of the Purchase Agreement."*
- Raise with Glegond's counsel before the IP Assignment is finalized.

## Issue 5 — Six-Month Survival Period Is Inadequate (Moderate-High)

**Document / Section:** SPA, § 8.1

**Problem:** All representations and warranties (including the organic certification rep in § 4.8, the tax rep in § 4.9, and the IP rep in § 4.7) survive only six months post-Closing—i.e., until October 1, 2026. The parties intend this to replace the applicable statute of limitations (§ 8.1, last sentence).

**Why it matters to Rilux:**

- *Organic certification:* A USDA NOP certification issue (e.g., a finding of non-compliance, a suspension, or a discovery that the certifying agent's accreditation lapsed) could surface after the annual inspection cycle, which may fall after October 2026. Loss of organic status would be existential to the Business.
- *Taxes:* § 4.9 represents that all returns are filed and all taxes paid. A state or federal tax audit can be opened well after 6 months. The § 8.2(c) indemnity for Pre-Closing Taxes is subject to the survival period, meaning a Claim Notice must be delivered before October 1, 2026.
- *IP:* The IP Assignment is not executed until June 22, 2026. If there is a defect in the assignment (e.g., an undisclosed license, a prior claimant), Rilux may not discover it before the survival period expires.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a tiered survival structure: general reps survive 18 months; fundamental reps (organization, authority, capitalization) survive until the applicable statute of limitations; tax reps (§ 4.9) survive until the later of (a) 18 months post-Closing or (b) 30 days after the applicable statute of limitations for the relevant tax period expires; organic certification rep (§ 4.8) survives 24 months (to cover at least one full certification cycle).
- If Seller resists, at minimum extend § 4.8 and § 4.9 to 18 months and add a specific indemnity for loss of organic certification that is not subject to the general cap in § 8.6(b).

## Issue 6 — No Indemnification Mechanism in IP Assignment for Seller Rep Breaches (Moderate)

**Document / Section:** IP Assignment Agreement, § 3 (reps) vs. absence of an indemnification article

**Problem:** Seller makes four representations in § 3 (authority, no prior licenses, no infringement, formulation completeness). However, the IP Assignment contains no indemnification section. The SPA's indemnification article (Article VIII) covers claims "arising out of this Agreement" (the SPA) and the exclusive remedy clause (§ 8.7) is limited to the SPA. It is unclear whether a breach of an IP Assignment rep gives rise to a claim under SPA § 8.2(a) (which covers "any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty of Seller in Article IV"—i.e., the SPA's Article IV, not the IP Assignment's § 3).

**Why it matters to Rilux:** If the formulations delivered under § 2.4 are incomplete (e.g., a critical culture blend parameter is missing), or if Seller previously granted a license to a third party (breach of § 3(b)), Rilux's remedy is uncertain. It may be limited to general contract damages under Delaware law, without the benefit of the SPA's procedural framework, and potentially subject to the SPA's exclusive remedy clause if a court reads it broadly.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add an indemnification section to the IP Assignment: *"Seller shall indemnify and hold harmless the Company and its Affiliates from and against all Losses arising out of or resulting from any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty of Seller in Section 3."*
- Clarify in the SPA (via amendment or side letter) that the exclusive remedy in § 8.7 does not limit remedies available under the Ancillary Agreements, or alternatively, that breaches of Ancillary Agreement reps are deemed breaches of SPA § 4.7 and are covered by § 8.2(a).
- Confirm the survival period for IP Assignment reps (currently silent—arguably governed by the SPA's § 8.1 at 6 months, which is too short given the § 2.4 delivery deadline of 10 business days post-Effective Date).

## Issue 7 — Bill of Sale "AS IS" With No Condition Warranty (Moderate)

**Document / Section:** Bill of Sale, § 3.2

**Problem:** The Conveyed Assets (bottling line equipment and four bulk milk tankers) are conveyed "AS IS, WHERE IS" with an express disclaimer of merchantability and fitness. Seller's only warranty is title (§ 3.1).

**Why it matters to Rilux:** The pasteurizer, fillers, homogenizer, and CIP system are the core processing assets. If any unit has a latent defect (e.g., a cracked heat exchanger plate, a worn seal on the flow diversion valve), the Company bears the cost of repair or replacement with no recourse against Seller. For a dairy processor, equipment failure can also trigger a pasteurization violation under the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance, with regulatory and product-loss consequences.

**Recommended fix:**

- At minimum, add a limited warranty: *"Seller represents and warrants that, as of the Closing Date, the Conveyed Assets are in good working order and repair, ordinary wear and tear excepted, and are free from known material defects."*
- If Seller will not provide a condition warranty, negotiate a price credit or escrow for a defined period (e.g., 90 days) to cover latent defects discovered promptly post-closing.
- Confirm that the Company's commercial property / equipment breakdown policy (Lakeshore, per the insurance letter) covers mechanical breakdown of the Conveyed Assets, and that the policy was bound with knowledge of the equipment's age and condition.

## Issue 8 — Indemnification Basket and Cap (Moderate)

**Document / Section:** SPA, § 8.6(a)–(b)

**Problem:** Seller's indemnification exposure is subject to a deductible (basket) of $845,000 (§ 8.6(a))—Rilux absorbs the first $845K of losses—and a cap of $8,450,000 (10% of the Base Purchase Price) (§ 8.6(b)).

**Why it matters to Rilux:** For an $84.5M acquisition of a business whose value is heavily dependent on organic certification, the milk supply contract, and the IP, a 10% cap may be insufficient if a fundamental issue emerges (e.g., loss of organic status, termination of the milk supply). The $845K basket means that multiple smaller issues (each below the basket) are individually unrecoverable, and even a single $700K loss is entirely absorbed by Rilux.

**Recommended fix:**

- Negotiate a "tipping" basket rather than a "deductible" basket: once aggregate losses exceed $845K, Seller is liable for the *entire* amount from dollar one, not just the excess.
- Carve out fundamental reps (organization, authority, capitalization) and the organic certification rep from both the basket and the cap.
- If the cap cannot be raised, at minimum exclude Pre-Closing Taxes (§ 8.2(c)) and fraud from the cap.

## Issue 9 — IP Assignment Timing Gap (Moderate-Low)

**Document / Section:** SPA, § 6.9; IP Assignment, § 2.1; SPA, § 4.7

**Problem:** SPA § 6.9 requires Seller to execute the IP Assignment "following the Closing." The IP Assignment is dated June 22, 2026—82 days after the April 1, 2026 Closing. Section 2.1 provides the assignment is "effective as of the Closing Date for all purposes," which cures the title gap retroactively. However, during the 82-day interval, the record owner of all five Marks (per Schedule 1, Part I) was Glegond Dairy Group, LLC, not the Company. Any third-party infringement during that window is technically actionable by Seller, not the Company, although § 1.2 (Assigned IP definition) purports to assign "all rights to sue for and recover damages for past, present and future infringement."

**Why it matters to Rilux:** If a competitor launched a confusingly similar mark between April 1 and June 22, the Company's standing to sue may be challenged. The retroactive assignment should cover this, but it creates unnecessary litigation risk.

**Recommended fix:**

- Confirm in writing (side letter or amendment) that Seller's execution of the IP Assignment constitutes an acknowledgment that all infringement claims accruing before the Effective Date are assigned to the Company, and that Seller will not assert any independent claim for pre-June 22 infringement.
- Going forward, if this deal structure is repeated, require the IP Assignment to be executed *at* Closing (as a closing deliverable under § 3.2) rather than post-closing.

## Issue 10 — Non-Compete Scope May Not Cover All Company Products (Low-Moderate)

**Document / Section:** SPA, § 6.8; § 1.1 ("Business")

**Problem:** The non-compete prohibits Seller from processing or selling "organic fluid milk or organic yogurt" in the United States for 3 years. However, the "Business" definition in § 1.1 includes "organic fluid milk, **cream** and yogurt products." Organic cream and half-and-half are not covered by the non-compete. If Seller's retained conventional operations include a cream line that could be converted to organic, Seller could arguably enter the organic cream market without violating § 6.8.

**Why it matters to Rilux:** The Company produces organic heavy cream and half-and-half (IP Assignment, Schedule 1, Part II, item 2). If Seller launches a competing organic cream product, Rilux has no contractual restriction.

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend § 6.8 to cover "organic fluid milk, organic cream, organic half-and-half, or organic yogurt" to align with the Business definition.
- Alternatively, broaden to "any organic dairy product" to capture the full scope of the Company's product line.

**Document / Section:** Insurance Certificate Cover Letter, § 5.2

**Problem:** The broker letter (April 8, 2026) states that complete policy copies, including all endorsements, will be delivered "within approximately thirty (30) days"—i.e., by approximately May 8, 2026. We have only the certificates, which "conferr [no] rights" and "do[] not amend, extend or alter the coverage" (§ 5.1).

**Recommended fix:**

- Verify that the PCR policy's $5M limit is adequate given the Company's production volume and the potential cost of a multi-state recall of organic dairy products.

## Issue 12 — Power of Attorney in Bill of Sale (Low — Confirm Mechanics)

**Document / Section:** Bill of Sale, § 4.2

**Problem:** Seller appoints the Company as its attorney-in-fact to (a) demand and receive Conveyed Assets and (b) endorse title documents in Seller's name. The POA is "coupled with an interest," which makes it irrevocable. This is standard and favorable to the Company. However, we should confirm that the Vermont DMV title transfers for the four tankers (Bill of Sale, Schedule A, Part II) were actually completed using this mechanism, and that the Company is now the registered owner of record.

**Recommended fix:**

- Request confirmation from the Company's operations team that all four tankers have been retitled in the Company's name with the Vermont DMV.
- If any title transfer is pending, flag as a post-closing action item with a deadline.
