## Issues Ranked by Risk to Breidoun

### 1. IP Assignment Agreement § 8.3 Overrides the SPA's IP Carve-Out from the Indemnification Cap

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.5(b)(iii): The Cap "does not apply to … (iii) any breach of the representations and warranties in Section 4.12 (Intellectual Property)."
- SPA § 9.5(b) (last sentence): "Seller's aggregate liability for the matters in clauses (ii) and (iii) shall not exceed the Purchase Price."
- IP Assignment Agreement § 8.3: "Assignor's aggregate liability for Losses arising out of any breach of this Agreement **or of the representations and warranties in Section 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement**, whether claimed under this Agreement or under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **shall be subject to the Cap set forth in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement, notwithstanding Section 9.5(b) of the Purchase Agreement**."
- SPA § 11.10 (Order of Precedence): "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, the Ancillary Agreement controls as to its subject matter."

**Why it matters:** The SPA deliberately carved IP out of the 10% Cap and set a separate ceiling of 100% of the Purchase Price for IP and Fundamental Rep claims. The IP Assignment Agreement, executed *after* Closing, re-imposes the 10% Cap on all IP-related claims, including breaches of SPA § 4.12, and explicitly states it does so "notwithstanding Section 9.5(b)." Because the IP Assignment Agreement is an Ancillary Agreement (SPA § 1.1) and SPA § 11.10 gives it precedence "as to its subject matter," a court would likely hold that § 8.3 controls. The practical effect is that Breidoun's maximum recovery for any IP problem—including a scenario in which Seller or an Affiliate actually owned the Recipes and Specifications or the Ostrander Marks—is **10% of the Purchase Price** rather than 100%.

This is particularly acute because the very reason the IP Assignment Agreement was triggered is that "Buyer determines that Seller or any of its Affiliates holds or may hold" IP (SPA § 6.10). If that determination turns out to reflect a real breach of SPA § 4.12(b), Breidoun's recovery is capped at the lowest possible level.

**Recommended fix:**
- If the IP Assignment Agreement has not yet been fully performed (e.g., the 10-business-day delivery of Recipes under § 3.1 is still pending), negotiate an amendment to § 8.3 to conform to SPA § 9.5(b)(iii). Proposed language:

  > "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Assignor's liability for Losses arising out of any breach of the representations and warranties in Section 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement shall be subject to the limitations set forth in Section 9.5(b) of the Purchase Agreement and shall not be subject to the Cap in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement."

- If the agreement is already fully performed and Seller will not agree to an amendment, document the issue for the file and assess whether the 10% Cap is acceptable given the current state of the IP (i.e., whether the confirmatory assignment has resolved the underlying concern).

**Open question:** What specifically prompted Buyer to invoke § 6.10? Was it a discovered registration in Seller's name, a shared domain, a former employee's portfolio, or a precautionary measure? The answer affects both the size of the potential claim and the leverage we have to negotiate.

### 2. Buyer's Notice of Disagreement on Working Capital Was Delivered 16 Days Late; Seller Has Rejected It

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 2.4(a): Seller delivered the Closing Statement within 45 days after Closing.
- SPA § 2.4(b): Buyer must deliver a Notice of Disagreement "within 30 days after delivery of the Closing Statement." If Buyer does not, "the Closing Statement and Seller's calculation of Closing Working Capital shall be final and binding on the Parties."
- IP Assignment Agreement, Recital C: "Seller delivered the Closing Statement to Buyer on **August 3, 2026**."
- IP Assignment Agreement, Recital D: "Buyer delivered a Notice of Disagreement to Seller on **September 18, 2026**. Seller has notified Buyer that it rejects the Notice of Disagreement as untimely."

**Why it matters:** Thirty days after August 3, 2026 is **September 2, 2026**. Buyer's notice was delivered on September 18—16 days late. Under the plain text of SPA § 2.4(b), the Closing Statement is now "final and binding." If Seller's estimate of Closing Working Capital was higher than the true figure, Breidoun overpaid at Closing (SPA § 2.3) and may be unable to recover the difference. The dollar exposure depends on the magnitude of the discrepancy, which we cannot assess without the Closing Statement and the underlying workpapers.

Recital D of the IP Assignment Agreement confirms that Seller has affirmatively rejected the notice and that Buyer "expressly reserves its position." That reservation is helpful as a matter of record but does not cure the timing defect.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Immediate:** Obtain the Closing Statement, the underlying schedule, and the Target Working Capital figure from Schedule 1.1(b) of the SPA. Quantify the dollar delta between Seller's number and what we believe is correct.
- **Legal argument:** Assess whether there is a basis to argue the deadline was not triggered as stated. Possible angles:
  - Did Seller's Closing Statement include "reasonable supporting detail" as required by § 2.4(a)? If the statement was deficient, one could argue the 30-day clock did not begin to run until a compliant statement was delivered. This is a fact-intensive argument.
  - Was there any communication between the parties between August 3 and September 18 that could constitute a waiver or course of performance? Check the email record.
  - SPA § 11.5 requires waivers to be in writing. Seller's rejection letter is not a waiver. But if Seller engaged in substantive discussion of the disputed items before raising the timeliness objection, there may be an estoppel or waiver argument.
- **Practical:** If the dollar amount is modest relative to the Purchase Price, the cost of litigating the timeliness issue may not be justified. If it is material, we should consider whether to raise the issue in the context of the IP Assignment Agreement negotiations (see Issue 1 above) as part of a global resolution.

**Open question:** What is the dollar amount in dispute? What was the Target Working Capital (Schedule 1.1(b))? What was Seller's estimate? We need the Closing Statement to proceed.

### 3. Governing Law and Forum Conflict Between the SPA and the IP Assignment Agreement

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 11.8: New York law; exclusive jurisdiction in New York County state and federal courts.
- IP Assignment Agreement § 10.1: **Delaware** law.
- IP Assignment Agreement § 10.2: Exclusive jurisdiction in the **Delaware Court of Chancery** (or Superior Court / federal District Court if Chancery lacks subject-matter jurisdiction).

**Why it matters:** Under SPA § 11.10, the IP Assignment Agreement controls "as to its subject matter." IP disputes are squarely within its subject matter. The practical consequences of a Delaware governing law and forum include:

- Delaware courts may interpret indemnification caps, baskets, and survival periods differently than New York courts. For example, Delaware's "manifest error" standard for expert determinations (SPA § 2.4(c)) and its treatment of "materiality" qualifiers in bring-down conditions (SPA § 7.1(a)) may diverge from New York precedent.
- The Delaware Court of Chancery is a court of limited jurisdiction (primarily equity and internal corporate affairs). A pure indemnification/breach-of-representation claim may not fall within Chancery's subject-matter jurisdiction, forcing a transfer to the Superior Court or federal court under the fallback in § 10.2. This creates procedural uncertainty and potential delay.
- Litigating in Delaware is more expensive and logistically burdensome for a New York-based buyer than litigating in New York County.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation of the IP Assignment Agreement (see Issue 1), propose conforming § 10 to the SPA:

  > "This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of the State of New York, without regard to conflict of laws principles. Each Party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts located in New York County, New York."

- If Seller resists, at minimum argue that the fallback jurisdiction (Delaware Superior Court or D. Del.) should be the primary forum, not Chancery, to avoid subject-matter-jurisdiction disputes.

### 4. Key Employee Agreement: "Business" Defined More Narrowly Than in the SPA

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1: "Business" means "inflight catering, **galley provisioning** and **crew meal services** for regional air carriers."
- Key Employee Agreement § 1: "Business" means "the preparation of **hot inflight meals** for regional air carriers."

**Why it matters:** The non-compete in Key Employee Agreement § 7.1 restricts Solvig only from the narrower "Business." If the Company's operations include galley provisioning (loading galleys with supplies, which is distinct from preparing hot meals) or crew meal services, Solvig could leave and perform those functions for a competitor without breaching § 7.1. Given that the SPA Recital B describes the Company as operating "commissaries that supply inflight meals, **galley provisioning** and **crew meals**," the narrower definition in the Key Employee Agreement appears to be a drafting error that creates a gap in the restrictive covenant.

The non-solicitation covenants (§§ 7.2, 7.3) are not limited by the "Business" definition in the same way—they refer to "products or services of the Business" in § 7.2(a), which would inherit the narrow definition.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend Key Employee Agreement § 1 to conform the "Business" definition to the SPA:

  > "'Business' means inflight catering, galley provisioning and crew meal services for regional air carriers, as more particularly described in the Purchase Agreement."

- If Solvig resists (she may argue the narrower scope was a negotiated term), the retention payment structure (§ 4.3) gives the Company leverage: the second $45,000 installment is due at the one-year anniversary and requires continued employment. An amendment could be conditioned on her continued cooperation.

**Open question:** Does the Company actually perform galley provisioning and crew meal services as distinct from hot inflight meal preparation? If the operations are in fact limited to hot meal preparation, the definitional gap may be immaterial. We should confirm with the client.

### 5. Key Employee Agreement § 7.5: Consideration Acknowledgement References "Proceeds from the Purchase Agreement" That May Not Exist

**Documents and Sections:**
- Key Employee Agreement, Recital C: "Employee will benefit directly from the transactions under the Purchase Agreement."
- Key Employee Agreement § 7.5: "She acknowledges that … she has received adequate consideration for them, including **her proceeds from and benefits under the transactions contemplated by the Purchase Agreement** and the retention payment in Section 4.3."
- SPA: No provision in the SPA provides for any payment, equity, or other distribution to Solvig personally. She is not a party to the SPA.

**Why it matters:** New York courts enforce non-competes where the employee receives adequate consideration. Continued employment and a retention bonus are generally sufficient. However, § 7.5 specifically references "proceeds from … the Purchase Agreement" as part of the consideration. If Solvig receives no such proceeds (and the SPA contains no mechanism for her to do so), a court could find that the acknowledgement is factually incorrect and use that to undermine the enforceability of the restrictive covenants. This is a secondary risk—the retention payment and continued employment likely suffice on their own—but the erroneous reference creates an unnecessary vulnerability.

**Recommended fix:**
- Amend § 7.5 to remove the reference to "proceeds from … the Purchase Agreement" or to clarify that "benefits under the transactions" means the compensation and retention payment provided in this Agreement:

  > "…including the compensation, benefits and retention payment provided under this Agreement, which constitute adequate consideration for the restrictions herein."

**Open question:** Did Seller or the Company make any side payment or equity grant to Solvig in connection with the transaction that is not reflected in the SPA? If so, the recital and § 7.5 may be accurate and no fix is needed.

### 6. IP Assignment Agreement § 2.1 (Confirmation) Undermines the Basis for a Breach Claim Under SPA § 4.12(b)

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.12(b): "The Company is the sole and exclusive owner of all Company Intellectual Property … and **neither Seller nor any of its Affiliates holds any right, title or interest in any Company Intellectual Property**."
- IP Assignment Agreement § 2.1: "Assignor confirms that, as of the Closing, the Company was and is the sole and exclusive owner of the Assigned IP, and that **no member of the Assignor Group retains any right, title or interest in any Assigned IP**."
- IP Assignment Agreement § 2.2: "To the extent any member of the Assignor Group holds any right, title or interest in the Assigned IP, Assignor … irrevocably assigns …"

**Why it matters:** Section 2.1 is a present-tense confirmation that the SPA § 4.12(b) representation was true. If Seller later argues that § 2.1 establishes no breach of § 4.12(b) ever occurred, Buyer's indemnification claim under SPA § 9.2(a) for a rep breach would fail—leaving only the IP Assignment Agreement's own indemnity (§ 8.1), which is subject to the 10% Cap under § 8.3 (see Issue 1). The "to the extent" language in § 2.2 is a fallback that preserves the assignment mechanism, but it does not negate the confirmation in § 2.1.

This is a structural problem: the agreement simultaneously (a) confirms no IP was held by the Assignor Group (supporting Seller's position that no SPA rep was breached) and (b) assigns any IP that may have been held (acknowledging the possibility that one was). Seller can point to § 2.1 to defeat a breach-of-rep claim; Buyer can point to § 2.2 to enforce the assignment. But for *monetary* recovery, § 2.1 is a significant obstacle.

**Recommended fix:**
- In any renegotiation, add a clarifying sentence to § 2.1:

  > "This confirmation is made for the purpose of effectuating the assignment under Section 2.2 and shall not be construed as a representation that no breach of Section 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement has occurred or as a waiver of any claim under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement."

- Alternatively, add to § 8.1 a specific indemnity for breaches of SPA § 4.12 that is not subject to § 8.3's Cap override (which would also resolve Issue 1).

### 7. Linen and Cart Washing Services Agreement (Pellacourt): Limited but Non-Trivial Considerations

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.8: "Material Contract" includes "each contract with a supplier of food, **linen**, equipment or **galley services**."
- Pellacourt Agreement § 6.1: Initial term of three years from January 6, 2025 (expires January 6, 2028); automatic one-year renewals unless 90 days' non-renewal notice is given.
- Pellacourt Agreement § 6.4: Termination for convenience on 120 days' notice after the first anniversary (i.e., after January 6, 2026).
- Pellacourt Agreement § 11.2: Customer may assign without consent "to any affiliate or to any successor to all or substantially all of its business or the Facilities."
- Pellacourt Agreement § 3.3: The Customer's "air carrier customers" have inspection rights at the Vendor's plant.

**Why it matters for Breidoun:**

(a) **Material Contract / Disclosure Schedule.** The Pellacourt agreement is almost certainly a "Material Contract" under SPA § 4.8 (it is a contract with a supplier of linen and galley services). We need to confirm it was listed on the Disclosure Schedule at Section 4.8. If it was not, that is a breach of the § 4.8 representation, subject to the indemnification provisions. **Open question: Was the Pellacourt agreement listed on the Disclosure Schedule?**

(b) **No change-of-control consent required.** The agreement has no change-of-control or anti-assignment-triggered-by-ownership-change provision. Because this is a stock purchase, the contracting party (Ostrander Skyline Catering, Inc.) is unchanged. No consent from Pellacourt was needed for the transaction. This is a non-issue.

(c) **Auto-renewal deadline.** The current initial term expires January 6, 2028. To avoid a one-year auto-renewal, the Company must give notice by **October 8, 2027** (90 days prior). If Breidoun intends to replace Pellacourt or renegotiate, we should calendar this deadline. The termination-for-convenience right (§ 6.4, 120 days' notice) provides an earlier exit if needed.

(d) **Third-party inspection rights.** Section 3.3 grants the Company's air carrier customers the right to inspect Pellacourt's plant. This creates a web of obligations: if an air carrier customer inspects and finds a sanitation issue, the resulting claim could flow through the Company to Pellacourt under § 8.1, but the air carrier's right of access is contractual and not something the Company can unilaterally control. This is a minor operational consideration, not a deal risk.

(e) **Fees unknown.** Exhibit C references a rate card "agreed by the Parties on the Effective Date" but the actual rates are not in the document. We cannot confirm whether annual spend exceeds the $100,000 Material Contract threshold without the rate card and volume data. **Open question: What are the annual fees under the Pellacourt agreement?**

**Recommended fix:**
- Confirm the agreement is on the Disclosure Schedule. If not, assess whether to raise it as an indemnification claim (subject to the Cap and basket).
- Calendar the October 8, 2027 non-renewal deadline.
- No amendment to the agreement itself is warranted at this time.

### 8. SPA § 7.1(a): Materiality Qualifier on the Bring-Down Condition (Post-Closing Observation)

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 7.1(a): Buyer's Closing condition is that Seller's reps "are true and correct **in all material respects** as of the Closing Date."

**Why it matters:** The bring-down is qualified by materiality. This means that at Closing, even if a rep was technically false, Buyer could not walk unless the falsity was material. Closing occurred on July 1, 2026, so this is no longer a live issue for the transaction. However, it is relevant to the *indemnification* analysis: some courts have held that a materiality qualifier in a bring-down condition signals the parties' intent that only material breaches give rise to indemnification claims. Under New York law, this is not a settled rule, but it could be raised as a defense by Seller in any indemnification dispute. Note that the SPA does not contain a general materiality qualifier in the representations themselves (e.g., § 4.12 is not qualified by "material"), which is favorable to Buyer. The issue is limited to the interplay between the bring-down condition and the indemnification claim.

**Recommended fix:** None at this stage. Be aware of the argument if Seller raises it in response to an indemnification claim.
