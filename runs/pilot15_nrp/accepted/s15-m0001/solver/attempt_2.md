## ISSUE 1 – IP INDEMNIFICATION CAP REDUCED FROM 100% TO 10% BY CONFIRMATORY ASSIGNMENT

**Documents:** Confirmatory IP Assignment Agreement § 8.3; SPA § 9.5(a)–(b); SPA § 11.10

**What happened:** SPA § 9.5(b)(iii) expressly excludes breaches of the IP representations (SPA § 4.12) from the 10% Cap and instead provides that "Seller's aggregate liability for the matters in clauses (ii) and (iii) shall not exceed the Purchase Price" (i.e., 100%). This was a negotiated protection recognizing that the Recipes, Specifications, and Ostrander Marks are the core value of the business.

IP Assignment § 8.3 provides:

> "Assignor's aggregate liability for Losses arising out of any breach of this Agreement or of the representations and warranties in Section 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement, whether claimed under this Agreement or under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, shall be subject to the Cap set forth in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement, **notwithstanding Section 9.5(b) of the Purchase Agreement**. Amounts paid under this Agreement count toward the Cap, and the Cap is not increased by this Agreement."

**Why it matters:** The confirmatory assignment was necessitated because Seller (or an Affiliate) in fact held IP interests at Closing—meaning SPA § 4.12(b) ("neither Seller nor any of its Affiliates holds any right, title or interest in any Company Intellectual Property") was breached. Breidoun's recovery for that breach, and for any future IP claims (e.g., a third party asserting ownership of a recipe or mark), is now capped at 10% of the Purchase Price rather than 100%. In a business whose value is substantially its IP, this is a severe reduction in protection.

The order-of-precedence clause (SPA § 11.10) states that "the Ancillary Agreement controls as to its subject matter." The IP Assignment is an Ancillary Agreement (SPA § 1.1). Although one could argue that indemnification structure is the "subject matter" of the SPA rather than the IP Assignment, the express "notwithstanding" language in § 8.3 makes a strong textual case that the drafters intended § 8.3 to override § 9.5(b).

**Complicating factor:** SPA § 6.10 required the confirmatory assignment to be "in form reasonably satisfactory to Buyer." Breidoun joined the agreement (IP Assignment § 9.1) and accepted its terms. If Breidoun's counsel reviewed the document before execution, it will be difficult to argue that § 8.3 was inadvertent or that the form was not "reasonably satisfactory."

**Recommendation:**

- Open a dialogue with Seller's counsel immediately. Argue that SPA § 11.10's "subject matter" qualifier means the IP Assignment controls only on matters of assignment and perfection of IP, not on the indemnification architecture of the SPA. The indemnification cap is a global deal-structure provision negotiated in the SPA, and a post-closing ancillary document should not unilaterally rewrite it.
- If Seller resists, consider whether the economic impact is material enough to warrant a dispute. If the Purchase Price is, say, $20 million, the difference between a 10% cap ($2 million) and a 100% cap ($20 million) on an IP claim is $18 million.
- **Proposed amendment language** (if negotiating a fix): Delete the second sentence of IP Assignment § 8.3 and replace with: "This Section 8.3 does not limit or reduce the exclusions from the Cap set forth in Section 9.5(b) of the Purchase Agreement. The Cap in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement applies to Losses under this Agreement only to the extent they do not arise from a breach of Section 4.12 of the Purchase Agreement."
- **Open question:** Was § 8.3 in the first draft Seller sent, or was it inserted later? Do we have the negotiation history? If Breidoun's counsel flagged it and it was still included, that undermines our position. If it appeared in a late revision without discussion, we have a stronger argument.

## ISSUE 2 – UNTIMELY NOTICE OF DISAGREEMENT; WORKING CAPITAL ADJUSTMENT LIKELY LOCKED IN AT SELLER'S NUMBER

**Documents:** SPA § 2.4(a)–(b); IP Assignment Recitals C and D

**What happened:** SPA § 2.4(a) required Seller to deliver the Closing Statement within 45 days after Closing (July 1, 2026), i.e., by August 15, 2026. Seller delivered it on August 3, 2026 (IP Assignment Recital C). SPA § 2.4(b) gave Buyer 30 days from delivery to object. Thirty days after August 3 is **September 2, 2026**. Breidoun delivered its Notice of Disagreement on **September 18, 2026**—16 days late (IP Assignment Recital D).

SPA § 2.4(b) provides: "If Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement within that period, the Closing Statement and Seller's calculation of Closing Working Capital shall be final and binding on the Parties."

**Why it matters:** At Closing, Breidoun paid the Base Purchase Price adjusted for the difference between Seller's good-faith estimate of Closing Working Capital and Target Working Capital (SPA § 2.3). If Seller's estimate was higher than actual Closing Working Capital, Breidoun overpaid at Closing and would have been entitled to a refund under § 2.4(e). But if the Notice is untimely, Seller's number is final, and Breidoun cannot recover the overpayment.

**Possible arguments (weak):**

- *Delivery timing:* SPA § 11.1 deems notice given "when sent by email with confirmation of transmission" or "one Business Day after deposit with a nationally recognized overnight courier." If the Closing Statement was sent by email on August 3 but the confirmation of transmission failed or was not received until August 4 or 5, the 30-day clock might start later. However, IP Assignment Recital C records Seller's statement that it "delivered" the Closing Statement on August 3, and Breidoun did not dispute that recital when it joined the IP Assignment. Even a two-day shift (deadline of September 4) does not save the September 18 Notice.
- *Waiver or estoppel:* Seller rejected the Notice as untimely but then proceeded to negotiate the IP Assignment (which Breidoun joined on September 25). One could argue Seller waived strict compliance by continuing to deal with Breidoun on other post-closing matters. This is a stretch; the rejection was express (Recital D).
- *Manifest error:* SPA § 2.4(c) allows the Independent Accountant's determination to be set aside for "manifest error," but that provision applies only after a timely Notice of Disagreement has triggered the resolution process. It does not cure the missed deadline.

**Recommendation:**

- **Open question:** What was the dollar amount in dispute? If the working capital delta is immaterial (e.g., under $50,000), it may not be worth litigating the timeliness issue. If it is material (e.g., $500,000+), we should assess whether to assert a waiver/estoppel argument or simply write it off.
- **Process fix going forward:** Implement an internal calendar protocol for all post-closing adjustment deadlines. The 30-day window in § 2.4(b) is short and unforgiving.
- Do **not** concede the point in writing. Breidoun has "expressly reserve[d] its position" (Recital D). Maintain that reservation in all correspondence.

## ISSUE 3 – GOVERNING LAW AND FORUM CONFLICT BETWEEN SPA AND IP ASSIGNMENT

**Documents:** SPA § 11.8; IP Assignment § 10.1–10.2

**What happened:** The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The IP Assignment is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (or Superior Court / D. Del. if Chancery lacks jurisdiction).

**Why it matters:** The central dispute (Issue 1) turns on the interaction between SPA § 9.5(b) and IP Assignment § 8.3, mediated by SPA § 11.10 (order of precedence). A New York court and a Delaware court could reach different conclusions on:

- How to construe a "notwithstanding" clause in an ancillary document that purports to override a term in the master agreement;
- Whether the "subject matter" qualifier in an order-of-precedence clause limits the ancillary document's supremacy to its core subject (here, IP assignment) or extends to cross-cutting provisions like indemnification caps;
- Whether the IP Assignment, as a "Transaction Document," falls within the SPA's New York forum clause (SPA § 11.8 covers "disputes under any Transaction Document") or within the IP Assignment's Delaware forum clause.

**Recommendation:**

- If we litigate Issue 1, we want it in New York under New York law. File first in New York County Supreme Court (or SDNY) and argue that SPA § 11.8's reference to "any Transaction Document" encompasses the IP Assignment, making New York the exclusive forum.
- Alternatively, argue that the dispute is really about the SPA's indemnification structure (a New York-law question) and that the IP Assignment's Delaware forum clause applies only to disputes "arising out of or relating to [the IP Assignment] or the Assigned IP" in their own right (e.g., a dispute over whether the assignment was effective, or whether Seller delivered the recipe cards).
- **Open question:** Which court has personal jurisdiction over Seller (a Delaware LLC)? If Seller has no substantial contacts with New York, a New York court might dismiss for inconvenient forum despite the forum selection clause. Confirm Seller's principal place of business and any New York operations.

## ISSUE 4 – KEY EMPLOYEE NON-COMPETE COVERS NARROWER "BUSINESS" THAN THE SPA

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement § 1 (definition of "Business"); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Business")

**What happened:** The SPA defines "Business" as "inflight catering, galley provisioning and crew meal services for regional air carriers." The Key Employee Agreement defines "Business" as "the preparation of hot inflight meals for regional air carriers."

**Why it matters:** Solvig's non-compete (Key Employee Agreement § 7.1) prohibits her from working for "any Person engaged in the Business" during the Restricted Period (employment + 2 years). Because the Key Employee Agreement's definition of "Business" excludes galley provisioning and crew meal services, Solvig could join a competitor that provides galley provisioning or crew meal services to regional carriers without breaching § 7.1. Given that the Company's business (per SPA Recital B) includes all three of those activities, this is a meaningful gap in protection.

**Recommendation:**

- If Solvig is still employed (the agreement was effective July 1, 2026 and she is presumably still with the Company), negotiate an amendment conforming the "Business" definition to the SPA's broader definition.
- **Proposed language:** Replace the definition of "Business" in Key Employee Agreement § 1 with: "'Business' means inflight catering, galley provisioning and crew meal services for regional air carriers, and the preparation, production and supply of meals, foodservice equipment provisioning, and related services to airlines and airport operators."
- If Solvig resists, assess whether the gap is material. If the Company's revenue is predominantly from hot meal preparation (and galley provisioning/crew meals are minor), the practical risk may be low.
- **Open question:** What percentage of Company revenue is from (a) hot inflight meal preparation, (b) galley provisioning, and (c) crew meal services? This determines the practical significance of the gap.

## ISSUE 5 – KEY EMPLOYEE AGREEMENT CONSIDERATION LANGUAGE IS DEFECTIVE

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement § 7.5

**What happened:** Section 7.5 states that Solvig "has received adequate consideration for [the restrictive covenants], including her proceeds from and benefits under the transactions contemplated by the Purchase Agreement and the retention payment in Section 4.3."

**Why it matters:** Solvig is an employee of the Company, not a seller or shareholder. She receives no "proceeds from" the Purchase Agreement. The reference to "proceeds" is factually incorrect and could be used by Solvig (or a court) to argue that the stated consideration was illusory or that the drafter did not carefully consider what Solvig actually received. In New York, a non-compete entered into in connection with a change of control requires adequate consideration beyond continued employment. The $90,000 retention payment is real consideration, but the muddled language weakens the enforceability analysis.

**Recommendation:**

- If we amend the agreement to fix Issue 4, clean up § 7.5 at the same time.
- **Proposed language:** "Employee acknowledges that the restrictions in this Section 7 are reasonable in duration, geographic scope and subject matter. She acknowledges that they are necessary to protect the goodwill acquired by Buyer under the Purchase Agreement and the Company's Confidential Information, and that she has received adequate consideration for them, including the retention payment in Section 4.3, her continued employment at the compensation and benefits set forth in Section 4, and her continued access to and role in managing the Company's recipes, specifications and customer relationships."

## ISSUE 6 – CUSTOMER CONSENTS NOT A CLOSING CONDITION

**Documents:** SPA § 6.7; SPA § 7.1

**What happened:** SPA § 6.7 requires Seller to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain change-of-control consents from regional air carrier customers before Closing. However, § 7.1 (Conditions to Buyer's Obligations) does not include receipt of those consents as a closing condition. The deal closed on July 1, 2026 regardless.

**Why it matters:** If any material air carrier contract contains a change-of-control consent requirement and the consent was not obtained, the carrier may have the right to terminate the contract post-closing. Breidoun would have no specific closing-condition remedy; its only recourse would be an indemnification claim for breach of the § 6.7 covenant, which is subject to the 10% Cap (SPA § 9.5(a)) and the 0.75% basket (SPA § 9.5(c)).

**Recommendation:**

- **Open question:** Which customer consents were actually obtained before Closing? Review the Disclosure Schedule (Section 4.4) and any consent letters in the closing file. If all material carrier consents were obtained, this is a non-issue. If some were not, assess the revenue at risk.
- If consents are missing, send a notice to Seller under SPA § 9.4 (Claims Procedure) preserving the indemnification claim. Do this before the 18-month survival period expires (i.e., by approximately January 1, 2028).

**What happened:** The agreement has an initial three-year term from January 6, 2025, expiring January 5, 2028, with automatic one-year renewals unless 90 days' non-renewal notice is given. Either party may also terminate for convenience on 120 days' notice after the first anniversary (i.e., after January 6, 2026).

**Why it matters for Breidoun:**

**Recommendation:**

- Calendar the October 7, 2027 non-renewal deadline and the 120-day convenience termination notice requirement.
- Confirm the agreement is listed on SPA Schedule 4.8.
- **Open question:** What are the annual fees under this agreement? If they exceed $100,000 (likely, given multiple facilities and daily cart collections), it is a Material Contract on both grounds.
