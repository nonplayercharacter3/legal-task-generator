### 1. Missed Deadline to Deliver Notice of Disagreement on Closing Statement

**Documents:** SPA § 2.4(a)–(b); Buyer Managers' Written Consent, Recital C and Resolution 2

**Facts.** SPA § 2.4(a) required Seller to deliver the Closing Statement within 45 days after the June 30, 2026 Closing. The Managers' Written Consent (Recital C) states that Stipuv received the Closing Statement on **August 4, 2026**. SPA § 2.4(b) provides that if Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement "within 30 days after Buyer's receipt of the Closing Statement," the Closing Statement "shall be final and binding on the parties." Thirty days after August 4 is **September 3, 2026**.

The Managers' Written Consent is dated **September 14, 2026**—eleven days after the deadline expired. Resolution 2 authorizes the CEO and CFO to "prepare, execute and deliver" the Notice of Disagreement going forward. It does not ratify a prior delivery. The consent's Recital C expressly states: "The Company has not yet delivered a Notice of Disagreement to Seller."

**Why it matters.** If the Notice was not in fact delivered on or before September 3, the $1,240,000 working capital surplus claimed by Seller is now final and binding. Stipuv owes that amount to Seller under SPA § 2.4(d), payable within five Business Days of "final determination"—which has arguably already occurred by operation of the deemed-finality clause. The underlying dispute (exclusion of unearned deferred revenue from current liabilities) appears meritorious on the accounting principles, but the procedural default may foreclose it.

**Recommendation.**

- **Immediately confirm** whether any Notice of Disagreement (or partial notice) was actually transmitted to Seller on or before September 3, 2026, by email, courier, or hand delivery. Check the CEO's and CFO's sent folders and the courier log.
- If no notice was timely delivered, assess whether Seller has already invoiced or demanded the $1,240,000. If not, consider whether Seller's silence or course of performance could support an argument of waiver or estoppel (weak, but worth evaluating under New York law).
- If a notice was timely delivered, confirm the exact date and method and preserve the evidence.
- In either case, this issue should be raised with the client **before** any further action is taken, because it affects Stipuv's cash position and its leverage in any broader negotiation with Seller.

**Further information needed.** The actual Closing Statement and its supporting documentation; the Estimated Closing Statement delivered pre-Closing; any correspondence between the parties regarding the Closing Statement from August 4 to the present; confirmation of whether a Notice of Disagreement was in fact sent before September 3.

### 2. Uncapped Indemnification Exposure for Seller Guarantees

**Documents:** SPA §§ 9.3(b), 9.5(c); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Seller Guarantees"); SPA § 6.8

**Facts.** SPA § 9.3(b) requires Buyer to indemnify Seller Indemnitees for "any Seller Guarantee, including any demand, draw or claim made by any beneficiary under any Seller Guarantee and any payment or performance by Seller or any of its Affiliates thereunder, whether arising before, on or after the Closing Date." SPA § 9.5(c) expressly excludes Section 9.3(b) claims from both the 10% cap and the basket. The only limitation is that the aggregate liability for breaches of Fundamental Representations is capped at the Purchase Price (§ 9.5(c), last sentence), but that cap applies to Fundamental Reps, not to § 9.3(b).

"Seller Guarantees" is defined broadly (SPA § 1.1) to include "parent performance guarantees, payment guarantees, surety bonds, performance bonds and other credit support" issued by Seller or its Affiliates for the benefit of building owners, managing agents, or general contractors under any elevator contract, "including those listed in Schedule 6.8."

**Why it matters.** In the New York elevator industry, large building contracts routinely require parent-level performance guarantees or surety bonds in the millions. If the Company (now owned by Stipuv) fails to perform under a contract and the beneficiary draws on a Seller Guarantee, Seller pays the beneficiary and then has an **uncapped** right to indemnification from Stipuv under § 9.3(b). There is no time limit stated for this indemnity (the covenant-based survival under § 9.1 runs "until fully performed," and the guarantees themselves may have multi-year terms). Stipuv's exposure is theoretically unlimited and has no defined expiration.

**Recommendation.**

- **Request Schedule 6.8** (the list of Seller Guarantees) and all underlying guarantee instruments, bond forms, and contract insurance/security requirements. Quantify the total face value of outstanding Seller Guarantees.
- **Request a payoff or release schedule** from Seller showing which guarantees have been or will be released post-Closing and the timeline.
- If the total exposure is material, consider negotiating with Seller a cap or a defined expiration for § 9.3(b) claims (e.g., limited to guarantees outstanding at Closing, or capped at a stated amount). This is a difficult ask post-Closing but worth raising if the quantum is significant.
- Operationally, ensure the Company's contract management team tracks all contracts supported by Seller Guarantees and flags any renewal or amendment that could increase Seller's obligations (see SPA § 6.8, which prohibits such increases without Seller's consent).

**Further information needed.** Schedule 6.8; all Seller Guarantee instruments; the underlying customer contracts they secure; any draws or demands made to date.

### 3. Subordination Clause May Create Unintended Event of Default

**Documents:** Note §§ 6(a)–(b), 9(a), 10(b)

**Facts.** Note § 6(b) provides that if a payment default under Senior Indebtedness "has occurred and is continuing," Maker "shall make no payment under this Note until that default has been cured or waived." Note § 9(a) defines an Event of Default as Maker's failure to "pay any principal or interest under this Note when due," subject to a 10-Business-Day cure period after written notice. The carve-out in § 9(a) references only set-offs under § 5, **not** the payment prohibition in § 6(b).

Note § 10(b) states that Holder's remedies "are subject to Section 6," which should protect Maker from acceleration during a subordination hold. But the interplay is unclear: if a scheduled Note payment date arrives while a Senior Indebtedness default is continuing, § 6(b) prohibits payment. Is that a "failure to pay when due" under § 9(a)? If yes, the 10-day cure period runs, and if the Senior Indebtedness default is still uncured at the end of 10 days, Maker is technically in Event of Default—even though § 6(b) told it not to pay.

**Why it matters.** If Stipuv or the Company later incurs bank debt (which is likely for a business of this size) and experiences a temporary payment disruption, Stipuv could find itself in technical default on the Seller Note, triggering default interest (Note § 2(c), +200 bps) and potentially giving Seller the right to accelerate the entire $7.5M balance (Note § 10(a)). This is an avoidable trap.

**Recommendation.**

- **Raise with Seller's counsel** and seek a clarifying amendment to Note § 9(a) adding a carve-out: "other than any failure to pay to the extent such failure is attributable to the operation of Section 6(b)." Alternatively, add language to § 6(b) stating that the payment prohibition does not constitute a failure to pay "when due" for purposes of § 9(a).
- In the interim, ensure that any Senior Indebtedness facility includes adequate notice and cure provisions so that a technical bank default does not cascade into a Note default.
- Document the analysis in a memo to the file in case the issue is later disputed.

### 5. Short Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1

**Facts.** Non-Fundamental Representations survive only **six months** after the Closing Date. The Closing Date is June 30, 2026, so the survival period for all reps other than Organization, Authority, Capitalization, and Brokers expires **December 30, 2026**. This includes the Financial Statements rep (§ 4.5), Undisclosed Liabilities (§ 4.6), Elevator Code Compliance (§ 4.12), Litigation (§ 4.13), Taxes (§ 4.14), Employees and Labor (§ 4.15), and Insurance (§ 4.17).

**Why it matters.** Six months is short for an elevator service business. Latent code violations, undisclosed liabilities from prior work, or tax issues may not surface until after December 30, 2026. After that date, Stipuv's only recourse for most Seller reps is a Fraud claim (which requires "actual and intentional fraud," SPA § 1.1)—a very high bar.

**Recommendation.**

- **Calendar the December 30, 2026 deadline** and begin a pre-expiration review of all non-Fundamental Reps no later than November 2026.
- Direct the Company's management team to flag any issues that could implicate a Seller rep (e.g., code compliance findings, tax inquiries, litigation threats, insurance claims) and ensure a Claim Notice is delivered before December 30 if warranted (SPA § 9.4(a)).
- If the client is comfortable with the risk, no action is needed, but the client should make an informed decision about the short survival period.

### 7. Seller May Transfer the Note Without Stipuv's Consent

**Documents:** Note § 8(a)

**Facts.** Note § 8(a) permits Holder (Seller) to "sell, assign, transfer, pledge or negotiate this Note, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Maker." The only requirement is that Holder give Maker written notice within 10 Business Days after the transfer.

**Why it matters.** Stipuv could find itself owing $7.5M (plus interest) to an unknown third party—potentially a distressed-debt fund or an affiliate of Seller with different enforcement postures. Stipuv has no right of first refusal, no consent right, and no restriction on the transferee's identity. Combined with the subordination clause (Issue 3), a hostile or aggressive noteholder could exploit technical defaults.

**Recommendation.**

- **Negotiate an amendment** to Note § 8(a) adding a consent right or a negative list (e.g., no transfer to a competitor of the Company, no transfer to a Person that is an Affiliate of Seller's other business ventures). Even a "no transfer to a direct competitor" covenant would be valuable.
- If Seller resists, at minimum request that any transferee be subject to the same subordination and set-off provisions (which should carry with the Note by its terms, but confirm).
- In the interim, monitor for any notice of transfer under § 8(a).

### 10. Operational Constraint on Contracts Supported by Seller Guarantees

**Documents:** SPA § 6.8

**Facts.** SPA § 6.8 prohibits Buyer (and the Company) from "extend[ing], renew[ing] or amend[ing] any contract supported by a Seller Guarantee in a manner that increases Seller's obligations under that Seller Guarantee without Seller's prior written consent."

**Why it matters.** In normal business, the Company will seek to renew or expand customer contracts. If a contract is supported by a Seller Guarantee and the renewal involves a higher contract value or longer term (which could increase the guarantee exposure), Stipuv needs Seller's consent. This creates an ongoing dependency on Seller for routine business decisions and could slow contract negotiations with building owners.

**Recommendation.**

- Identify all contracts currently supported by Seller Guarantees (from Schedule 6.8, once obtained) and flag them in the Company's contract management system.
- Establish an internal approval process: any renewal, amendment, or extension of a flagged contract that could increase the guarantee amount requires prior clearance from Stipuv's legal team and, if necessary, Seller's written consent.
- Work with the Company's sales team to begin the process of obtaining releases or replacement guarantees (as § 6.8 contemplates) to eliminate the constraint over time.

## LOWER-PRIORITY ISSUES

### 13. Tension Between SPA § 9.7 and Note § 5 on Set-Off

**Documents:** SPA § 9.7; Note § 5(a); SPA § 11.9

**Facts.** SPA § 9.7 permits Buyer to set off "any amount to which any Buyer Indemnitee is entitled under this Article 9" against the Note, "in accordance with the terms of the Seller Note." Note § 5(a) limits set-off to claims "timely asserted by a Claim Notice delivered under Section 9.4… before the expiration of the applicable survival period." SPA § 11.9 provides that the SPA prevails over the Note in case of conflict.

**Why it matters.** The SPA's broader language ("any amount… entitled under this Article 9") could be read to include claims that arise after the survival period (e.g., covenant breaches that survive until fully performed). The Note's language is narrower (requires timely Claim Notice before survival expires). Because § 9.7 says "in accordance with the terms of the Seller Note," the Note's mechanics likely control. But the § 11.9 precedence clause creates ambiguity.

**Recommendation.** This is unlikely to be an issue in practice (most claims will be made within the survival period), but note the tension in the file. If a dispute arises, Stipuv should argue that § 11.9 gives the SPA's broader set-off right precedence.

### 14. Additional Insured Endorsement Scope

**Documents:** Broker Cover Letter § 3.1

**Facts.** Stipuv is added as additional insured on the CGL and umbrella policies "on a primary and non-contributory basis with respect to the Company's operations." The scope is limited to the Company's operations, not Stipuv's own operations or its role as parent.

**Why it matters.** If a claim arises from Stipuv's own actions (e.g., a management decision at the parent level that causes injury), the additional insured endorsement may not cover it. Stipuv's protection is limited to claims arising out of the Company's operations.

**Recommendation.** This is standard and likely sufficient. No action needed unless Stipuv anticipates parent-level activities that could give rise to liability.

### 15. KEA – Good Reason Triggers and Role Changes

**Documents:** KEA § 6(d); KEA § 7(b)

**Facts.** Good Reason includes "a material diminution in Executive's title, authority or duties." A Good Reason resignation triggers 12 months of salary continuation, unpaid bonus, unpaid retention bonus installments, and 12 months of COBRA premiums.

**Why it matters.** As Stipuv integrates Lockwen into its portfolio, there is a natural tendency to reorganize reporting lines, combine roles, or shift responsibilities. Any change that could be characterized as a "material diminution" in Okafor-Wyse's role could trigger a Good Reason claim and a seven-figure severance package (12 months × $245,000 salary + 30% bonus + up to $75,000 retention + COBRA).

**Recommendation.**

- Before making any changes to Okafor-Wyse's title, reporting structure, or responsibilities during the Initial Term (through June 30, 2028), obtain legal review to assess Good Reason risk.
- If a reorganization is planned, consider offering enhanced compensation or a negotiated amendment to the KEA to avoid the Good Reason trigger.
- Note that Okafor-Wyse must give notice within 60 days of the Good Reason condition arising (KEA § 6(d)), so Stipuv has a window to cure if a change is made.
