## 1. Bottom Line

1. **The SPA does not carry over several protections the LOI promised.** The execution draft omits the product recall indemnity (LOI §5(c)), caps the pre-Closing tax indemnity although the LOI leaves it uncapped (LOI §5(c); SPA §10.5(a)), does not treat tax representations as fundamental (LOI §4), and has no cash-free, debt-free mechanics (LOI §2). Each is a drafting fix, but Seller will resist some, and a few carry price implications.
2. **Dr. Santangelo is the most urgent individual item.** Her non-compete, non-solicit and invention assignment terminate automatically when Daikesk acquires the Company (Employment Agreement §§9.1(a), 9.2). The SPA's only related condition (§7.2(d)) would be satisfied even after those protections lapse.
3. **The TSA as drafted covers only CT systems** (TSA §1.1, Exhibit A). The SPA and LOI cover CT, MRI and ultrasound. The TSA also lets Seller delegate services to anyone (§12.3) and drops key FDA-related protections after the term (§3.5(c)). These gaps go directly to regulated complaint handling and recall readiness.
4. **Recommendation:** Do not sign until the Tier 1 items are resolved. Section 5 lists the points that must go back to the client for business decisions.

## 4. Issues in Detail

### Tier 1: Critical (resolve before signing)

**1. Product recall indemnity omitted**
- **Where:** LOI §5(c) makes product recall liabilities for Company Products sold, installed or serviced before Closing uncapped and not subject to the deductible. The LOI's closing paragraph in §5 says these remain Seller's responsibility. SPA §10.2 covers only representation breaches, covenant breaches and Pre-Closing Taxes. SPA §4.18 is the only recall-related term, and it is a representation.
- **Why it matters:** Section 4.18 speaks as of signing and as of Closing. It covers recalls "since January 1, 2022," and its knowledge qualifier in (b) reaches only facts Seller knew of. A recall launched after Closing for a pre-Closing unit may breach no representation at all. Where a breach is shown, recovery is subject to the $1,395,000 deductible (§10.4), the $18.6M cap (§10.5(a)) and 18-month survival (§9.1). Daikesk would carry the cost of a recall across the installed base.
- **Recommended fix:** Add §10.2(d), a special indemnity for all Losses from any recall, field correction, market withdrawal or safety action relating to Company Products sold, installed or serviced before Closing, using the LOI §5(c) language. It should sit outside §10.4 and §10.5(a) and have its own survival period. Buyer should control the regulatory response, consistent with TSA §2.3 and SPA §10.6(a). Consider a dedicated recall escrow (see item 6).
- **Decision:** Drafting, but the client must set the survival period and any sublimit.

**2. Pre-Closing Taxes and covenant breaches capped at $18.6M**
- **Where:** SPA §10.5(a) caps "all Losses for which Seller is liable under Section 10.2" at $18.6M. That includes §10.2(b) (covenants) and §10.2(c) (Pre-Closing Taxes). The only exceptions in §10.5(b) are Fraud and Fundamental Representations. LOI §5(c) leaves pre-Closing taxes uncapped. SPA §9.1 addresses survival of representations and covenants but not the §10.2(c) tax indemnity.
- **Why it matters:** The draft contradicts the LOI. A large tax assessment or a covenant breach (for example, under §6.7) would share one cap with every representation claim. The survival period for the tax indemnity is also open to dispute.
- **Recommended fix:** Carve §10.2(b) (to the extent it concerns Taxes) and §10.2(c) out of §10.5(a). Add express survival for §10.2(c) to 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations, mirroring §9.1(b).

**3. Dr. Santangelo's non-compete, non-solicit and invention assignment end at Closing**
- **Where:** Emp. §9.1(a) defines a Change of Control to include any acquisition of more than 50% of the voting stock by a Person other than Glosiv. Emp. §9.2 terminates §§7.2 (non-compete), 7.3 (non-solicit) and 7.4 (assignment of inventions) automatically at consummation, unless her written consent to continuation is delivered before consummation. Emp. §7.2 sets an 18-month Restricted Period covering the US, Canada, the EU and the UK. SPA §7.2(d) requires only that the Key Employee Agreement be "in full force and effect." SPA §6.7 binds only Seller and its Affiliates.
- **Why it matters:** Daikesk's own purchase triggers the Change of Control. At Closing, the CTO who owns the product roadmap would be free to compete immediately, to recruit Company engineers (§7.3(a) ends) and to solicit customers (§7.3(b) ends). Inventions made after Closing would no longer be assigned to the Company (§7.4 ends). Confidentiality (§7.1) and return of property (§7.6) survive, but they are much weaker protection. Because §7.2(d) is satisfied when the agreement remains in force, the condition gives Daikesk no protection on this point.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - (a) Before signing, negotiate with Dr. Santangelo a new restrictive covenant agreement and a confirmatory IP assignment, both effective at Closing.
  - (b) Require her written consent under Emp. §9.2, delivered before Closing.
  - (c) Add to SPA §7.2 a condition that those documents are in effect, that she remains employed and that she has not resigned or given notice.
  - (d) Add the documents to the §3.2 closing deliverables.
  - Employment counsel should confirm the enforceability of a post-Closing non-compete under Delaware law and whether the scope in Emp. §7.2 is defensible in a sale-of-business context.
- **Decision:** The client must decide what consideration to offer her and whether her continued employment is a walk right.

**4. Chain-of-title gap for Dr. Santangelo's pre-employment work**
- **Where:** Emp. §7.4 assigns only Inventions made "during the Employment Period," which began September 18, 2023 (Emp. §3.1). Emp. §7.5 and Schedule 1 exclude a prior invention: "Doctoral research on iterative reconstruction for low-dose cone-beam imaging, published 2014 (no patent filings)," with "None other." SPA §4.12 represents that the Company owns or validly uses all IP necessary for the Company Products and that each contributor has signed a written assignment.
- **Why it matters:** Iterative reconstruction for cone-beam imaging is relevant to CT systems. If any Company Product uses the Schedule 1 method, or other work she did before September 2023, the Company may not own it and may have only an implied license. Section 4.12 may be inaccurate as applied to her, but a representation claim is capped, time-limited and a weak remedy for a core technology gap.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm in diligence whether Schedule 1 subject matter is used in Company CT reconstruction. Obtain, at signing, a confirmatory nunc pro tunc assignment (or an exclusive license, if assignment is not achievable) from Dr. Santangelo covering all Company-related IP she contributed, including pre-Effective Date work. Add a specific indemnity for chain-of-title defects outside the cap and deductible. Make the assignment a closing deliverable.

**5. TSA excludes MRI and ultrasound**
- **Where:** TSA §1.1 defines "Company Products" as "the CT systems listed on Exhibit A." Exhibit A lists three CT models and AurexServ "for systems listed above." TSA §2.1(a) limits field service to installed base in the United States and Canada. By contrast, SPA Recital B and the SPA §1.1 definition of "Company Products" cover CT, MRI and ultrasound. LOI §3 describes the installed base of "each" product line. LOI §6 says the TSA will cover the services the Company currently receives, including field service and complaint handling for the installed base of Company Products.
- **Why it matters:** As drafted, no one is obligated to provide field service, complaint intake, MDR support or software support for MRI or ultrasound systems after Closing. The Company would take on complaint and reporting obligations for those lines with no support. The Exhibit B fee estimates (see item 11) appear to cover CT only. Installed base outside the US and Canada is also uncovered.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm with the client and with Seller what services are provided for MRI and ultrasound, and by whom. Expand the TSA definition of Company Products and Exhibit A to all product lines and all service software used with them. Extend §2.1(a) to the full installed base by country. Re-price Exhibit B.

**6. No escrow, set-off or guarantee behind Seller's obligations**
- **Where:** SPA §10.5(a) caps Seller's indemnity at $18.6M. Neither Article 2 nor Article 10 provides for an escrow or holdback. SPA §2.3(c) requires the working capital true-up to be paid directly within five Business Days, with no set-off right. SPA §2.4 addresses only tax withholding. There is no parent guarantee and no Seller financial-capacity covenant.
- **Why it matters:** The cap protects Daikesk only if Glosiv pays. The draft gives Daikesk no security. We do not know Glosiv's asset base after the sale or whether it intends to distribute the proceeds. Recovery above the escrow would require suing Glosiv.
- **Recommended fix:** Require an indemnity escrow sized and timed to the client's risk assessment, with a separate or longer component for recall and tax matters. Add a right of set-off against any amount Seller owes Buyer, including the working capital true-up. Alternatively, require a guarantee from a creditworthy parent or evidence of Glosiv's net worth.
- **Decision:** Client must decide escrow size and duration, and whether to rely on representations and warranties insurance instead. LOI §5 states that Buyer is evaluating R&W insurance.

**7. No cash-free, debt-free mechanics and no working capital definition**
- **Where:** SPA §2.1 fixes the price at $186,000,000 with no cash, debt or transaction-expense adjustment. SPA §2.3 uses "net working capital" without defining it or specifying accounting principles, and fixes the Target Working Capital at $24,500,000. SPA §1.1 has no Indebtedness, Cash or Transaction Expenses definitions. LOI §2 says the price assumes a cash-free, debt-free basis with normalized working capital, and that the target is to be agreed during diligence.
- **Why it matters:** Daikesk could inherit debt, unpaid transaction bonuses, deferred revenue or cash leakage without any price reduction. The $24.5M target is not shown to reflect a normalized level. Without a defined calculation, the process in §2.3(b) invites disputes. Seller prepares the estimate (§2.3(a)) and Buyer has no review right.
- **Recommended fix:** Add defined terms and price adjustments for Cash, Indebtedness and Transaction Expenses. Require payoff letters and lien releases as closing deliverables. Define Net Working Capital, attach Accounting Principles and an illustrative calculation, and confirm the $24.5M target against the normalized level. Give Buyer review and objection rights over the Estimated Working Capital. Consider a collar.
- **Decision:** Economic. The client must confirm the target and the cash-free, debt-free position.

**8. TSA regulatory protections fall away, and direct regulatory costs are excluded**
- **Where:** TSA §3.5(c) lists surviving sections as §§2.3(b), 3.5, 5.4, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 12. It omits §2.3(a) (two-Business-Day forwarding; 24 hours for death or serious injury), §2.3(c) (inspection access), §4.2 (knowledge transfer) and Section 7 (data security). TSA §10.1 excludes indirect, consequential and lost-profit damages. Its carve-outs cover only third-party indemnity, breach of §§7–8, and gross negligence or willful misconduct. TSA §10.2 caps general liability at fees paid and payable. TSA §9.1 indemnifies only third-party claims arising from gross negligence, willful misconduct or violation of Applicable Law. TSA §11.2 makes force majeure an excuse for non-performance, with data access as the only backstop, and only after five Business Days.
- **Why it matters:** After the TSA ends, FDA inspection access and Provider's forwarding duty end. Provider keeps Recipient Data without surviving security obligations. If a missed complaint leads to a late MDR, a correction, a recall or an FDA penalty, the Company's own costs are first-party losses. They are likely excluded as consequential or lost profits and capped at fees. Force majeure could excuse late forwarding. The 24-hour clock for death or serious injury (§2.3(a)) is the most time-critical obligation in the TSA. Ordinary negligence in performing the Services is not indemnified.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - Extend survival to §§2.3(a) and 2.3(c), to Section 7 for as long as Provider holds Recipient Data, and to §4.2 for any migration still in progress.
  - Carve regulatory costs (recall, correction, MDR-related costs, fines and penalties) caused by Provider's breach of §2.3 or Applicable Law out of §10.1 and out of the §10.2 cap.
  - Provide that force majeure cannot excuse §2.3(a) forwarding, and that Recipient may invoke backup arrangements immediately.
  - Add ordinary negligence to the §9.1(a) indemnity.
  - Regulatory counsel should confirm the forwarding periods against the 21 C.F.R. parts the TSA itself cites (803, 806 and 820).

### Tier 2: High (resolve in this turn of drafting)

**9. TSA allows delegation to anyone; no insurance**
- **Where:** TSA §12.3 lets Provider assign the Agreement or delegate any Services "to any Person without the consent of Recipient." Recipient may not assign without Provider's consent. The TSA contains no insurance provision. TSA §2.1(a) has Provider dispatching field engineers to customer sites.
- **Why it matters:** Daikesk would have no say over who performs field service or complaint handling. A subcontractor could have weaker quality controls, and a Provider change of control could pass the services to a competitor. There is no insurance requirement for on-site work or for professional or product liability connected to complaint handling.
- **Recommended fix:** Require Recipient consent to delegation of any Service, with an exception for Affiliates named in Exhibit B. Provider remains liable for subcontractors, and the subcontract must flow down Applicable Law and §§7–8. Provider may assign only with consent or to a successor that takes the whole relevant business and assumes the TSA. Recipient may assign to its Affiliates and to an acquirer of the Company. Add an insurance covenant, with limits set by the client's risk function.

**10. No durable access to Company software or data after the TSA term**
- **Where:** TSA §6.2 licenses Provider's systems and software only during the Service Term and only as necessary to receive the Services, and provides that each party keeps its own IP. TSA §6.3 vests in Recipient only work product created "exclusively for Recipient." TSA §3.5(b) requires delivery of data "in the format in which Provider maintains it." TSA §4.2 gives source-code access only for repositories "that relate to the Services." Exhibit A lists AurexServ as a Company Product. SPA §4.12 represents that the Company owns the service software used with the Company Products.
- **Why it matters:** The TSA does not say who owns the core AurexServ code. Section 6.2 could be read to leave core code with Seller. After the term, the Company loses access to Provider's quality management system and ERP, which hold device history and complaint records, and the data may arrive in a format it cannot use.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm AurexServ ownership in diligence. Require assignment, or a perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free license, to Company-specific software, documentation and source code, with a source-code deposit at Closing. Delete "exclusively" from §6.3. Extend the license to Provider's systems until migration is complete and records are validated. Require data delivery in an agreed, validated, readable format and a record-retention obligation.

**11. TSA term, migration and uncapped fees**
- **Where:** TSA §3.1 sets a 12-month term. §3.2 allows one extension of up to three months, at 10% higher fees, on 60 days' notice. §4.2 requires a migration plan within 45 days but sets no milestones or deadlines. §5.1 prices the Services at "fully loaded cost," including "overhead allocated on the same basis Provider used before the Closing." §2.4 caps volume at pre-Closing levels. §2.5 requires only good-faith consideration of pre-Closing services omitted from Exhibit B. Exhibit B estimates are $1,150,000 (B-1), $185,000 (B-2), $240,000 (B-3) and $310,000 (B-4) per month. That totals $1,885,000 per month, or about $22.6M over 12 months. The estimates are "for planning only," and no cap applies.
- **Why it matters:** The Company depends on Seller for field service, complaint handling, quality systems and IT. Twelve months plus a single three-month extension may not be enough to validate a QMS migration and build field service capacity. Fees are uncapped, and Seller controls the overhead allocation.
- **Recommended fix:** Business decision on term. Give Recipient the right to extend in further increments on notice. Add migration milestones with Provider obligations and remedies. Set a not-to-exceed fee for each Service. Define the overhead methodology, and audit it under §5.4 (which allows one audit per year; consider more). Require Provider to supply any pre-Closing service at cost under §2.5. Add service credits (see item 31).

**12. Tax representations should be fundamental under the LOI**
- **Where:** LOI §4 states that representations on organization, authority, capitalization and taxes "would be treated as fundamental representations." SPA §1.1 defines Fundamental Representations as §§4.1–4.3 only. SPA §9.1(b) gives §4.14 survival to 60 days after the statute of limitations. SPA §10.4 and §10.5(a) apply the deductible and the general cap to §4.14.
- **Why it matters:** This departs from the LOI. Tax representation breaches are subject to the deductible and the $18.6M cap.
- **Recommended fix:** Add §4.14 to the Fundamental Representations. At a minimum, carve it out of the deductible and the §10.5(a) cap, as LOI §5 describes.

**13. "Fraud" is narrowly defined**
- **Where:** SPA §1.1 defines Fraud as "actual and intentional fraud under Delaware common law in the making of a representation or warranty in Article 4." The term is used in §10.4 (deductible carve-out), §10.5(b) (cap carve-out) and §10.9(a) (exclusive remedy carve-out).
- **Why it matters:** Fraud is the principal route around the cap and the survival limits. The definition excludes fraud in the closing certificates delivered under §7.2(e) and §7.3(c), and fraud in the Ancillary Agreements. It also covers only Article 4 representations.
- **Recommended fix:** Define Fraud to cover the making of representations in this Agreement, in the Disclosure Schedule, in any certificate delivered under it, and in the Ancillary Agreements. Decide whether to seek a recklessness standard; expect Seller to resist.
- **Decision:** Client should weigh how far to push this given Seller's likely response.

**14. No integration, non-reliance or pro-sandbagging language**
- **Where:** SPA Article 11 has no integration clause, no non-reliance clause and no provision on the effect of Buyer's knowledge. LOI §§12–17 are binding as stated. NDA §7.1 disclaims any representation about information provided, with liability arising only under a definitive agreement. NDA §7.2 says neither party is bound absent a definitive agreement.
- **Why it matters:** Without an integration clause, the LOI and NDA remain in play for pre-signing matters. Under NDA §7.1, Daikesk's recourse for diligence information will be only what the SPA says, so the SPA representations must be complete (see item 24). Seller will likely ask for an anti-reliance clause that limits extra-contractual claims. Without a pro-sandbagging clause, Seller may argue that Daikesk waived breaches it learned of in diligence. That argument is live here, given items 4 and 5.
- **Recommended fix:** Add an integration clause that supersedes the LOI as to the Transaction and the NDA as to the Company, while preserving the NDA for Glosiv's retained information (see item 21). Accept only a narrow non-reliance clause that expressly preserves claims based on the SPA, the certificates and the Ancillary Agreements. Add an express statement that Buyer's rights are not affected by any investigation or knowledge.

**15. Indemnity is asymmetric against Daikesk**
- **Where:** SPA §10.3 has Buyer indemnify for its representations and covenants. §10.4 applies the deductible only to Seller's §10.2(a) claims. §10.5(c) caps Buyer's §10.3(a) representation indemnity at $18.6M, but leaves its §10.3(b) covenant indemnity uncapped. §10.5(a) caps Seller's covenant indemnity.
- **Why it matters:** Daikesk's representation exposure has no deductible at all, while Seller's has a $1,395,000 deductible. Daikesk's covenant exposure is uncapped, while Seller's is capped at the same $18.6M. The structure is the reverse of what a buyer wants.
- **Recommended fix:** Apply the deductible to Buyer's representation claims. Cap Buyer's covenant indemnity at the same level as Seller's, or cap both. Alternatively, leave Seller's covenant indemnity uncapped for key covenants, such as §§6.6 and 6.7 and the TSA-related covenants.

**16. Survival is too short for regulatory and product-liability exposure**
- **Where:** SPA §9.1 sets 18-month survival for general representations. That applies to §4.15 (compliance), §4.16 (FDA clearances, quality system, MDR and UDI), §4.18 (recalls and product liability) and §4.19 (insurance).
- **Why it matters:** Device and FDA-related claims often surface after 18 months. Given item 1, this is a material gap.
- **Recommended fix:** Extend survival for §§4.15–4.18 to at least 36 months, or to the statute of limitations plus 60 days. The client must set the period. The recall special indemnity (item 1) should have its own survival term.

**17. Dr. Santangelo's Good Reason and severance exposure, some of which Daikesk could trigger**
- **Where:** Emp. §2.1 (reports to the Company's CEO). Emp. §2.4 (headquarters in Wilmington). Emp. §5.4(b) (material diminution in title, authority, duties) and §5.4(c) (relocation of more than 50 miles). Emp. §6.2 (on termination without Cause or for Good Reason: 12 months of base salary, a pro-rated bonus and 12 months of COBRA). Emp. §9.3 (within 12 months after a Change of Control: 18 months of base salary, a lump sum equal to target bonus and 18 months of COBRA). Emp. §4.1 (base salary of $410,000, which the Board may increase but not decrease). Emp. §4.2 (target bonus of 50%). SPA §3.2(d) (Buyer designates officers to resign at Closing).
- **Why it matters:** At a $410,000 base and a 50% target, the change-of-control package is about $615,000 in base continuation plus a $205,000 target-bonus lump sum, plus 18 months of COBRA and a pro-rated bonus. Those amounts rise with any increase. Daikesk could trigger Good Reason through its own decisions. Examples include asking her to resign as an officer under SPA §3.2(d), changing her reporting line, or moving her office. The notice and cure mechanics in Emp. §5.4 give Daikesk a cure opportunity but do not prevent the claim.
- **Recommended fix:** In the new agreement (item 3), obtain her waiver of Good Reason arising from the transaction and from the agreed post-Closing role. Exclude her from the §3.2(d) officer resignations, or confirm in writing that she remains CTO. Decide whether to offer a retention package. Equity under Emp. §4.3 is granted at the discretion of the plan administrator, so any new equity will have to come from Daikesk.
- **Decision:** Client must decide retention economics and her post-Closing role.

**18. Exclusivity has lapsed, and the LOI timetable has been missed**
- **Where:** LOI §13 gives 75 days of exclusivity from March 9, 2026, which ran to about May 23, 2026. LOI §16(c) terminates the LOI on expiry of exclusivity unless extended. §13 is not in the list of provisions that survive termination in §16. LOI §11 targeted signing within 75 days. The LOI closing paragraph required countersignature by March 16, 2026. SPA §6.5 provides exclusivity only from signing.
- **Why it matters:** Unless extended in writing (no extension is in the folder), no exclusivity is in effect. Seller is free to talk to others now. We also do not know whether the LOI was countersigned. If it was not, the LOI's binding provisions (§§12–17) never took effect. The NDA would still govern confidentiality.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm countersignature and whether exclusivity was extended. If not, obtain a written extension immediately, and consider a pre-signing exclusivity covenant in the signing process.
- **Decision:** Client must decide whether to pay for an extension or accept the risk.

**19. Interim covenants are too thin and miss FDA-specific protections**
- **Where:** SPA §6.1 requires only ordinary-course operation and commercially reasonable efforts to preserve relationships. It restricts only amendments, terminations and waivers under Material Contracts. Emp. §4.1 lets the Board increase Dr. Santangelo's salary. Nothing in the SPA requires notice of FDA correspondence, complaints, recalls or product changes. Nothing restricts transaction or retention payments.
- **Why it matters:** Before Closing, Seller could raise Dr. Santangelo's pay, grant new change-of-control payments that become Company liabilities (the SPA has no Transaction Expenses definition; see item 7), enter new contracts, or make product or quality-system changes without Buyer's consent. Daikesk might not learn of a Form 483, a complaint trend or a recall decision until after it has closed.
- **Recommended fix:** Add specific negative covenants: no new change-of-control, retention or transaction payments unless Seller bears them; no compensation changes for the Key Employee without consent; no new or amended Material Contracts; no product changes that would require a new 510(k) and no quality-system changes without consultation; no recall or field action without consultation, except as legally required. Add a notice covenant for FDA correspondence, Form 483s, complaints involving death or serious injury, and recall decisions, within a short window (one or two Business Days). Require maintenance of the quality system and of key personnel.

**20. Disclosure Schedule and exhibits not in the folder**
- **Where:** SPA §1.1 defines the Disclosure Schedule as delivered on the signing date. The Article 4 lead-in qualifies every representation by it. Sections 4.4(c), 4.5, 4.9, 4.11, 4.17 and 4.19 each refer to schedules. SPA §11.8 applies disclosures across sections "to the extent its relevance … is reasonably apparent on the face of the disclosure." SPA Exhibit A (Form of Stock Power) is not attached.
- **Why it matters:** Every representation is only as strong as the schedule that qualifies it. The face-of-document standard in §11.8 is customary, but a general data-room disclosure would undercut the representations.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain and review the Disclosure Schedule before signing. Confirm that there is no general data-room disclosure. Narrow §11.8 so that disclosures must be specific and apparent on their face without reference to other documents.

**21. NDA: contact restrictions, clean team, post-closing use and survival**
- **Where:** NDA §5.1 bars Daikesk from contacting the Company's employees, customers, suppliers or distributors about the transaction without Glosiv's written consent, and requires use of Glosiv's designated contacts. NDA §4.1 allows pricing, customer-specific terms, roadmaps and unreleased designs to be designated "Clean Team Information," shared only with a clean team admitted in writing. NDA §4.2 addresses antitrust and the HSR waiting period. NDA §2.1 limits use to the Potential Transaction. NDA §9.1 sets a term of two years, to January 12, 2028, and §9.1 does not list §5 among the obligations that continue. SPA §6.2 gives access to personnel and records, with no reference to a clean team. SPA §6.1 gives Buyer consent rights over Company operations.
- **Why it matters:** Daikesk needs to speak to Dr. Santangelo (item 3), to Halvorsen (the OEM consent in SPA §7.2(c)) and to key customers. Each requires Glosiv's written consent under §5.1. No clean team agreement is in the folder, so competitively sensitive roadmaps and pricing may be shared without the protections §4.1 requires. Once the SPA closes, Daikesk owns the Company's information. But the NDA's use restriction still reaches information Glosiv disclosed, and the SPA does not say that the NDA ends as to Company information. Buyer consent rights in SPA §6.1, and any integration planning before HSR clearance, raise gun-jumping questions that NDA §4.2 signals.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain Glosiv's written consent under §5.1 now, for named contacts and any planned approaches. Confirm that a clean team agreement exists, and reference it in SPA §6.2. Have antitrust counsel review the §6.1 consent rights and any integration planning. Add to the SPA a provision that the NDA terminates at Closing as to Company information, while Glosiv's retained information stays protected. Clarify the duration of §5.

**22. Financing risk and specific performance**
- **Where:** LOI §10 says the Transaction is not subject to a financing condition, and Buyer will use cash and committed financing. SPA §5.4 is a sufficiency-of-funds representation given at signing and at Closing. SPA §7 contains no financing condition. SPA §8.1(b) leaves the Outside Date blank ("[●], 2027"). SPA §8.2 preserves liability for "willful breach," which is undefined. SPA §11.11 gives each party a right to specific performance.
- **Why it matters:** If Daikesk cannot fund at Closing, Glosiv can seek specific performance to compel the $186M purchase (§11.11), or damages for willful breach (§8.2). The draft has no reverse termination fee and no financing-related outside date. The client's financing commitments are not in the folder, so their adequacy is unverified.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm the committed financing covers the Purchase Price and transaction costs, and its expiry relative to the Outside Date. Define "willful breach." Fill in the Outside Date.
- **Decision:** Client must decide whether to accept the no-financing-condition risk or negotiate a reverse termination fee or financing-linked outside date.

### Tier 3: Medium and Low (resolve as time allows)

**23. Materiality scrape applies only to Losses.** SPA §10.7 disregards materiality "for purposes of calculating Losses." Materiality qualifiers still apply when deciding whether a representation was breached, which is a double layer that narrows recovery. *Fix:* Apply the scrape both to determining breach and to calculating Losses.

**24. Missing and weak representations.**
- SPA §4.7 covers only GAAP balance-sheet liabilities, and only immaterial post-June liabilities. Off-balance-sheet and contingent liabilities, including product claims, are not covered.
- SPA §4.12 makes non-infringement knowledge-qualified, and does not address ownership of AurexServ specifically.
- SPA §4.16 says only that each product "has received clearance" or is exempt. It does not address post-clearance modifications (including software updates), the completeness of complaint files, or MDR timeliness.
- SPA §4.18 contains no product-warranty representation and no complaint-handling representation.
- SPA §4.19 contains no claims-history or adequacy representation.
- Missing entirely: sufficiency of assets (important because the TSA supplies services); data privacy, HIPAA and cybersecurity; healthcare fraud-and-abuse and anti-corruption compliance (to confirm given the customer base and any international sales); top customers and suppliers, including Halvorsen; affiliate transactions (see SPA §6.10); and undisclosed transaction-related payments.

**25. Financial representations are stale.** SPA §4.6 covers the interim balance sheet at June 30, 2026, and §4.8 looks back to December 31, 2025. Today is November 2, 2026, so the June statements will be at least four months old by signing. *Fix:* Update §4.6 to the most recent quarter-end, and align §4.8 with it.

**26. Consent efforts and cost allocation.** SPA §6.3 requires only commercially reasonable efforts to obtain the OEM consent and is silent on cost or on amendments. Consent is a closing condition (§7.2(c)), which is helpful. SPA §4.5 lists only the OEM consent and HSR filings. The Halvorsen OEM Supply Agreement is not in the folder. SPA §1.1 describes it as the supplier of detector arrays and gantry components, so it may be a single-source dependency. TSA §2.6 splits third-party consent costs equally and requires only good-faith cooperation if a consent is not obtained. *Fix:* Require reasonable best efforts. Seller should bear all consent fees and any cost of amendments, with no amendment without Buyer's consent. Add any TSA-related consents to §4.5. In the TSA, Seller should bear the cost of Seller-side licenses and provide a functionally equivalent alternative at no cost.

**27. Seller restrictive covenants do not protect customers.** SPA §6.7(b) bars solicitation of Company employees only. It does not restrict solicitation of customers, distributors or installed-base service accounts, and LOI §7 is the same. *Fix:* Add a non-solicitation and non-interference covenant for customers, distributors and suppliers. Confirm that Affiliates are bound. Limit the TSA carve-out in §6.7(a) to performance under the TSA.

**28. Insurance access and run-off.** SPA §4.19 covers only the listing of policies, their status and premium payment. There is no covenant on occurrence-based coverage for pre-Closing products, on Company access to Seller's policies, or on run-off for claims-made product liability. *Fix:* Add a covenant giving the Company access to Seller's policies for pre-Closing occurrences, and require Seller to maintain or tail the product-liability coverage.

**29. Governing law and forum conflict.** TSA §12.6 selects New York law and exclusive jurisdiction in the Manhattan courts. SPA §§11.9–11.10 select Delaware law and the Court of Chancery for any Ancillary Agreement. SPA §11.4 provides that an Ancillary Agreement controls over the SPA on its subject matter, so the TSA's New York forum could override the SPA. The NDA (§§10.6–10.7) is Delaware. *Fix:* Align the TSA to Delaware and the Court of Chancery, unless the client prefers New York for a deliberate reason.

**30. PHI and the business associate agreement.** TSA §7.3 requires a BAA "on customary terms at the Closing" if PHI is involved, but attaches no form and makes its requirement conditional. TSA §1.1 "Recipient Data" includes service records, device history and service-software logs. TSA §2.1(c) covers remote diagnostic connectivity. NDA §4.3 says neither party intends to disclose PHI and requires redaction of any PHI that appears. CT service data and logs may contain patient identifiers. "Shall enter into" is an agreement to agree. *Fix:* Attach a BAA form and sign it at signing. Confirm whether the diligence materials (complaint files, service records) contained unredacted PHI, and how any such material was handled under NDA §4.3.

**31. TSA service levels, key personnel and an undefined term.** "Complaint-handling Service" appears in TSA §§3.4, 11.2 and 12.7 but is not defined in §1.1. Exhibit B sets service levels (remote response within 4 hours, on-site within one Business Day, 99.5% availability, Priority 1 tickets within two hours), but there are no service credits and no termination right for chronic failure. TSA §2.2 measures performance only against pre-Closing levels. There are no key-personnel commitments. *Fix:* Define the term. Add service credits and a chronic-failure termination right. Add key-personnel commitments.

**32. Tax drafting.**
