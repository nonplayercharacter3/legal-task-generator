## 1. Bottom Line

The execution draft of the Stock Purchase Agreement ("SPA") is not ready to sign. Five problems need to be fixed before signing, and each affects price, risk allocation, or the ability to keep the business running after Closing:

1. **Dr. Santangelo's restrictive covenants and IP assignment terminate automatically at Closing.** Her Executive Employment Agreement ("EEA") says so, and the SPA does not address it (EEA §§9.1(a), 9.2; SPA §7.2(d)).
2. **The LOI's product recall indemnity is missing from the SPA.** Recall exposure falls under a general representation that is subject to the deductible and cap and expires after 18 months (LOI §5(c); SPA §§4.18, 9.1, 10.2, 10.4, 10.5).
3. **LOI economics are not carried into the SPA.** Taxes are not fundamental, pre-Closing taxes are capped, and there is no cash-free/debt-free mechanism (LOI §§2, 4, 5(c); SPA §§1.1, 2.1–2.3, 10.5(a)).
4. **There is no credit support.** There is no escrow, holdback, guaranty, or set-off right against a seller whose post-Closing solvency is unknown (SPA Article 10; §2.3(c)).
5. **LOI exclusivity has lapsed.** Exclusivity ran for 75 days from March 9, 2026, which means it ended on or about May 23, 2026, and the LOI itself terminates on that expiration unless extended in writing (LOI §§13, 16(c)).

The Transition Services Agreement ("TSA") also has a scope gap that could leave the MRI and ultrasound installed base without service after Closing (TSA §1.1, Exhibit A). Several decisions need to go back to the client, and they are flagged in Section 5.

## 3. Priority 1: Must Be Resolved Before Signing

### Issue 1. Dr. Santangelo's non-compete, non-solicit, and IP assignment fall away at Closing

- **Where:**
  - EEA §9.1(a): Change of Control includes any Person other than Glosiv acquiring more than 50% of voting stock. Daikesk's purchase of 100% triggers it at Closing.
  - EEA §9.2: On a Change of Control, §7.2 (non-compete), §7.3 (non-solicit) and §7.4 (assignment of inventions) "automatically terminate" as of consummation, unless her written consent to continuation is delivered to the Company before consummation.
  - EEA §7.2 (18-month Restricted Period, US/Canada/EU/UK); §7.7 (reformation clause); §10.6 (Delaware law).
  - SPA §1.1 ("Key Employee," "Key Employee Agreement"); §7.2(d) (condition is only that the Key Employee Agreement "shall be in full force and effect"); §4.12 (IP rep, made as of Closing); §6.1(c).
  - EEA §3.2 (at-will); §5.5 (60 days' notice to resign).
- **Why it matters:**
  - At Closing, the CTO's non-compete, non-solicit, and invention assignment lapse without any action by Daikesk. The Company would then have no contractual restraint on the person who leads R&D and product strategy (EEA §2.2).
  - Inventions she conceives after Closing would not be assigned to the Company (EEA §9.2 terminates §7.4). The §4.12 rep, which is made as of Closing, would be inaccurate for ongoing development.
  - The §7.2(d) condition is satisfied by a resignation, because the agreement remains "in force" after she leaves. The condition gives Daikesk no walk right if she leaves.
  - Enforceability of the 18-month, four-jurisdiction covenant is uncertain under Delaware law (EEA §10.6). Reformation (§7.7) helps but does not guarantee enforcement. Confirm.
- **Recommended fix:**
  1. Before signing, obtain Dr. Santangelo's written consent under EEA §9.2 to continuation of §§7.2–7.4, delivered to the Company before Closing and acknowledged by the Company.
  2. Preferably, have her sign a new employment, retention, and restrictive covenant agreement with the Company, effective at Closing, with covenants tailored to Delaware enforceability principles and an IP assignment that survives the Change of Control.
  3. In the SPA, add a closing condition that she (a) remains employed and has not given notice of resignation or Good Reason, and (b) has signed the consent and new agreement. Add a Seller covenant to procure this, and a Seller rep that no notice has been received.
  4. Before any direct approach to her, obtain Glosiv's written consent under NDA §5.1 (see Issue 20).
- **Client decision:** Yes. Retention package, whether her signing is a walk right, and whether to renegotiate Good Reason (Issue 6).

### Issue 2. Product recall liabilities are not indemnified

- **Where:**
  - LOI §5(c): product recall liabilities for Company Products "sold, installed or serviced before Closing" are uncapped and not subject to the deductible. LOI §5 (closing paragraph) says these matters remain Seller's responsibility even if a R&W policy is obtained.
  - SPA §10.2: Seller indemnity covers only (a) representations, (b) covenants, and (c) Pre-Closing Taxes. Recalls are not listed.
  - SPA §4.18: recall rep runs from January 1, 2022 and is a general representation.
  - SPA §§9.1 (18-month general survival), 10.4 (deductible), 10.5(a) (cap).
  - SPA §10.6(a): Seller may assume defense of third-party claims, except FDA or regulatory actions.
- **Why it matters:** A post-Closing recall or field action on the installed base is the largest tail risk for an imaging-systems business. As drafted, Daikesk could recover only by proving that a recall-related rep was false when made. That recovery would be subject to the $1,395,000 deductible and $18.6M cap, and the claim would expire 18 months after Closing. A recall triggered by a product sold or installed before Closing, but first known after month 18, would be uncovered. The rep also starts in 2022, but the LOI covers all products installed or serviced before Closing.
- **Recommended fix:**
  1. Add SPA §10.2(d): a specific indemnity for "Recall Losses," defined to track LOI §5(c) and to include notification, correction, replacement, and regulatory penalty costs.
  2. Exclude Recall Losses from the deductible (§10.4) and the general cap (§10.5(a)). Cap them at the Purchase Price, consistent with the fundamental-rep cap.
  3. Set an express survival period (see client decision).
  4. Give Buyer control of recall decisions and regulatory communications, consistent with TSA §2.3 (Recipient retains final authority) and TSA §9.3.
  5. Extend the recall rep's lookback to cover the installed base.
  6. Confirm that recall costs are treated as direct damages under the TSA as well (see Issue 8).
- **Client decision:** Yes. Survival period, cap, and whether recall exposure should be secured by escrow (Issue 4).

### Issue 3. LOI economics and tax terms are not carried into the SPA

- **Taxes.**
  - LOI §4 says tax representations are fundamental. SPA §1.1 "Fundamental Representations" covers only §§4.1–4.3. Tax (§4.14) is a general representation subject to the deductible and cap (§§10.4, 10.5(a)). Survival of 60 days after the statute of limitations (SPA §9.1(b)) is consistent with the LOI.
  - LOI §5(c) says pre-closing taxes are uncapped. SPA §10.5(a) caps all Seller liability under §10.2, including §10.2(c) Pre-Closing Taxes, at $18.6M.
- **Cash-free/debt-free.** LOI §2 says the price assumes delivery "on a cash-free, debt-free basis with a normalized level of net working capital." SPA §§2.1–2.3 adjust only for working capital. There is no closing indebtedness, closing cash, or transaction expense deduction. Any debt or debt-like items at Closing would pass to Daikesk through the Company at no price reduction.
- **Asymmetric covenant indemnity.** Seller's covenant indemnity (§10.2(b)) is capped under §10.5(a). Buyer's covenant indemnity (§10.3(b)) is not capped (§10.5(c) caps only §10.3(a)).
- **Why it matters:** Taxes and covenant breaches are capped contrary to the LOI, and debt-like items could leave with the Company without a price adjustment. The asymmetry places more covenant exposure on Daikesk than on Seller.
- **Recommended fix:**
  1. Make §4.14 a Fundamental Representation, or carve Pre-Closing Taxes and tax-rep breaches out of the deductible and §10.5(a) cap, subject to the Purchase Price cap.
  2. Add definitions of Closing Indebtedness, Closing Cash, and Transaction Expenses, and deduct them in §§2.1–2.3.
  3. Make Seller's covenant indemnity uncapped or subject to the same cap as Buyer's covenant indemnity.
- **Client decision:** Confirm the $24.5M Target Working Capital (SPA §2.3(a)) and the list of debt-like items. LOI §2 says the target would be agreed during diligence. The folder contains no support for $24.5M.

### Issue 4. No credit support for indemnity or the post-Closing true-up

- **Where:** SPA Article 10 (no escrow, holdback, or set-off right); §2.3(c) (true-up payable by Seller directly); SPA preamble (Seller is Glosiv); LOI §5 (R&W insurance under evaluation).
- **Why it matters:** After Closing, Daikesk's recovery for the true-up, the $18.6M general cap, and recall exposure depends on Glosiv's continued existence and solvency. Glosiv could distribute the sale proceeds and dissolve.
- **Recommended fix:** Escrow or holdback sized to the general cap plus expected recall exposure for the survival period. Set-off right against amounts payable to Seller under the SPA. A Glosiv covenant not to dissolve or distribute below a stated net worth, or a guaranty from a creditworthy parent if Glosiv is a holding company. If R&W insurance is pursued, the recall exposure remains Seller's under LOI §5, so the escrow is still needed for it.
- **Client decision:** Yes. Escrow amount and duration, R&W insurance decision, and guaranty requirement.

## 4. Priority 2: Significant Issues to Resolve Before Signing

### Issue 6. Good Reason and change-of-control severance exposure

- **Where:**
  - EEA §5.4(b) (material diminution in title, authority, duties, or responsibilities) and §2.1 (CTO reports to the Company's CEO).
  - EEA §5.4(c) (relocation more than 50 miles from Wilmington, per §2.4).
  - EEA §9.3 (for 12 months after a Change of Control, 18 months' base salary continuation, a lump sum equal to target bonus, and 18 months of COBRA). EEA §6.2(b) (prorated bonus).
  - EEA §4.2 (target bonus of 50%); §4.3 (long-term incentives come from "the Company or its parent," which will no longer be Glosiv).
  - SPA §4.17: no rep on transaction-triggered payments.
- **Why it matters:** At base salary of $410,000 (EEA §4.1), the enhanced severance is $615,000 in base continuation plus a $205,000 target bonus lump sum, totaling $820,000 before COBRA and prorated bonus. The non-Change-of-Control severance is $410,000 plus 12 months of COBRA. Integrating the CTO role, which is the most likely integration step, would likely trigger Good Reason. The Company would then bear the cost, and Dr. Santangelo would be free to leave with no restraint (Issue 1).
- **Recommended fix:** Negotiate, as part of the retention package, a written acknowledgment that the planned role does not constitute Good Reason, and a waiver of §9.3 enhancements. Require Seller to bear the cost of any CoC severance triggered by pre-Closing arrangements, or reduce the price. Add a rep listing all transaction-triggered or change-in-control payments.
- **Client decision:** Yes. Who bears the cost and what retention package to offer.

### Issue 7. TSA does not cover the full product line or geography

- **Where:**
  - SPA §1.1 "Company Products" (all CT, MRI and ultrasound systems and related software); Recital B; LOI §3 (installed base for all three product lines).
  - TSA §1.1 "Company Products" (CT systems "listed on Exhibit A"); TSA Exhibit A (three CT models plus AurexServ software only).
  - TSA §2.1(a) (field service limited to installations in the United States and Canada).
  - TSA §2.4 (Provider need not provide any service not in §2.1 or Exhibit B).
- **Why it matters:** If the Company still has an MRI or ultrasound installed base, field service, complaint handling, and software support for that base would not be covered by the TSA. Under TSA §2.3, the Company remains the manufacturer with complaint and reporting responsibility, so Daikesk would have to build those capabilities quickly. Any installed base outside the US and Canada is also uncovered.
- **Recommended fix:** Conform TSA "Company Products" to the SPA definition. Add MRI and ultrasound models to Exhibit A. Expand the geographic scope to match the installed base. Replace the "good faith consideration" standard in §2.5 with an obligation to provide all services provided in the 12 months before Closing.
- **Client decision:** Confirm with the client's operations team whether MRI and ultrasound products remain in active service, where the installed base is, and whether Daikesk has the capacity to self-perform service.

### Issue 8. TSA liability, regulatory-operations, and continuity gaps

- **Where:**
  - TSA §9.1: Provider indemnity covers only third-party claims arising from gross negligence, willful misconduct, violation of law, or breach of §§7–8. Ordinary negligence is not covered.
  - TSA §10.1: consequential damages and lost profits are excluded, with carve-outs only for indemnity, §7/§8 breaches, and gross negligence or willful misconduct.
  - TSA §10.2: the general cap equals fees paid and payable under the TSA. Based on Exhibit B estimates ($1,885,000 per month), this is roughly $22.6M over the 12-month term.
  - TSA §2.3(a): complaints forwarded within two Business Days (24 hours only for death or serious injury).
  - TSA §3.5(c): survival omits §2.3(a) (forwarding), §2.3(c) (regulator access), and §7 (data security).
  - TSA §11.2: force majeure (including "epidemic" and "failure of public utilities") excuses Complaint-handling performance. Only after five Business Days must Recipient self-perform.
  - TSA §12.3: Provider may assign or delegate any Service to any Person without consent.
  - TSA §3.4: 60-day transition protection before Complaint-handling can be suspended.
  - TSA §3.2: one extension of up to three months, at a 10% fee increase.
  - TSA Exhibit B: service levels with no credits or chronic-failure remedy.
- **Why it matters:** Glosiv's ordinary negligence in field service causing injury would not be indemnified. Recall costs and regulatory losses could be argued to be consequential and excluded. A two-Business-Day forwarding window may not leave enough time for Part 803 reporting obligations cited in TSA §1.1. Regulator access and record retention would end at termination. Glosiv could delegate complaint handling to a competitor. Service credits are absent. Twelve months may be too short for a regulated migration of complaint and quality systems.
- **Recommended fix:**
  1. Indemnity for ordinary negligence in performing Services that causes bodily injury or property damage, and for fines or penalties. Define "third-party claim" to include actions by Governmental Authorities.
  2. Treat recall and corrective-action costs as direct damages. Consider uncapped liability for regulatory breaches.
  3. Forwarding of MDR-relevant complaints within one Business Day, or same-day for death or serious injury.
  4. Add §§2.3(c), 3.5(b) (data delivery), and 7 to the survival list.
  5. Limit force majeure so it does not excuse complaint-handling or regulatory deadlines. Require a business-continuity plan.
  6. No delegation of Complaint-handling or field service to a Daikesk competitor without Recipient consent. Add insolvency and change-of-control termination rights.
  7. Add service credits and a chronic-failure termination right.
  8. Allow multiple extensions for regulatory migration.
- **Client decision:** Appetite for cost and capacity to self-perform complaint handling during the transition.

### Issue 9. Ownership of service software and quality data is unclear

- **Where:**
  - SPA §4.12: Company "owns or has the valid right to use" IP used for the Company Products, including service software.
  - TSA Exhibit B, B-3 (Provider hosts service software); TSA §6.2 (Provider systems licensed only during the Service Term, non-transferable).
  - TSA §6.3 (updates owned by Recipient only if created "exclusively for Recipient").
  - TSA §4.2 (migration; "reasonable knowledge-transfer assistance," including source code "that relate to the Services").
  - TSA §2.1(d) and Exhibit B, B-4 (ERP, CRM, quality management, and email hosted on Provider's systems).
  - TSA §6.1 (commingling permitted in shared systems).
  - TSA Exhibit A (AurexServ, "all versions released").
- **Why it matters:** If AurexServ is owned or controlled by Glosiv, the Company's rights could end with the TSA, and software updates made during the TSA could belong to Glosiv. That would undermine the §4.12 rep and the continuity of service for the installed base. Complaint and quality records migrating off Glosiv systems also raise FDA record-control questions. TSA §2.3(b) cites 21 C.F.R. Part 820.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm ownership of AurexServ and all service-software code. Require an assignment to the Company at Closing of Company-specific software, a perpetual license to any Glosiv software needed for Company Products, and source-code delivery or escrow. Expand TSA §6.3 to cover all updates to service software for Company Products. Require a data-migration plan with a defined export format for quality and complaint data (TSA §3.5(b)).

### Issue 10. Conflicting governing law and forum

- **Where:** TSA §12.6 (New York law; exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan courts). SPA §11.9 (Delaware law for the SPA "and all Ancillary Agreements") and §11.10 (exclusive Court of Chancery forum for Ancillary Agreements). SPA §11.4 (Ancillary Agreements control over the SPA on their subject matter). NDA §§10.6–10.7 (Delaware and Chancery).
- **Why it matters:** The same transaction could be litigated in two forums under two bodies of law. TSA service disputes could go to New York courts under New York law, while SPA indemnity claims go to Chancery.
- **Recommended fix:** Conform TSA §12.6 to Delaware law and the Court of Chancery, with the jury waiver aligned with SPA §11.10.

### Issue 11. OEM supply consent (Halvorsen): efforts-only standard and MAE carve-out

- **Where:**
  - SPA §6.3 (Seller uses "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain the OEM Supply Consent).
  - SPA §7.2(c) (closing condition: consent obtained, "in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer").
  - SPA §§4.4(c), 4.5 (carve-outs for Disclosure Schedule items).
  - SPA §1.1 "OEM Supply Agreement" (detector arrays and gantry components incorporated in Company Products).
  - SPA §1.1 "Material Adverse Effect," carve-out (c) (effects of "the announcement of the transactions"). The disproportionate-effect exception applies only to clauses (a) and (b).
  - LOI §9(c) (material third-party consents identified in diligence).
  - TSA §2.6 (third-party consents for TSA services; costs split equally).
- **Why it matters:** Halvorsen supplies critical components. Under an efforts-only standard, Seller need not pay a consent fee or accept unfavorable terms. Under carve-out (c), if Halvorsen terminates or reprices because of the announcement, that loss would not count toward an MAE, and Daikesk could be forced to close without a remedy. A consent could also be listed on the Disclosure Schedule, which would remove the §4.4 rep. We have not seen the OEM Supply Agreement, so we cannot assess change-of-control, termination, or sole-source terms.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain and review the OEM Supply Agreement. Keep the consent as a hard condition, with consent costs borne by Seller. Require Buyer approval of any amendment negotiated with Halvorsen. Narrow carve-out (c) so that announcement-related losses from key suppliers, key employees, and customers count toward an MAE. Add a specific indemnity if Halvorsen terminates or reprices within a stated period after Closing.
- **Client decision:** Whether Daikesk would waive the condition if consent is obtained on different terms, and how costs are shared.

### Issue 12. Financing and specific performance

- **Where:** LOI §10 (cash on hand and committed financing; no financing condition). SPA §5.4 (rep of sufficient funds at Closing). SPA §11.11 (specific performance available to each party). SPA §8.1 (no reverse termination fee). SPA §7.2 (no financing condition).
- **Why it matters:** Without a financing condition, Glosiv could seek specific performance to compel Daikesk to close even if financing fails. Under SPA §8.2, willful breach is preserved as a basis for liability.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm the committed financing and check that its conditions match the SPA. If the financing carries conditions Daikesk cannot satisfy, consider a financing-out or reverse termination fee. Confirm that the §5.4 rep is supported by commitment papers.
- **Client decision:** Yes. Financing structure and acceptance of specific-performance exposure.

### Issue 13. Interim operating covenants are too thin for a medical device target

- **Where:** SPA §6.1 (ordinary course; consent "not unreasonably withheld"; specific restrictions only on Material Contracts, including the Key Employee Agreement). SPA §6.2 (access). No covenant on FDA submissions, MDR or recall decisions, 510(k) maintenance, complaint handling, pricing, capital expenditure, compensation changes, new material contracts, or FDA correspondence. SPA §8.1(b) (Outside Date is blank).
- **Why it matters:** Between signing and Closing, Seller could change pricing, sign exclusive supply deals, alter complaint handling, or receive an FDA warning letter without consulting Daikesk. The Outside Date is not yet set, so the interim period could be long.
- **Recommended fix:** Add specific negative covenants and a regulatory-consultation covenant. Require notice within two Business Days of any FDA communication, complaint trend, MDR decision, or recall evaluation. Give Buyer a right to participate in regulatory strategy. Require maintenance of existing clearances. Prohibit amendments to the OEM Supply Agreement and the Key Employee Agreement without consent.

### Issue 14. Insurance and product-liability tail

- **Where:** SPA §4.19 (lists policies, including product liability, that are in force). No covenant on occurrence-based coverage or tail coverage. TSA is silent.
- **Why it matters:** If the Company is covered under Glosiv group policies, coverage for pre-Closing products may end at Closing. Claims from injuries caused by pre-Closing units could be uninsured.
- **Recommended fix:** Seller covenant to maintain occurrence-based policies and give the Company access to them for pre-Closing occurrences, or to purchase a tail policy. Confirm which policies are Company-specific and which are Glosiv group policies.

### Issue 15. Fraud definition, knowledge qualifier, and materiality scrape

- **Where:**
  - SPA §1.1 "Fraud" (actual and intentional fraud under Delaware common law, limited to Article 4 representations). Used in §§10.4, 10.5(b), and 10.9.
  - SPA §10.7 (materiality scrape applies only "for purposes of calculating Losses"; breach is still determined with materiality qualifiers, including the §7.2(a) bring-down).
  - SPA §1.1 "knowledge" is not defined, but "to Seller's knowledge" is used in §§4.12, 4.13, and 4.18(b).
- **Why it matters:** The fraud definition excludes recklessness and misstatements in certificates (SPA §§3.2(g), 7.2(e)). It also excludes statements in diligence responses. The scrape does not change whether a rep was breached. An undefined knowledge standard invites disputes about who knew what.
- **Recommended fix:** Broaden "Fraud" to include recklessness and certificates delivered under the SPA. Make the scrape apply to both breach and damages. Define Seller's knowledge as the actual knowledge of named individuals (for example, the CEO, CFO, head of quality or regulatory, and the CTO) after reasonable inquiry. Consider a Seller rep that data-room responses on complaints and MDRs were accurate.
