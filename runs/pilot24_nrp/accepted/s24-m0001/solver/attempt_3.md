## Issue 1 (CRITICAL): Key Employee Agreement Anti-Assignment Clause Will Void Restrictive Covenants if Assigned Without Oyelaran's Consent

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 2.1(c) – identifies the KEA as an Assigned Contract
- KEA § 8.3 – prohibits assignment without Executive's prior written consent; provides that any purported assignment without consent is void and the covenants in Section 6 (non-compete, customer non-solicit, employee non-solicit) terminate
- APA § 2.8 – provides that if an Assigned Contract cannot be assigned without an unobtained consent, the APA does not constitute an assignment

**Why it matters for Seles:**
Seles is paying $18.75 million in part for the value of a stable field operations leader who is contractually barred from competing for 18 months post-employment. If Seller assigns the KEA at Closing without Oyelaran's written consent, the assignment is void *and* the restrictive covenants terminate by their own terms. Seles would then have no contractual barrier to Oyelaran joining a competitor the day after Closing. This is not merely a failed assignment—it is an affirmative destruction of the covenant protection.

**Recommended fix:**
Obtain Oyelaran's written consent to the assignment as a condition to Closing (add to APA § 7.2). Alternatively, structure the transaction so that Seles enters into a new employment agreement with Oyelaran containing equivalent restrictive covenants, effective at Closing, and the KEA is not assigned but rather terminated by mutual agreement at Closing with Seller paying any accrued obligations through the Closing Date (consistent with APA § 6.4).

> **⚑ FLAG FOR CLIENT:** We need to confirm immediately whether Oyelaran's consent has been obtained or is being negotiated. If she is unwilling to consent, the deal structure for retaining her (and the covenant protection) needs to be rethought before we respond to Seller.

## Issue 2 (CRITICAL): 90-Day Survival Period for General Representations Is Inadequate for an Elevator Safety Business

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 1.1 – defines "General Representations" as all Article IV reps other than Fundamental Representations (§§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.8)
- APA § 4.12 – Elevator Code Compliance and Inspections (a General Representation)
- APA § 9.1 – General Representations survive only 90 days after Closing

**Why it matters for Seles:**
Section 4.12 represents that all Covered Units are in compliance with Elevator Codes, all periodic safety tests (Category 1, Category 5, full-load, governor) have been timely completed and recorded, and there are no open deficiency notices. In the elevator industry, inspection cycles, re-inspections after corrective work, and the surfacing of latent maintenance deficiencies routinely extend well beyond 90 days. A unit that passed inspection at Closing but fails a follow-up or reveals a deferred maintenance issue at month four would fall outside the survival window, and Seles would have no indemnification recourse under § 9.2(a).

This is particularly acute in light of Schedule Supplement No. 1 (see Issue 3), which already reveals 14 units with open deficiency notices—evidence that the § 4.12 representation was inaccurate or on the verge of inaccuracy at signing.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate an extension of the survival period for Section 4.12 specifically (or for all General Representations) to at least 18 months, aligned with the Indemnity Escrow Release Date in the Escrow Agreement (§ 4.1). Alternatively, elevate Section 4.12 to a Fundamental Representation (six-year survival), though this will be a significant ask.

> **⚑ FLAG FOR CLIENT:** The client should confirm how quickly it expects to discover post-Closing elevator compliance issues in its own inspection cycle. If Seles' internal process identifies problems within 90 days, the risk is lower; if not, this is a material gap.

## Issue 3 (HIGH): Schedule Supplement No. 1 – Late Objection and $640,000 Special Escrow at Risk

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 6.5 – Schedule Supplement mechanism; 5-Business-Day objection period
- Escrow Agreement § 3.3 – recites that Seller delivered Schedule Supplement No. 1 on February 9, 2026 (disclosing 14 open state inspection deficiency notices on Covered Units); Buyer objected on February 23, 2026
- Escrow Agreement § 3.3(a) – Seller's position: objection was untimely; matters deemed cured
- Escrow Agreement § 3.3(b) – Buyer's position: matters not cured; Losses indemnifiable
- Escrow Agreement § 3.4(b) – on the Indemnity Escrow Release Date, remaining Special Escrow goes to Seller absent a Final Order or Joint Written Instructions in Buyer's favor

**Why it matters for Seles:**
Counting from February 9, 2026 (a Monday), five Business Days expires on February 17, 2026 (accounting for the weekend and Presidents' Day on February 16). Buyer's objection on February 23 was approximately seven Business Days late. Under the plain language of APA § 6.5, the disclosure is deemed to have cured the § 4.12 inaccuracy. If Seller's reading prevails, Seles has no indemnification claim for the 14 deficiency notices, and the $640,000 Special Escrow—though held for now—will be released to Seller at the 15-month mark unless Seles obtains a Final Order in its favor.

The practical effect: Seles may be on the hook for remediation costs, fines, or lost revenue associated with 14 non-compliant elevator units, with no recourse against Seller.

**Recommended fix:**
- Assess the merits of a "tolling" or "waiver" argument: Did Seller's delivery of the supplement constitute a breach of the ordinary-course covenant (§ 6.1) that tolled the objection period? Was the supplement so material that the 5-day period was unreasonable as applied?
- If the objection period is indefensibly missed, consider whether the parties can amend the Escrow Agreement to extend the resolution deadline beyond 15 months, or to add a mechanism for Seles to claim against the Indemnity Escrow Fund (rather than only the Special Escrow) for the deficiency-notice remediation costs.
- Quantify the actual remediation exposure for the 14 units to determine whether the $640,000 is adequate security or whether additional protection is needed.

> **⚑ FLAG FOR CLIENT:** We need the client to (a) confirm the exact date and method of delivery of the February 9 supplement and the February 23 objection (to verify the business-day count), and (b) provide an engineering estimate of remediation costs for the 14 units so we can assess whether $640,000 is sufficient coverage.

## Issue 4 (HIGH): 15-Month Deadline to Resolve Special Escrow Dispute

**Documents and Sections:**
- Escrow Agreement § 4.1 – Indemnity Escrow Release Date is 15 months after Closing
- Escrow Agreement § 3.4(b) – Special Escrow releases to Seller on that date absent a Final Order or Joint Written Instructions
- Escrow Agreement § 3.4(c) – if Buyer has commenced an action that has not resulted in a Final Order, Buyer may notify the Escrow Agent to continue holding the funds

**Why it matters for Seles:**
Fifteen months from Closing is the outer boundary for Seles to either (a) settle with Seller, (b) obtain a Final Order in Chancery or Superior Court, or (c) invoke § 3.4(c) to keep the funds pending. Elevator remediation disputes can involve re-inspections by state authorities, which have their own timelines outside Seles' control. If the state inspection authority has not cleared the units by month 14, Seles may face a situation where it cannot yet prove the full extent of its Losses but the escrow is about to release.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate an extension of the Special Escrow release date to 24 months, or tie it to the later of 15 months after Closing or 90 days after the state inspection authority issues its final determination on all 14 units. Section 3.4(c) provides a partial safety valve, but it requires that an action already be "commenced"—it does not extend the deadline if Seles has not yet filed suit.

## Issue 5 (MEDIUM): Cap on General Representation Claims Limited to Indemnity Escrow Amount

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 9.4(b) – Seller's aggregate liability for breaches of General Representations shall not exceed the Indemnity Escrow Amount ($1,875,000)
- APA § 9.4(c) – overall cap is the Purchase Price ($18,750,000)

**Why it matters for Seles:**
If multiple General Representations are breached (e.g., § 4.12 elevator compliance, § 4.9 condition of assets, § 4.7 material contracts), Seles' total recovery for all such breaches combined is $1,875,000. In a business with hundreds of elevator units, a systemic compliance failure could easily exceed that amount. Note that Excluded Liability claims under § 9.2(c) are not subject to the § 9.4(b) cap (only the § 9.4(c) Purchase Price cap applies), which provides some additional protection for pre-Closing elevator fines and remediation costs.

**Recommended fix:**
Seek to increase the § 9.4(b) cap to at least $3,000,000, or to carve § 4.12 out of the cap entirely (subject only to the Purchase Price cap). Alternatively, increase the Indemnity Escrow Amount from $1,875,000 (10% of Purchase Price) to 15% ($2,812,500).

## Issue 6 (MEDIUM): Ambiguity in Whether Excluded Liability Claims Are Subject to the 90-Day Survival Period

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 9.1 – survival periods stated for General Reps (90 days) and Fundamental Reps (6 years); covenants survive "in accordance with their terms"
- APA § 9.2(c) – Seller indemnifies for Excluded Liabilities (a covenant, not a rep)
- APA § 9.1 (last two sentences) – notice requirement applies to "any claim for indemnification under Section 9.2(a) or Section 9.3(a)"

**Why it matters for Seles:**
The notice-and-survival language in § 9.1 expressly references only §§ 9.2(a) and 9.3(a) (representation/warranty breaches). It does not reference § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities). Read literally, Excluded Liability claims are not subject to the 90-day survival period and may be brought at any time. This is favorable to Seles for pre-Closing elevator fines, penalties, and remediation costs (expressly Excluded Liabilities under § 2.4). However, Seller may argue that the general survival framework applies to all indemnification claims. The ambiguity creates litigation risk.

**Recommended fix:**
Add clarifying language to § 9.1 stating that the survival periods for representations and warranties do not limit claims under § 9.2(c) (Excluded Liabilities) or § 9.2(b) (covenant breaches), which survive in accordance with their terms without a fixed expiration.

## Issue 7 (MEDIUM): TSA Liability Cap Is Low Relative to Operational Risk

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 5.1 – monthly fees: $24,500 (Daytime Dispatch) + $18,000 (After-Hours Emergency) = $42,500/month
- TSA § 6.1 – base Service Period is 6 months (maximum 9 with extension under § 6.2)
- TSA § 9.1 – Provider's aggregate liability capped at total fees paid and payable (approximately $255,000 for 6 months; $382,500 for 9 months)

**Why it matters for Seles:**
The cap means that if Provider's dispatch failures cause Seles to lose customers, incur overtime costs, or face contractual penalties with customers, Seles' recovery is limited to roughly $255,000–$382,500. The carve-out for bodily injury/death in entrapment calls is critical and appropriate, but economic losses from service degradation (missed calls, late dispatch, lost work orders) are subject to the cap.

**Recommended fix:**
Increase the cap to 2× total fees, or add a specific service-level credit mechanism (e.g., if Provider misses the 95% answer-within-30-seconds standard for two consecutive months, fees for the third month are reduced by 50%). The existing corrective-plan requirement in § 4.3 is a starting point but does not provide monetary relief.

## Issue 8 (MEDIUM): Fenmark Uniform Rental Agreement – Uncertain Status and Operational Dependency

**Documents and Sections:**
- APA § 2.1(c) – Assigned Contracts are those "listed in Section 2.1(c) of the Disclosure Schedule"
- APA § 2.2(d) – all contracts that are not Assigned Contracts are Excluded Assets
- Fenmark Agreement § 8.2 – permits assignment without consent to "a successor to all or substantially all of its business to which this Agreement relates," on written notice and written assumption by the successor
- Fenmark Agreement § 2.4 – minimum 40 Wearers; if below for more than two consecutive months, Fenmark may charge for 40
- Fenmark Agreement § 6.4 – early termination for convenience during initial term (through June 3, 2027) requires payment of unamortized replacement charges

**Bearing on the deal:**
The Fenmark Agreement has moderate bearing. It provides uniforms (including safety-compliant high-visibility vests and flame-resistant coveralls) to the field workforce. It is not a customer contract, so its loss would not directly affect revenue. However:

1. **Assignment status is unclear.** We do not have the Disclosure Schedule. If the Fenmark Agreement is not listed as an Assigned Contract, it is an Excluded Asset, and Seles will not receive it at Closing. Seles would then need to either negotiate directly with Fenmark or source a new vendor.
2. **The successor-assignment clause is favorable.** If Seles qualifies as "a successor to all or substantially all of [Tripux's] business to which this Agreement relates," it can assume the agreement without Fenmark's consent—just written notice and a written assumption. This lowers the friction of bringing the agreement into Seles post-Closing.
3. **Minimum volume risk.** If Seles does not hire all of Tripux's field staff (APA § 6.4 makes hiring optional), the Wearer count could drop below 40, triggering the minimum-charge provision.
4. **Safety compliance.** The high-visibility and flame-resistant garments meet ANSI/ISEA 107 and NFPA 70E standards. If Seles loses this agreement and cannot quickly replace it, field workers may be without compliant PPE, creating OSHA exposure.

**Recommended fix:**
Confirm with the client whether the Fenmark Agreement is listed in the Disclosure Schedule as an Assigned Contract. If not, add it. If the client does not intend to hire all 40+ field staff, negotiate with Fenmark pre-Closing to reduce the minimum or to assign the agreement to Seles with a revised Wearer count. In any event, prepare a contingency plan for uniform sourcing effective at Closing.

## Issue 9 (LOW): Key Employee Agreement – Good Reason Triggers Create Potential Severance Liability for Seles

**Documents and Sections:**
- KEA § 5.2(b) – "Good Reason" includes material diminution in title, authority, or duties, or relocation by more than 50 miles
- KEA § 5.3 – severance for Good Reason resignation: 12 months' Base Salary ($185,000) + pro-rated bonus (target $46,250) + COBRA employer portion
- APA § 2.3(b) – liabilities under Assigned Contracts arising after Closing are Assumed Liabilities

**Why it matters for Seles:**
If the KEA is successfully assigned to Seles (see Issue 1), Seles becomes the "Company" under the agreement. Any post-Closing restructuring that diminishes Oyelaran's title, authority, or duties—or relocates her by more than 50 miles—could trigger a Good Reason resignation and a severance obligation of approximately $231,250 plus COBRA premiums. This is an Assumed Liability under APA § 2.3(b).

**Recommended fix:**
Before Closing, confirm with the client its plans for Oyelaran's role post-Closing. If any changes to title, reporting structure, or location are contemplated, either (a) obtain her written consent to the changes as part of the assignment consent (Issue 1), or (b) budget for the potential severance cost.

## Issue 10 (LOW): TSA – No Obligation to Expand Service Volume

**Documents and Sections:**
- TSA § 2.7 – Provider is not required to provide Services at a volume materially greater than pre-Closing volumes (adjusted for increases in Covered Units under Assigned Contracts)

**Why it matters for Seles:**
If Seles wins new customer contracts or adds units during the 6–9 month transition period, Provider can refuse to handle the incremental dispatch and call volume. This creates a capacity gap precisely when Seles may be most vulnerable (before its own systems are in place).

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate a volume corridor in the TSA (e.g., Provider shall handle up to 120% of pre-Closing volume without additional charge, and up to 150% at a specified per-unit increment). Alternatively, accelerate Seles' own dispatch build-out to reduce dependence on the TSA period.

## Conclusion

The transaction documents are broadly well-drafted, but the combination of (a) the KEA's void-on-assignment clause, (b) the 90-day survival period for the elevator compliance representation, and (c) the likely-late objection to Schedule Supplement No. 1 creates a concentrated risk profile around the single most valuable aspect of this acquisition: the compliance status of the elevator fleet and the retention of the field operations leader. I recommend we resolve Items 1–4 on the client-input table before circulating a response to Seller's counsel.
