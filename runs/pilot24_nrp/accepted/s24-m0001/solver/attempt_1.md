## Issue 1 – Key Employee Agreement: Assignment Consent Required; Covenants Terminate Without It

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement § 8.3; APA § 2.1(c), § 4.4, § 2.8

**The problem.** Section 8.3 of the Key Employee Agreement provides:

> "Company may not assign this Agreement, or any of its rights or obligations under this Agreement, without the prior written consent of Executive, including in connection with any merger, consolidation or reorganization of Company or any sale of all or substantially all of the assets of Company or of the Business. Any purported assignment by Company without the prior written consent of Executive is void. **If Company makes or purports to make such an assignment, the covenants in Section 6 terminate and are of no further force or effect.**"

Section 6 contains an 18-month non-compete (covering DE, MD, NJ, and PA), a customer non-solicit, and an employee non-solicit. These are the primary contractual protections Seles would have against Oyelaran (Director of Field Operations) leaving and taking the team, customers, or institutional knowledge to a competitor.

The APA lists the Key Employee Agreement as an Assigned Contract in § 2.1(c). However, the only consent identified in APA § 4.4 (and the Disclosure Schedule) is the municipal licensing authority's consent to transfer the elevator contractor license. **Oyelaran's consent is not listed as a required consent anywhere in the APA.**

If Seles closes without Oyelaran's written consent:
- The assignment of the Key Employee Agreement is void (§ 8.3);
- All Section 6 restrictive covenants terminate immediately;
- Oyelaran is free to compete with Seles the day after Closing.

APA § 2.8 (Non-Assignable Assets) provides that if an Assigned Contract cannot be assigned without a consent not obtained at Closing, the APA "does not constitute an assignment" and Seller shall "hold that item for the benefit of Buyer." This is inadequate here: the Key Employee Agreement is personal to Oyelaran, the covenants run to "Company" (Tripux), and Tripux will no longer operate the Business post-Closing. Holding the agreement "for the benefit of" Seles does not make Seles a party entitled to enforce the covenants, and it does not prevent the automatic termination triggered by a purported assignment.

**Why it matters.** Oyelaran oversees field operations, dispatch, safety testing coordination, and relationships with inspection authorities. She is the single most important person in the Business. Losing the non-compete and non-solicit covenants would expose Seles to immediate competitive risk.

**Recommendation.**
- Obtain Oyelaran's written consent to the assignment **before Closing** and add it to the list of required consents in APA § 4.4 and the Disclosure Schedule.
- Make the consent a condition to Closing under § 7.2 (parallel to the municipal license consent).
- If Oyelaran withholds consent, evaluate whether the deal can proceed without the covenants (it likely cannot, given her role) or whether a new agreement with Seles directly (with fresh consideration) can be negotiated.

> **⚠ FLAG FOR CLIENT:** We need to confirm whether Oyelaran's consent has been sought or obtained. If it has not, this is a deal-critical item that must be raised with Seller immediately. We also need the client's view on whether the transaction is viable without the restrictive covenants.

## Issue 2 – Schedule Supplement No. 1: Buyer's Objection Appears Late on the Face of the Documents

**Documents:** APA § 6.5; Escrow Agreement § 3.3

**The problem.** APA § 6.5 permits Seller to supplement the Disclosure Schedule before Closing. A supplemented matter "shall be deemed to have cured any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty arising from that matter" **unless Buyer delivers a written objection within 5 Business Days** after receipt.

Per Escrow Agreement § 3.3:
- Seller delivered Schedule Supplement No. 1 on **February 9, 2026** (a Monday), disclosing open state inspection deficiency notices on 14 Covered Units.
- Buyer delivered its written objection on **February 23, 2026** (a Monday).

Counting 5 Business Days from February 9 (excluding weekends; no federal holidays fall in this window):

| Business Day | Date |
|---|---|
| 1 | Tue, Feb 10 |
| 2 | Wed, Feb 11 |
| 3 | Thu, Feb 12 |
| 4 | Fri, Feb 13 |
| 5 | **Mon, Feb 16** |

The objection deadline was **February 16, 2026**. Buyer's objection on February 23 was **five Business Days late**. Seller's position in Escrow Agreement § 3.3(a) that the objection was untimely appears correct on the face of the documents.

**Consequences if Seller's position prevails:**
- Section 4.12 (Elevator Code Compliance) is deemed cured for purposes of the closing condition (§ 7.2(a)) and the indemnification provisions (Article IX).
- Buyer cannot bring a § 9.2(a) claim for breach of the Section 4.12 representation.
- The $640,000 Special Escrow is subject to release to Seller under Escrow Agreement § 3.4(b) on the Indemnity Escrow Release Date (15 months post-Closing) absent a Final Order or Joint Written Instructions in Buyer's favor.

**Silver lining – Excluded Liabilities survive independently.** Even if Section 4.12 is deemed cured, APA § 2.4 expressly excludes from Assumed Liabilities "any fines, penalties or remediation costs relating to the condition or inspection status of any elevator or escalator serviced by the Business before the Closing." Those remediation costs remain Excluded Liabilities, and Seller indemnifies Buyer for them under § 9.2(c). Critically, § 9.2(c) claims are **not** subject to the $93,750 basket (§ 9.4(a)) or the $1,875,000 General Rep cap (§ 9.4(b))—only the overall Purchase Price cap in § 9.4(c) applies. So Buyer's economic recovery for the actual remediation costs may be available regardless of the timeliness dispute.

However, the practical difference is significant: a § 9.2(a) claim is satisfied first from the escrow (§ 9.6), giving Buyer a secured source of recovery. A § 9.2(c) claim is also satisfied first from the escrow under § 9.6, but the Indemnity Escrow ($1,875,000) may be depleted by other claims, and the Special Escrow ($640,000) would have been released to Seller.

**Why it matters.** The 14 units with open deficiency notices represent real, quantifiable remediation costs and potential regulatory exposure. The timeliness issue determines whether Seles has a clean indemnification path or must litigate the procedural question first.

**Recommendation.**
- Assess whether there is any arguable basis for treating the objection as timely (e.g., was the supplement received later than February 9 due to delivery method? Does § 11.2's notice provision affect the "receipt" date?). The APA defines notice as "deemed given when delivered personally, by nationally recognized overnight courier, or by email with confirmation of transmission." If the supplement was sent by courier on February 9 but received on February 10 or 11, the deadline shifts.
- If no procedural argument exists, consider whether to (a) litigate the timeliness question (expensive, uncertain, and the Special Escrow could be released to Seller during the pendency if the Indemnity Escrow Release Date arrives before a Final Order—though § 3.4(c) allows Buyer to preserve the funds by giving notice of a pending action), or (b) accept the cure and pursue the § 9.2(c) Excluded Liability claim for actual remediation costs.
- In either case, ensure the § 3.4(c) notice is delivered before the Indemnity Escrow Release Date if litigation is pending.

> **⚠ FLAG FOR CLIENT:** We need to confirm the exact date and method by which Schedule Supplement No. 1 was delivered to Seles, and the exact date the objection was sent. If there is any gap between "sent" and "received," the 5-Business-Day clock may not have started on February 9. We also need the client's risk tolerance: do they want to litigate the timeliness question, or accept the cure and pursue Excluded Liability indemnification for the actual remediation costs?

## Issue 3 – 90-Day Survival Period for General Representations Is Inadequate for Elevator Code Compliance

**Documents:** APA § 9.1, § 4.12, § 1.1 (definition of "General Representations")

**The problem.** Section 4.12 (Elevator Code Compliance) is a General Representation, not a Fundamental Representation. Under § 9.1, General Representations survive only **90 days** after the Closing Date. No claim for indemnification under § 9.2(a) may be made unless written notice is delivered before the survival period expires.

Section 4.12 covers: compliance with Elevator Codes, current certificates of operation, timely completion of all periodic safety tests (Category 1, Category 5, full-load, governor tests), and absence of open deficiency notices. In an elevator business with potentially hundreds of Covered Units, 90 days is an extremely short window to:
- Review maintenance logs and test records for every unit;
- Identify units where tests were not actually performed despite records indicating they were;
- Discover units operating without current certificates;
- Identify latent code violations that only surface during a post-Closing inspection cycle.

**Why it matters.** If Seles discovers a Section 4.12 breach on day 91, the claim is time-barred. The Indemnity Escrow is held for 15 months, but that does not extend the survival period—Buyer must still notice the claim within 90 days.

**Recommendation.**
- Negotiate an extension of the survival period for Section 4.12 specifically (e.g., 18 months, aligned with the Indemnity Escrow Release Date, or at minimum 12 months to cover a full inspection cycle).
- If Seller resists, ensure Seles has a concrete post-Closing audit plan that can be executed within 90 days: assign internal or third-party engineers to review every Covered Unit's file, verify test records against inspection authority databases, and confirm certificate status.
- As a fallback, confirm that the § 9.2(c) Excluded Liability indemnification (which is a covenant, not a rep, and survives "in accordance with its terms" under § 9.1) provides a separate, longer-tail remedy for pre-Closing code violations.

## Issue 4 – Special Escrow Release Mechanics Create a Hard Deadline

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement §§ 3.4(b), 3.4(c), 4.1

**The problem.** The $640,000 Special Escrow Fund is released to Seller on the Indemnity Escrow Release Date (15 months after Closing) unless:
- Buyer has obtained a Final Order in its favor;
- Buyer and Seller have delivered Joint Written Instructions; or
- Buyer has commenced an action and delivered written notice of that action to the Escrow Agent and Seller **before** the Indemnity Escrow Release Date (§ 3.4(c)).

If none of these conditions is met, the entire Special Escrow goes to Seller, even if Buyer's underlying claim has merit but simply hasn't been adjudicated yet.

**Why it matters.** This creates a litigation deadline that is independent of the normal statute of limitations. If Seles is still negotiating or mediating the Schedule Supplement dispute at month 14, it must file suit and give notice before month 15 or lose the $640,000.

**Recommendation.**
- Calendar the Indemnity Escrow Release Date immediately upon Closing.
- If the dispute is not resolved by month 12, begin preparing for litigation or settlement.
- If litigation is filed, ensure the § 3.4(c) notice is delivered to both the Escrow Agent and Seller well before the Release Date.
- Consider negotiating an extension of the Special Escrow release date in the APA or a side letter, tied to the resolution of the timeliness dispute.

## Issue 5 – Interplay Between Schedule Supplement Cure and Excluded Liability Indemnification

**Documents:** APA §§ 2.4, 6.5, 9.2(c), 9.4

**The problem.** As noted in Issue 2, even if the Schedule Supplement is deemed effective (curing Section 4.12), the remediation costs for pre-Closing inspection deficiencies remain Excluded Liabilities under § 2.4, and Seller must indemnify for them under § 9.2(c). However, the documents do not expressly state that a Schedule Supplement cannot cure or limit § 9.2(c) claims. Section 6.5 says the supplement cures matters "for purposes of Section 7.2 and of the indemnification provisions of Article IX."

A narrow reading of § 6.5 could support Seller's argument that the supplement cures *all* Article IX claims "arising from that matter"—including § 9.2(c) claims for the same underlying deficiency notices. The text limits the cure to "any inaccuracy in or breach of any representation or warranty," which arguably does not reach § 9.2(c) (which is not a rep or warranty but a covenant to indemnify for Excluded Liabilities). But the phrase "for purposes of...the indemnification provisions of Article IX" is broad enough to create ambiguity.

**Why it matters.** If Seller successfully argues that the Schedule Supplement also limits § 9.2(c) claims, Seles would have no indemnification recourse for the remediation costs of the 14 units—neither under § 9.2(a) (cured rep) nor § 9.2(c) (covenant deemed cured). Seles would bear the full cost of bringing those units into compliance.

**Recommendation.**
- Seek a clarifying amendment to § 6.5 or a side letter confirming that the Schedule Supplement cures only the Section 4.12 representation and does not affect Buyer's rights under § 9.2(c) for Excluded Liabilities.
- Alternatively, negotiate that the Special Escrow ($640,000) is the exclusive security for the Schedule Supplement matters, and that § 9.2(c) claims for Excluded Liabilities are not subject to the cure.
- At minimum, document Seles's position (that § 6.5 cures only reps and warranties, not covenants) in a writing to Seller before Closing.

## Issue 6 – TSA Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for Emergency Call Failures

**Documents:** TSA § 9.1, § 5.1

**The problem.** Provider's aggregate liability under the TSA is capped at "the total fees paid and payable under Section 5.1." At $42,500/month for 6 months, the cap is approximately **$255,000** (or up to $382,500 with a 3-month extension).

The cap does not apply to:
- Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct; or
- "third-party claims for bodily injury or death caused by Provider's failure to perform the After-Hours Emergency Call Answering Service in accordance with Section 2.3."

**Why it matters.** The carve-out covers the most catastrophic scenario (death or serious injury from a missed entrapment call). However, it does not cover:
- Economic losses from a missed or delayed non-fatal emergency call (e.g., a business loses revenue because its elevator is out of service for an extra day due to a dispatch failure);
- Regulatory fines resulting from a response-time failure;
- Seles's own costs of covering a dispatch failure (e.g., overtime pay for technicians called in after a delayed notification).

These would be subject to the $255,000 cap.

**Recommendation.**
- The cap is acceptable given the bodily injury/death carve-out, but consider negotiating a higher cap (e.g., 2× total fees) or adding a carve-out for regulatory fines and penalties.
- Ensure Seles's own insurance program (which it must obtain post-Closing, since Seller's policies are Excluded Assets under APA § 2.2(c)) covers gaps in the TSA cap.

## Issue 7 – TSA "No Obligation to Expand" May Constrain Seles During Transition

**Documents:** TSA § 2.7

**The problem.** Provider is not required to provide any Service "at a volume materially greater than the volume of the same function performed for the Business during the 12 months before the Closing, adjusted for any increase in the number of Covered Units under the Assigned Contracts."

**Why it matters.** If Seles wins new customers or adds units during the 6–9 month transition period, the dispatch and call-answing volume could exceed what Provider is obligated to handle. Provider could then argue it is not required to staff up or expand capacity, potentially degrading service quality at the worst time.

**Recommendation.**
- Negotiate a volume threshold (e.g., Provider must handle up to 120% of historical volume without additional charge) or a mechanism for Provider to scale capacity with reasonable notice.
- Alternatively, build contingency capacity into Seles's own operations plan so it is not fully dependent on Provider for dispatch during the transition.

## Issue 8 – Fenmark Uniform Rental Agreement: Limited Bearing, but Confirm Assigned Contract Status

**Documents:** Fenmark Agreement §§ 8.2, 2.4, 6.1; APA § 2.1(c)

**Analysis.** The Fenmark agreement is a routine vendor contract for uniform rental, laundering, and replacement. Its bearing on the deal is limited but not nil:

1. **Assignability.** Section 8.2 permits assignment "to a successor to all or substantially all of its business to which this Agreement relates, on written notice to the other party, if the successor assumes this Agreement in writing." No Fenmark consent is required. This is favorable.

2. **Minimum volume.** Section 2.4 requires at least 40 Wearers. If Seles hires fewer than 40 field employees, it still pays for 40.

3. **Term.** The initial 36-month term runs from June 3, 2024 to June 2, 2027, with automatic 12-month renewals. Seles would be locked in for at least 15 more months post-Closing (assuming a spring 2026 close).

4. **Assigned Contract status.** The APA defines Assigned Contracts as "all contracts listed in Section 2.1(c) of the Disclosure Schedule." We do not have the Disclosure Schedule. If the Fenmark agreement is listed, Seles assumes post-Closing payment obligations (weekly charges, loss/damage charges, etc.) under APA § 2.3(b). If it is not listed, it is an Excluded Asset under § 2.2(d), and Seles must arrange its own uniform program.

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the client whether the Fenmark agreement appears in Section 2.1(c) of the Disclosure Schedule.
- If it is an Assigned Contract, factor the 40-Wearer minimum and the remaining term into Seles's post-Closing operating budget.
- If it is not an Assigned Contract, confirm that Seles has a plan to onboard its own uniform vendor before the Fenmark contract lapses or is terminated by Tripux.

**Bottom line:** The Fenmark agreement does not create a material deal risk. It is a low-value vendor contract (~$40/Wearer/week × 40 minimum = ~$8,320/week ≈ $433,000/year) that is assignable without third-party consent. Its primary relevance is confirming the Assigned Contract list and budgeting for the minimum volume commitment.

## Issue 9 – Key Employee Agreement: Good Reason Trigger and Severance Exposure

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement §§ 5.2(b), 5.3

**The problem.** If Seles assumes the Key Employee Agreement (with Oyelaran's consent) and hires her, the "Good Reason" definition in § 5.2(b) includes "a material diminution in Executive's title, authority or duties." Post-Closing, Seles may reorganize field operations, change reporting lines, or modify Oyelaran's responsibilities. Any such change that Oyelaran characterizes as a "material diminution" could trigger a Good Reason resignation, entitling her to 12 months' base salary ($185,000), a pro-rated bonus, and COBRA premiums—totaling approximately **$230,000–$250,000**.

Additionally, § 3.3 provides a $20,000 retention payment on each March 1 anniversary during the Term. If Seles assumes the agreement, it owes this payment.

**Why it matters.** This is a manageable but real cost exposure. More importantly, if Oyelaran resigns for Good Reason, she is no longer bound by the employment-based covenants (though the post-employment restrictive covenants in § 6 would still run for 18 months from the end of employment).

**Recommendation.**
- Before Closing, agree with Oyelaran (as part of the consent negotiation) on her post-Closing title, reporting structure, and scope of duties to minimize Good Reason risk.
- Budget for the retention payment and potential severance in the deal model.

## Issue 10 – APA § 2.8 Safety Net Is Inadequate for the Key Employee Agreement

**Documents:** APA § 2.8

**The problem.** Section 2.8 provides that if an Assigned Contract cannot be assigned without a consent not obtained at Closing, "this Agreement does not constitute an assignment of that item" and Seller shall "hold that item for the benefit of Buyer and cooperate with Buyer in any lawful arrangement designed to provide Buyer its benefits."

As discussed in Issue 1, this provision is structurally inadequate for the Key Employee Agreement because:
- The agreement is personal to Oyelaran;
- The restrictive covenants protect "Company" (Tripux), not a successor;
- "Holding for the benefit of" Seles does not make Seles a party with enforcement rights;
- The automatic termination of covenants upon a "purported assignment" means even the act of trying to assign triggers the worst outcome.

**Recommendation.** This reinforces the need to resolve Issue 1 before Closing. The § 2.8 fallback should not be relied upon for the Key Employee Agreement.

## Issue 11 – Post-Closing Audit Timeline and Escrow Mechanics

**Documents:** APA §§ 9.1, 9.6; Escrow Agreement §§ 4.1–4.5

**The problem.** The interplay of the following provisions creates a compressed timeline for Seles:

| Event | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Notice General Rep claims (incl. § 4.12) | 90 days post-Closing (APA § 9.1) |
| Deliver Claim Notice to Escrow Agent | Before Indemnity Escrow Release Date (15 months post-Closing) (Escrow Agt § 4.1) |
| Seller to object to Claim Notice | 20 Business Days after Escrow Agent receipt (Escrow Agt § 4.3) |
| Special Escrow released to Seller (absent Final Order or § 3.4(c) notice) | 15 months post-Closing (Escrow Agt § 3.4(b)) |

Seles must complete its post-Closing audit of all Covered Units, identify any Section 4.12 breaches, and deliver written notice of claim **within 90 days**. It must then also deliver a formal Claim Notice to the Escrow Agent (with amount, contractual basis, and facts) to preserve the escrow as a source of recovery. These are separate steps under the documents: § 9.1 requires notice to the "Indemnifying Party" (Seller), while Escrow Agreement § 4.2 requires a Claim Notice to both Seller and the Escrow Agent.

**Recommendation.**
- Build a 90-day post-Closing audit plan into the integration timeline, starting on Day 1.
- Assign responsibility for the audit to a specific individual at Seles.
- Ensure that any claim noticed to Seller under § 9.1 is simultaneously structured as a Claim Notice under Escrow Agreement § 4.2 (stating amount, APA section, and facts) to preserve the escrow remedy.
- If the audit identifies issues on day 85+, escalate immediately to outside counsel to ensure timely delivery.

## Cross-Document Consistency Notes

- **Order of precedence (APA § 11.9):** The APA prevails over the Escrow Agreement and TSA in case of conflict. This is favorable to Seles, as the APA contains the broader indemnification framework.
- **Governing law and jurisdiction:** All four documents (APA, Escrow Agreement, TSA, Key Employee Agreement) are governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery (or Superior Court / federal court as fallback). The Fenmark agreement is also Delaware-governed but does not specify Chancery jurisdiction. No conflict.
- **Jury trial waiver:** Present in the APA, Escrow Agreement, and TSA. Absent from the Key Employee Agreement (which specifies "state and federal courts located in the State of Delaware" without a jury waiver). If a dispute arises under the Key Employee Agreement, a jury trial may be available.
- **Notice provisions:** Consistent across all documents (personal delivery, overnight courier, or email with confirmation). The Escrow Agreement adds that notices to the Escrow Agent are effective only on actual receipt (§ 9.1), which is standard.
