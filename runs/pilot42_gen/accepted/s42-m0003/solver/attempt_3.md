## Summary

The deal set consists of the Software License Agreement (March 1, 2024), a Side Letter (November 4, 2026), a Parent Guaranty from Fosais Holdings Corp. ## Issue 1: Non-Renewal Notice Likely Untimely — Agreement Auto-Renews

**Documents:** Side Letter §4.1–4.3; Agreement §14.1

**Facts.** The Initial Term ends February 28, 2027. Section 14.1 requires non-renewal notice "at least 120 days before the end of the then-current term." Counting back 120 days from February 28, 2027 yields October 31, 2026. Fosais delivered its notice on November 2, 2026—two days late. The Side Letter records Licensor's position that the notice is "of no effect" and the Agreement will renew for a three-year Renewal Term (through approximately February 28, 2030). Section 4.4 confirms neither party waives its position by executing the Side Letter.

**Why it matters for Fosais.** If the notice fails, Fosais is locked in for three more years with: (a) the full exclusivity obligation (Issue 2); (b) the 40-seat minimum (Issue 5); (c) continued parent guaranty coverage (Issue 4); and (d) exposure to up to 5% annual fee increases (Agreement §5.4). The commercial terms of the Side Letter (fee reduction, additional seats) do not cure the timing defect, and §16.2 gives the Agreement precedence over the Side Letter in any conflict.

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the client the exact date and method of delivery (email timestamp, courier receipt). If the email was sent on October 31 but the courier was received November 2, there may be an argument under §16.3 that the notice was "effective upon delivery by … email with confirmation of transmission." The Side Letter says it was delivered "by email to legal@dreitoul.com, with a copy by nationally recognized overnight courier." If the email was the primary delivery method and was sent on or before October 31, the notice may be timely. **This is the single most important fact to confirm immediately.**
- If the email was in fact sent November 2, assess whether Licensor's acceptance of the Side Letter (with its fee adjustments effective December 1, 2026) constitutes a waiver or estoppel. Section 4.4 makes this difficult, but Licensor's conduct of negotiating and signing a fee-reduction side letter two days after the missed deadline is factually inconsistent with its strict-timing position.
- If the notice is truly untimely and no waiver argument holds, consider whether to (a) litigate the renewal question in Delaware (expensive, uncertain), or (b) negotiate a mutual release from the Renewal Term as a condition of Fosais's continued performance under the Side Letter.

**Flag:** Needs resolution before Fosais performs under the renewed term or pays fees for the next billing cycle.

## Issue 2: Exclusivity Obligation Is Far Broader Than the Negotiated Deal

**Documents:** Agreement §4.3, §1.1(c), §1.1(l), §1.1(n); Side Letter Recital B

**Facts.** Section 4.3 prohibits Fosais from using, licensing, or developing any "Competing Product" for any "Solar Project" in the "Territory" during the Term. The definitions are sweeping: "Competing Product" covers any software providing solar design, panel layout, shading analysis, single-line diagram generation, or permit package preparation; "Solar Project" includes residential, commercial, industrial, community, and utility-scale; "Territory" is the entire United States and Canada.

Side Letter Recital B states that before signing, the parties' non-binding term sheet limited exclusivity to "residential rooftop projects in Arizona and Nevada only." The executed Agreement is dramatically broader.

**Why it matters for Fosais.** A solar installation company cannot realistically forego all competing design tools across two countries for the life of the agreement. If the term renews (Issue 1), this restriction extends to 2030. Even within the Initial Term, if Fosais wants to use a second design platform for utility-scale work or for projects outside AZ/NV, it is in breach. The exclusivity is also a condition of the pricing in the initial Order Form, so breach could give Licensor a termination right or a damages claim (which the parent guaranty would cover—see Issue 4).

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the client what the business understanding was at signing. If the term sheet scope (residential rooftop, AZ/NV) reflects the actual deal, this is a material discrepancy that may support a reformation or misrepresentation argument, though the non-binding nature of the term sheet weakens that.
- If the broader exclusivity was knowingly accepted, negotiate a carve-out for (a) utility-scale projects, (b) projects outside AZ/NV, or (c) a specific named competing product Fosais already uses.
- If the agreement renews, this becomes the central commercial constraint and should be the primary lever in any renegotiation.

## Issue 3: Licensee Indemnification Covers All Installations, With or Without the Platform

**Documents:** Agreement §11.2, §1.1(g)

**Facts.** Section 11.2 requires Fosais to indemnify Dreitoul against "any claim brought by a third party arising out of or relating to any Installation." Section 1.1(g) defines "Installation" as any solar system "designed, sold, installed, maintained or serviced by or on behalf of Licensee, **whether or not designed using the Platform**." The indemnity expressly covers bodily injury, death, property damage, roof or water damage, fire, electrical failure, code violations, and permitting deficiencies.

**Why it matters for Fosais.** This is not a software IP indemnity—it is a general liability indemnity for Fosais's entire construction business, running to its software vendor. A roof leak on a job that never touched HelioDraft triggers this clause. The indemnity is uncapped: Section 12.3(a) carves indemnification obligations out of the 12-month-fee liability cap. Combined with the Parent Guaranty (Issue 4), Fosais Holdings Corp. is on the hook as primary obligor for unlimited third-party construction claims.

**Recommendation.**
- Push to limit §11.2 to Installations actually designed using the Platform, or at minimum to claims arising from the Platform's output (designs, calculations, permit packages). The "whether or not designed using the Platform" language in §1.1(g) should be deleted or the indemnity should reference §8.3 (Licensee Outputs) rather than the broad "Installation" definition.
- If Licensor resists, request a sub-cap for the §11.2 indemnity (e.g., 2× annual fees) or require that Fosais's CGL policy (which it already carries at $2M per occurrence) be the exclusive funding source, with Dreitoul's rights limited to the policy proceeds.
- Confirm with the client whether its CGL policy actually covers the full range of claims listed (roof damage, electrical failure, code violations) and whether the $2M limit is adequate for its project portfolio.

## Issue 4: Parent Guaranty Amplifies All of the Above

**Documents:** Parent Guaranty §§1.2, 2.1, 2.3, 3.1, 3.2, 4.1(d), 4.2

**Facts.** Fosais Holdings Corp. guarantees "all obligations, liabilities and indebtedness" of the Licensee, including the full §11.2 indemnity. The guaranty is:
- As **primary obligor**, not merely surety (§2.1);
- A **guaranty of payment**, not collection—Dreitoul need not proceed against Fosais LLC first (§2.3);
- **Continuing**—it survives the Agreement and covers obligations that arise after renewal (§3.1);
- **Unaffected by renewal** (§3.2(a))—so if the auto-renewal in Issue 1 takes effect, the guaranty automatically extends;
- Subject to a **waiver of marshaling** (§4.1(d))—Dreitoul can go after the parent's assets before touching the LLC's;
- With **subrogation suspended** until all obligations are paid in full (§4.2).

**Why it matters for Fosais.** The parent corporation is exposed to unlimited, primary, non-contingent liability for the LLC's indemnification obligations (which are themselves uncapped and cover the entire construction business per Issue 3). If the agreement renews, this exposure extends three more years without any new action by the parent.

**Recommendation.**
- This is a structural issue that should have been addressed at signing. At this point, the practical lever is to fix the underlying indemnity (Issue 3), which reduces the guaranty's exposure correspondingly.
- Confirm with the client whether Fosais Holdings Corp. has other subsidiaries or assets that would be at risk if the guaranty were called. The waiver of marshaling means Dreitoul can pursue the parent directly.
- If renegotiating, request that the guaranty be limited to payment obligations (fees) and exclude the indemnification, or that it be subject to the same cap as the Agreement.

## Issue 5: Minimum Seat Count Creates a Fee Trap

**Documents:** Side Letter §2.3

**Facts.** The reduced rate of $2,160/seat is conditioned on maintaining at least 40 active seats. If seats drop below 40, the fee "immediately" reverts to the pre-adjustment rate ($2,400) from the first day of the following calendar month. There is no cure period, no pro-ration of the difference, and no cap on the number of months the higher rate applies.

**Why it matters for Fosais.** If the company downsizes its engineering staff (e.g., after a slow quarter or a project pipeline shift), it loses the fee reduction retroactively to the start of the month. The "immediately" language and the lack of a cure period make this a penalty, not a true minimum commitment. If the agreement renews (Issue 1), this condition persists.

**Recommendation.**
- Negotiate a cure period (e.g., 60 days to restore the seat count before the fee reverts) or a pro-rated adjustment rather than a full-month reversion.
- Clarify whether "active Seats" means seats with logged-in Authorized Users or simply seats that have not been formally returned. The Agreement's definition of "Authorized User" (§1.1(b)) ties to assignment under §2.3, but "active" is not defined.
- Confirm with the client current seat count and whether 40 is a realistic floor given headcount plans.

## Issue 6: Data Return Window May Be Inadequate

**Documents:** Agreement §7.4

**Facts.** Upon expiration or termination, Licensor makes Licensee Data available for export for only 30 days, after which it may delete the data.

**Why it matters for Fosais.** Solar projects have long permitting and construction timelines. If the agreement ends (or is terminated for cause), Fosais may have projects mid-permit or mid-construction that rely on Platform-generated designs, single-line diagrams, and permit packages. Thirty days to identify, export, and migrate all project data across potentially dozens of concurrent installations is tight. If data is lost, Fosais may need to recreate designs from scratch, with associated engineering costs and schedule delays.

**Recommendation.**
- Request an extension to 60 or 90 days, particularly for data relating to Installations that are in active construction or permitting at the time of termination.
- Confirm the export format. "Commonly used format" is vague. For CAD-based solar design, Fosais will need DWG, PDF, and possibly native file formats. Confirm with the client what formats are operationally necessary.
- If the agreement renews, this becomes less urgent but should still be addressed in any renegotiation.

## Issue 7: Confirm Licensor's Insurance Compliance

**Documents:** Agreement §13.2, §13.3

**Facts.** Dreitoul is required to maintain technology E&O and cyber liability insurance of not less than $2M per claim. No certificate from Dreitoul appears in the file. Section 13.3 requires certificates within 30 days of the Effective Date and upon each renewal "upon the other party's written request."

**Why it matters for Fosais.** If the Platform has a data breach or a software defect that causes a design error leading to a field incident, Fosais's first recourse is the §10.2 warranty (limited to correction or refund) and the §11.1 IP indemnity (which does not cover performance failures). The E&O/cyber policy is the practical source of recovery for a software-caused loss. If Dreitoul is not actually carrying the required coverage, Fosais's protection is illusory.

**Recommendation.**
- Send a written request to Dreitoul under §13.3 for a current certificate of insurance evidencing the §13.2 coverage. This is a low-friction ask that should be made regardless of the other issues.
- Confirm the policy is occurrence-based (not claims-made) or, if claims-made, that it includes a tail or extended reporting period.

## Issue 8: Side Letter Recital B — Discrepancy With Executed Agreement

**Documents:** Side Letter Recital B; Agreement §4.3

**Facts.** Recital B states the parties' pre-signing term sheet limited exclusivity to "residential rooftop projects in Arizona and Nevada only." The executed Agreement is far broader (all project types, all of US and Canada). The term sheet is described as "non-binding."

**Why it matters.** This does not create a standalone legal claim (non-binding term sheets are not enforceable), but it is relevant to: (a) any argument about the parties' actual agreement on exclusivity scope; (b) potential reformation or unconscionability arguments if the notice dispute goes to litigation; and (c) the client's internal understanding of what it agreed to.

**Recommendation.**
- Confirm with the client who negotiated the term sheet and whether the expansion from "residential rooftop in AZ/NV" to "all projects in US/Canada" was a deliberate business decision or a drafting error that went unnoticed.
- If it was a drafting error, document the client's understanding now while it is fresh. This may support a reformation argument if the non-renewal dispute escalates.
