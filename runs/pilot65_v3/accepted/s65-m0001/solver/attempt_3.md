## I. CRITICAL ISSUES

### Issue 1: Distribution Rights Gap Between the Rights Bill of Sale and the Stock Purchase Agreement

**Where:** Bill of Sale § 1.2 (definition of "Distribution Rights") and § 2.1 (conveyance); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Distribution Rights") and § 4.9(b) (representation of ownership).

**Description:** The SPA defines "Distribution Rights" broadly to include "all media, formats and methods of delivery now known or later devised, including theatrical, non-theatrical, physical home video, television (free, pay and basic), and all forms of on-demand and internet-delivered exhibition, including subscription, advertising-supported and transactional video-on-demand" (SPA § 1.1). Section 4.9(b) represents that, under the Rights Bill of Sale, "Rights Holdings conveyed to the Company all of the Distribution Rights in each Library Title held by Rights Holdings," and that the Company "owns or holds... the Distribution Rights in each Library Title" as of the Closing.

However, the Bill of Sale defines "Distribution Rights" narrowly as "the theatrical, non-theatrical and physical home-video rights in and to that Picture" (Bill of Sale § 1.2), and Section 2.1 conveys only those rights. The Bill of Sale does not convey television, digital, streaming (SVOD/AVOD/TVOD), or any other non-theatrical/non-physical-home-video rights. Section 2.2 assigns the Acquisition Agreements only "to the extent relating to the Distribution Rights" (i.e., the narrow Bill of Sale definition).

**Why it matters:** Subirt is paying a Base Purchase Price of $28,500,000 for a company it believes owns full distribution rights—including digital and streaming—in 41 films. If the Company only holds theatrical, non-theatrical, and physical home-video rights, the most valuable and fastest-growing revenue streams (streaming, digital) may not have been conveyed to the Company. This would render the § 4.9(b) representation inaccurate and could materially diminish the value of the acquisition. It also raises the question of who currently holds the un-conveyed rights (likely Rights Holdings or its upstream licensors), creating potential chain-of-title gaps.

**Recommended fix:** Before Closing, (a) confirm with Seller whether Rights Holdings held broader rights (TV, digital, streaming) in any Library Title and, if so, require a supplemental bill of sale or assignment conveying those rights to the Company prior to or at Closing; (b) if the broader rights were never held by Rights Holdings, require Seller to identify the actual rights holder and provide evidence of the Company's ownership or license; (c) in the alternative, if the parties intend the narrower rights package, amend the SPA definition of "Distribution Rights" and the § 4.9 representations to conform, and adjust the purchase price accordingly.

### Issue 2: General Representations Survive Only Nine Months (Tied to Note Maturity Date)

**Where:** SPA § 9.1(a) (survival of General Representations); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Note Maturity Date"); Seller Note § 2 (Maturity Date = 9 months after Issue Date).

**Description:** Section 9.1(a) provides that General Representations survive "until the earlier of (i) eighteen (18) months after the Closing Date and (ii) the Note Maturity Date." The Note Maturity Date is defined by reference to the Seller Note, which sets the Maturity Date at nine (9) months after the Issue Date (i.e., the Closing Date). Because 9 months is earlier than 18 months, all General Representations effectively survive for only **nine months** after Closing.

General Representations include § 4.5 (Financial Statements), § 4.6 (Absence of Certain Changes), § 4.7 (Material Contracts), § 4.8 (Litigation), § 4.9 (Library Titles; Distribution Rights), § 4.10 (Participations and Residuals), § 4.11 (Intellectual Property), § 4.13 (Employees), § 4.14 (Compliance with Laws), § 4.16 (Affiliate Arrangements), and § 4.17 (Bank Accounts). These are the representations most likely to give rise to post-Closing claims.

**Why it matters:** Nine months is an extremely short period to discover and assert claims for misstated financials, undisclosed litigation, defective chain of title, or unaccrued participations/residuals. The working capital true-up itself takes 90 days to prepare and up to 75 additional days to resolve disputes (SPA § 2.3(b)–(c)). If the Distribution Rights issue (Issue 1) is not resolved before Closing, Subirt may have less than nine months to investigate, quantify, and assert a claim—and that claim would be subject to the 12% Seller Cap ($3,420,000) and the $213,750 Deductible (SPA § 9.5(a)–(b)).

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate to decouple the General Representations survival from the Note Maturity Date. The standard formulation is 18 months (or 12 months for financial reps, 18 months for others). If Seller resists, at minimum extend survival to 12 months for § 4.9 (Library Titles) and § 4.5 (Financial Statements), given the heightened risk on those points. Alternatively, if the Note Maturity Date tie is a deliberate trade-off for the subordination and set-off rights, document that understanding and consider whether the short survival is acceptable given the specific risk profile.

## II. SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### Issue 3: Seller Note Governed by Delaware Law; All Other Documents Governed by New York Law

**Where:** Seller Note § 10.1 (Delaware governing law); SPA § 11.8 (New York); TSA § 11.6 (New York); Bill of Sale § 9.6 (New York).

**Description:** The Seller Note is governed by Delaware law and subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Delaware Court of Chancery (Seller Note § 10.2). Every other transaction document is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County.

**Why it matters:** If a dispute arises that implicates both the SPA (e.g., a set-off claim under § 9.8) and the Seller Note (e.g., whether the set-off constitutes a payment default), the parties could face conflicting choice-of-law analyses and potentially parallel proceedings in different jurisdictions. Delaware and New York differ on issues such as the enforceability of set-off rights against negotiable instruments, the standards for specific performance, and the treatment of subordination agreements.

**Recommended fix:** Conform the Seller Note's governing law and jurisdiction to New York, consistent with the SPA and the other Ancillary Agreements. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps because it is a Delaware LP), at minimum add a provision to the SPA stating that, in the event of a conflict between the SPA and the Seller Note regarding set-off or indemnification, the SPA's New York governing law controls for the SPA-based claim and the Note's Delaware law controls only for the Note's payment terms.

### Issue 4: Seller Note Change-of-Control Default Restricts Subirt's Strategic Flexibility

**Where:** Seller Note § 8.1(f).

**Description:** An Event of Default occurs if "Maker sells, transfers or otherwise disposes of a majority of the outstanding capital stock of the Company, or the Company sells all or substantially all of its assets, in either case to a Person that is not an Affiliate of Maker."

**Why it matters:** Subirt (Maker) owns Nightjar (the Company). If Subirt wishes to (a) sell Nightjar to a third party, (b) contribute Nightjar's shares to a joint venture or new holding company that is not an "Affiliate" under the SPA definition, or (c) have Nightjar sell substantially all of its assets (e.g., in a library sale), the Seller Note accelerates. With $6,000,000 principal plus accrued interest becoming immediately due, this could constrain Subirt's ability to restructure or exit the investment within the 9-month Note term. The "Affiliate" carve-out helps only if the transferee is under common control with Subirt.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate to (a) extend the cure period for this default (currently none—acceleration is immediate under § 8.2 for a § 8.1(f) default, though § 8.2 only provides automatic acceleration for § 8.1(d) Bankruptcy Events; for § 8.1(f), Holder must deliver written notice); (b) add a carve-out for reorganizations among Subirt and its Affiliates that do not involve a change of ultimate beneficial ownership; or (c) add a prepayment right triggered by such a transaction so Subirt can pay off the Note rather than face a default.

### Issue 5: Transition Services Agreement Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for Collection Accounts Risk

**Where:** TSA § 9.1 (liability cap); TSA § 1.1 (definition of "Collection Accounts"); TSA § 2.4 (Post-Closing Receipts).

**Description:** The TSA caps Provider's (Fural's) aggregate liability at "the total Service Fees paid in the six (6) months immediately preceding the event giving rise to the claim" (TSA § 9.1). At $42,500 per month, the cap is approximately $255,000. During the transition, Fural's Collection Accounts hold all revenue from the Library Titles (exhibitor, platform, and sub-distributor payments). If Fural misapplies, misdirects, or fails to remit Post-Closing Receipts, Subirt's recovery under the TSA is limited to ~$255,000.

The SPA provides a separate remedy for failure to remit Post-Closing Receipts under § 9.2(d) (uncapped, no deductible), but that claim is against Seller (Fural Media Partners, L.P.) under the SPA, while the TSA claim is against Provider (also Fural Media Partners, L.P.) under the TSA. The SPA's § 11.12 provides that the TSA "shall govern with respect to the Services," which could be read to limit Subirt to the TSA's lower cap for Collection Accounts issues.

**Why it matters:** Monthly revenue from 41 films across theatrical, digital, and home-video could substantially exceed $255,000. A misdirection or commingling event at Fural's Collection Accounts could result in losses far exceeding the TSA cap.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Add an express carve-out from the TSA § 9.1 cap for Provider's failure to remit Post-Closing Receipts in accordance with § 2.4, making such claims subject to the SPA's uncapped indemnity under § 9.2(d); or (b) increase the TSA cap to a multiple of the Monthly Service Fee (e.g., 12 months of fees) or a fixed dollar amount tied to the expected revenue flow; or (c) require Fural to maintain a trust or segregated account for Collection Accounts balances with a minimum balance covenant.

### Issue 6: Seller Note Freely Transferable Without Subirt's Consent

**Where:** Seller Note § 9.1.

**Description:** "Holder may sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, or any interest in it, in whole or in part, to any Person without the consent of Maker."

**Why it matters:** Fural can sell or pledge the $6,000,000 Note to any third party—potentially a distressed debt fund, a competitor, or an entity with whom Subirt has an adverse relationship—without Subirt's approval. The transferee becomes "Holder" with all rights under the Note, including the information rights in § 6 (monthly financials, title-by-title performance, box-office and streaming revenue reports). This could compromise Subirt's confidentiality and competitive position.

**Recommended fix:** Add a requirement that any transferee (a) be an "accredited investor" or institutional holder, (b) agree in writing to be bound by the confidentiality provisions of the SPA (§ 6.4) and the Note, and (c) not be a competitor of Subirt or Nightjar in the independent film distribution space. Alternatively, require Subirt's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) to any transfer.

### Issue 7: Board Consent Determines Acquisition Is Not a "Major Decision" Requiring Member Approval

**Where:** Buyer Board Consent § 11.1; SPA § 5.2 (Authority; Enforceability).

**Description:** The Board resolved that "the Acquisition does not constitute a 'Major Decision' requiring the approval of the members of the Company under Section 7.3 of the Operating Agreement." This determination was made "on the advice of the General Counsel."

**Why it matters:** If the Operating Agreement's definition of "Major Decision" encompasses acquisitions above a certain threshold (and $28.5M likely qualifies), the Board's determination could be incorrect. A defective authorization would undermine the representation in SPA § 5.2 that Buyer's execution "has been duly authorized by all necessary limited liability company action." While § 5.2 is a Fundamental Representation (surviving until 60 days after the statute of limitations expires), a breach would give Seller a claim against Subirt under § 9.3(a). More practically, it could create internal governance disputes among Subirt's members.

**Recommended fix:** Before Closing, obtain a written opinion from the General Counsel (or outside counsel) confirming that the Acquisition does not trigger the "Major Decision" threshold under Section 7.3 of the Operating Agreement, or obtain member approval if it does. Review the Operating Agreement definition of "Major Decision" against the transaction terms.

## III. MODERATE ISSUES

### Issue 8: Seller Note Information Rights Provide Fural Extensive Operational Visibility

**Where:** Seller Note § 6.1 (monthly reports), § 6.4 (additional information).

**Description:** For 9 months, Subirt must deliver to Fural monthly unaudited financial statements, title-by-title performance reports (gross receipts, distribution fees, recoupable expenses, participations and residuals by territory and media), box-office reports by exhibitor, and streaming/digital revenue reports by platform. Section 6.4 additionally requires Subirt to furnish "such additional information... as Holder may reasonably request."

**Why it matters:** This level of granular, title-by-title operational data gives Fural—despite the non-compete in SPA § 6.5—detailed insight into which titles are performing, which platforms are generating revenue, and what the margins look like. If Fural invests in or acquires another film distribution business, this information could be competitively harmful. The "reasonably request" standard in § 6.4 is open-ended.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Limit § 6.4 to information necessary for Fural to assess its credit exposure under the Note (i.e., material adverse changes in the Company's financial condition); (b) add a confidentiality covenant in the Note restricting Fural's use of the information to evaluating the Note and prohibiting disclosure to third parties (other than advisors under NDA); (c) consider whether the title-by-title and platform-level detail is necessary for a 9-month, $6M note and negotiate to reduce it to aggregate-level reporting.

### Issue 9: Funds Flow Memorandum – Ambiguous Reference to Post-Closing Receipts Account

**Where:** Funds Flow Memorandum § 10.4; Schedule 1, Wire 6.

**Description:** Section 10.4 designates the account for Post-Closing Receipts as "the Company operating account identified as Wire 6's receiving bank in Schedule 1, under the account name 'Nightjar Releasing, Inc. – Operating.'" However, Wire 6 in Schedule 1 is to "Nightjar Releasing, Inc. – Payroll" at Westbrook Federal Bank, account ending -1173. The "Operating" account at Westbrook Federal Bank is a different account from the "Payroll" account referenced in Wire 6.

**Why it matters:** If the account designation is ambiguous, there is a risk that Post-Closing Receipts are directed to the wrong account (payroll rather than operating), or that the designation letter delivered to Seller at Closing is contested. This could delay the flow of revenue to the Company and create friction with Seller during the transition.

**Recommended fix:** Revise § 10.4 to identify the Post-Closing Receipts account by its full bank name, ABA number, and account number (or masked account number with the full number provided separately in the verified designation letter). Confirm that the "Nightjar Releasing, Inc. – Operating" account at Westbrook Federal Bank is in fact the intended recipient and that it is distinct from the payroll account in Wire 6.

### Issue 10: Press Release Forward-Looking Statement on Continuing Acquisitions Program

**Where:** Press Release § 3.2 ("Subirt plans to... continue Nightjar's acquisitions program"); § 7.5(b) ("Yes. Subirt intends to continue Nightjar's acquisitions program and to support it with Subirt's resources.").

**Description:** The approved press release and spokesperson guidance commit Subirt publicly to continuing Nightjar's film acquisitions program.

**Why it matters:** If Subirt later decides to pause or wind down acquisitions (for strategic, financial, or market reasons), the public statement could create expectations with filmmakers, sales agents, and industry partners that are difficult to manage. It also could be cited in a dispute with Seller if Subirt's post-Closing conduct of the business is challenged under the SPA covenants.

**Recommended fix:** Soften the language to "Subirt intends to evaluate opportunities to continue Nightjar's acquisitions program" or "Subirt supports the Nightjar team's acquisitions activities." Ensure the spokesperson guidance in § 7.5(b) is consistent.

### Issue 11: Dual Role of Jonah Whitcombe (Subirt CFO / Nightjar Director)

**Where:** Buyer Board Consent § 7.2(b) (elected as Nightjar director); Annex 1 (identified as Subirt's Chief Financial Officer); Funds Flow Memorandum Schedule 3 (designated as the person who releases wires).

**Description:** Jonah Whitcombe serves simultaneously as Subirt's CFO (responsible for releasing Closing wires, preparing the Closing Statement, and administering the Seller Note) and as a director of Nightjar (the acquired company).

**Why it matters:** While common in parent-subsidiary structures, the dual role creates potential conflicts: as Nightjar's director, Whitcombe owes fiduciary duties to Nightjar; as Subirt's CFO, he is implementing Subirt's financial decisions (including the Seller Note payments and set-off elections). If a dispute arises between Subirt and Nightjar (e.g., over allocation of resources or the exercise of set-off rights), Whitcombe's position could be challenged.

**Recommended fix:** Document in the Board Consent or a separate resolution that Whitcombe's primary role in connection with the transaction is as Subirt's CFO, and that his service as a Nightjar director is in his capacity as Subirt's designee. Consider whether Nightjar's bylaws or certificate of incorporation require any disclosure of this relationship.

### Issue 12: TSA Counterparty Is Nightjar, Not Subirt

**Where:** TSA preamble and § 11.10 (no third-party beneficiaries other than indemnified Persons); SPA § 3.3(c) (Company executes TSA at Buyer's direction).

**Description:** The TSA is between Fural (Provider) and Nightjar (Recipient). Subirt is not a party. Subirt's rights are indirect—it must direct Nightjar to enforce the TSA. Section 11.10 confirms that no Person other than the Parties and indemnified Persons has rights under the TSA. However, § 11.9 permits Recipient to assign its rights to Buyer or any Affiliate without Provider's consent.

**Why it matters:** If Nightjar fails to enforce the TSA (e.g., if there is a dispute about whether a Service was performed to the Applicable Standard), Subirt must act through Nightjar. While the assignment right in § 11.9 provides a backstop, it has not been exercised as of Closing.

**Recommended fix:** Have Nightjar assign its rights under the TSA to Subirt (or to Subirt and Nightjar jointly) at or promptly after Closing, pursuant to § 11.9. This gives Subirt direct standing to enforce the TSA and to seek specific performance under § 11.8.
