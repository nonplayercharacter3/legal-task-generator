## Summary

Review of the five documents in the matter folder reveals several material departures from the commercial deal as reflected in the non-binding term sheet, a proposed amendment that narrows Mubart's protections, and an Order Form that significantly expands Zinix's stale-product credit rights. The most urgent issues are the exclusivity scope, the redefined stale-product regime, and the "regardless of cause" indemnification. Each is addressed below in order of significance.

## Issue 1 – Exclusivity Is Far Broader Than Negotiated

**Where:** Supply Agreement §7.1; compare Term Sheet §§4.1 and 4.3.

**The problem.** The term sheet (the document that reflects the commercial deal the parties actually discussed) scoped supplier exclusivity two ways:

- **Product scope:** "private-label sandwich bread and buns" only (Term Sheet §4.1).
- **Geographic scope:** Delaware, Maryland, and Pennsylvania only (Term Sheet §4.1).
- **Business carve-out:** "Mubart's branded products, foodservice business and other wholesale customers are outside the scope of Section 4.1" (Term Sheet §4.3).

The Supply Agreement §7.1 strips out all three limitations:

> "Supplier shall not, directly or indirectly, including through any Affiliate, manufacture, sell or supply **any bakery products** to **any other grocery retailer** **anywhere in the United States**."

No carve-out for branded products, foodservice, or other wholesale channels appears anywhere in the Supply Agreement.

**Why it matters.** If Mubart makes any other bakery products (muffins, pastries, tortillas, etc.) or sells to grocery retailers outside the three-state footprint, or serves foodservice or other wholesale customers, it is in breach of §7.1 the moment it does so. A breach gives Zinix termination rights under §15.1 and potentially damages. More practically, it chills Mubart's ability to grow any other line during the three-year Initial Term (through March 2, 2029) and any Renewal Terms.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Redline §7.1** to restore the term-sheet scope: limit to "private-label sandwich bread and bun products" (matching the defined "Products" in §1.7), limit geography to "the states of Delaware, Maryland and Pennsylvania," and add a new sentence: "Supplier's branded products, foodservice business, and other non-grocery-retail wholesale customers are excluded from this Section 7.1."
- **Follow-up:** Confirm with Mubart what other product lines, channels, and geographies it currently serves or plans to serve. If Mubart has, for example, a branded bread line sold to a different regional grocer in New Jersey, the current §7.1 is already a live breach.

**Needs Mubart input:** What bakery products does Mubart make beyond private-label bread and buns? What channels and geographies does it serve? Has it supplied any other grocery retailer since March 2, 2026?

## Issue 2 – Stale Product Regime in Order Form No. 1 Is Radically Expanded

**Where:** Order Form No. 1 §§3.1–3.2; compare Supply Agreement §§1.9 and 5.4.

**The problem.** Three changes work together to give Zinix a near-unlimited credit right:

| Element | Supply Agreement | Order Form No. 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | "Product unsold after its code date **and returned to Supplier within 48 hours** after that Code Date" (§1.9) | "any Product that is unsold at its Code Date **or is damaged or unsaleable in store**" (§3.1) |
| Return requirement | "Supplier shall collect Stale Product from each Delivery Point on its regular delivery route" (§5.4) | Credit issued "**without return**" (§3.2) |
| Reporting / verification | Not specified beyond collection | "Customer shall report Stale Product quantities by Delivery Point to Supplier **weekly through Customer's store reporting system**" (§3.2) |

Under the Order Form, Zinix can claim a full-price credit for any loaf or bun that is (a) past its code date, (b) damaged in Zinix's store (however and by whom), or (c) "unsaleable in store" for any reason Zinix asserts—without returning the product and based solely on Zinix's own system records. There is no Mubart audit right, no photo documentation requirement, no cap, and no dispute mechanism specific to stale-product credits.

**Why it matters.** Bread and buns are high-volume, low-margin items. If Zinix's stores generate even a modest "unsaleable" or "damaged" rate (say 5–10% of units), Mubart absorbs that cost with no product recovery and no verification. The "without return" language also eliminates any residual value (e.g., donation, animal feed, destruction cost savings). Over a three-year term this can become a material P&L item.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Redline Order Form §3.1** to conform to the Supply Agreement definition: "Stale Product" means Product unsold after its Code Date and returned to Supplier within 48 hours after that Code Date. Delete "or is damaged or unsaleable in store."
- **Redline Order Form §3.2** to require return of product (or at minimum, photographic evidence and a weekly reconciliation meeting) and to give Supplier a right to audit stale-product reports on reasonable notice.
- **If Zinix resists** (and it likely will, since this was probably a negotiated concession in the Order Form), fall back to: (a) capping stale-product credits at a percentage of weekly volume (e.g., 3%); (b) requiring item-level documentation; (c) reserving the right to dispute credits under §4.4's 15-day notice mechanism.
- **Follow-up:** Ask Mubart's operations team what stale-product rates they have experienced with other customers, so we can set a defensible cap.

**Needs Mubart input:** What stale-product credit rates are Mubart comfortable absorbing? Does Mubart have historical data from other private-label relationships?

## Issue 3 – Supplier Indemnification Is "Regardless of Cause"

**Where:** Supply Agreement §10.1; compare Term Sheet §10.1.

**The problem.** The term sheet limited Mubart's indemnity to:

> "third-party claims arising from Products that **fail to meet the product warranties**, including product liability claims and recall costs **caused by those failures**."

The Supply Agreement §10.1 is:

> "Supplier shall defend, indemnify and hold harmless Customer… from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to the Products, including all costs of any recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery and any claim for personal injury, illness or death, **regardless of cause**."

"Regardless of cause" eliminates the causal link between a product defect and the claim. It could be read to require Mubart to indemnify Zinix for, e.g., a customer injury that is actually caused by Zinix's own display, handling, or a third party, so long as the product was "involved." Combined with §11.3(a) (which, in the current Supply Agreement, excludes indemnification from the liability cap), Mubart's exposure is theoretically unlimited.

**Why it matters.** In a product-liability or recall scenario, the "regardless of cause" language means Mubart must defend and indemnify even if the root cause is Zinix's post-delivery handling (which §10.2(c) says Zinix should indemnify Mubart for). The two indemnities would collide, and "regardless of cause" gives Mubart little argument that the claim is outside its scope.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Redline §10.1** to delete "regardless of cause" and insert "to the extent caused by or resulting from a defect in, or non-conformity of, the Products with the Specifications or applicable law."
- **Alternatively**, add a carve-out: "provided that Supplier shall not be liable under this Section 10.1 for Losses to the extent caused by Customer's storage, handling, display, or sale of Products after delivery, or by Customer Materials."
- **Coordinate with Issue 4 below** (the Amendment), because the Amendment's narrowing of §11.3(a) interacts with this.

**Needs Mubart input:** Confirm Mubart's product liability insurance tower (limits, self-insured retention, coverage for "regardless of cause" scenarios) so we can assess whether the current language is insurable at acceptable cost.

## Issue 4 – Proposed Amendment No. 1 Narrows Mubart's Uncapped Indemnification Recovery

**Where:** Proposed Amendment No. 1, §2 (amending Supply Agreement §11.3(a)).

**The problem.** Current Supply Agreement §11.3(a) excludes "each party's indemnification obligations under Section 10" from the liability cap. That means both Mubart's and Zinix's indemnification duties are uncapped.

The Amendment replaces §11.3(a) with:

> "(a) **Supplier's** indemnification obligations under **Section 10.1**;"

Two changes:
1. It drops "each party's" and substitutes "Supplier's"—so **Customer's indemnification under §10.2 is no longer excluded from the cap.**
2. It references only §10.1 (not §10 generally)—confirming the narrowing.

**Why it matters.** Section 10.2 is Zinix's indemnity to Mubart for: (a) IP infringement from Customer Materials; (b) labeling non-compliance in Customer Materials; and (c) post-delivery storage, handling, display, or sale. If a Zinix-supplied label is misbranded (e.g., missing allergen statement, false health claim) and a third party sues Mubart, Mubart's recovery from Zinix under §10.2 would now be **capped at 12 months of payments** (per §11.2). For a high-volume bread program, that cap may be insufficient to cover a serious labeling or IP claim.

Meanwhile, Mubart's own indemnification (§10.1) remains uncapped—so the Amendment creates an asymmetry: Mubart's exposure is unlimited, but its recovery from Zinix is capped.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Do not accept the Amendment as drafted.** Redline §2 to restore the original language: "(a) each party's indemnification obligations under Section 10."
- **If Zinix's position is that it will not accept uncapped indemnification for Customer Materials** (a reasonable commercial ask), propose a middle ground: exclude Customer's indemnification under §10.2(a) and (b) (IP and labeling) from the cap, but allow §10.2(c) (post-delivery handling) to remain subject to the cap. Post-delivery handling claims are typically smaller and more insurable.
- **Negotiating position:** Make clear that Mubart will not agree to an amendment that increases Mubart's uncapped exposure while simultaneously capping Mubart's uncapped recovery. The two changes should be addressed together.

**Note:** The Amendment is marked "For Discussion Purposes Only" and is a Zinix draft. It has not been signed. We should respond in kind with a counter-draft or a letter of objection before it is circulated further.

## Issue 5 – Governing Law Conflict: Order Form Says New Jersey, Supply Agreement Says Delaware

**Where:** Order Form No. 1 §6; Supply Agreement §18.2 and §18.4.

**The problem.** The Supply Agreement §18.2 selects Delaware law and exclusive jurisdiction in New Castle County, Delaware. The Order Form §6 selects New Jersey law and exclusive jurisdiction in Bergen County, New Jersey, for "Disputes arising under this Order Form or relating to the Products ordered under it."

Under the order of precedence in Supply Agreement §18.4(b), the Order Form prevails over the Supply Agreement "as to the Products ordered under it." So for any dispute involving the ZG-101 through ZG-202 products, New Jersey law and Bergen County courts govern.

**Why it matters.** This is not a trivial venue choice. New Jersey and Delaware differ on issues that could arise here:

- **Consequential damages waivers:** New Jersey courts are generally enforceable but may read them differently in the food-industry context.
- **Indemnification scope:** New Jersey's "regardless of cause" analysis may differ from Delaware's.
- **Stale-product / credit disputes:** New Jersey's commercial code and case law on self-reported credits may be less favorable to the supplier.
- **Practical:** Bergen County is closer to Zinix's apparent headquarters; New Castle County is neutral.

It is also possible this was a Zinix drafting error or a holdover from a prior negotiation. The term sheet §11.1 clearly said "Delaware law; state or federal courts in New Castle County, Delaware."

**Recommended fix.**

- **Raise with Zinix immediately.** Ask whether §6 of the Order Form was intentional. If it was a drafting error, execute a corrected Order Form or a side letter conforming §6 to the Supply Agreement.
- **If Zinix insists on New Jersey**, assess the practical impact with local NJ counsel before accepting. At minimum, ensure that the indemnification and limitation-of-liability provisions are drafted to be enforceable under both Delaware and New Jersey law.
- **Do not let this slide.** A governing-law mismatch between the master agreement and the Order Form creates litigation risk and complicates any dispute.

## Issue 6 – Stale Product Credits Lack a Dispute Mechanism

**Where:** Order Form No. 1 §3.2; Supply Agreement §4.4.

**The problem.** Order Form §3.2 provides that Zinix reports stale-product quantities "through Customer's store reporting system" and credits are "applied against the next weekly invoice." There is no mechanism for Mubart to dispute a stale-product credit before it is applied. The general invoice-dispute provision in §4.4 (15 days to notify of a disputed amount) technically covers this, but the 15-day window runs from receipt of the invoice—by which time the credit has already been netted out and the invoice is for a lower amount. Mubart would be disputing a credit it never saw itemized.

**Why it matters.** Combined with Issue 2 (broadened definition, no return, self-reporting), the absence of a specific dispute right means Mubart may discover inflated stale-product credits only when its revenue is lower than expected, and the contractual remedy is muddled.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Redline Order Form §3.2** to add: "Supplier shall have the right to review and dispute stale-product credit entries within [10] business days of the weekly report. Disputed entries shall be excluded from the invoice until resolved. Supplier may audit Customer's stale-product records on [30] days' notice, no more than [once per quarter]."
- **Alternatively**, add a standing audit right in the Supply Agreement (e.g., new §5.6) covering all credit and deduction entries.

## Issue 7 – Customer Commitment (80% Floor) Lacks a Remedy

**Where:** Supply Agreement §7.2; Order Form No. 1 §2.4.

**The problem.** Section 7.2 requires Zinix to purchase not less than 80% of its requirements for each Product category from Mubart. Section 2.4 of the Order Form defines the two categories (sandwich loaves; buns). But nowhere in the Agreement is there a specific remedy if Zinix falls below 80%. Mubart's only recourse would be to argue material breach under §15.1, which requires a 30-day cure period and is a high bar for a percentage shortfall.

**Why it matters.** The 80% commitment is Mubart's primary protection against Zinix sourcing the other 20% (or more) from a competitor while Mubart is locked into exclusivity under §7.1. If Zinix can drift below 80% without consequence, the exclusivity becomes a one-way street: Mubart cannot serve other grocers, but Zinix can source from others.

**Recommended fix.**

- **Add a make-whole or minimum-purchase provision.** For example: "If Customer's actual purchases in any contract year fall below 80% of its requirements for a Product category, Customer shall pay Supplier the difference between 80% of actual requirements and actual purchases, calculated at the Order Form price for the relevant Product."
- **Alternatively**, add a termination right: "If Customer fails to meet the 80% commitment for two consecutive contract years, Supplier may terminate the applicable Order Form on 60 days' notice."
- **Follow-up:** Confirm with Mubart whether it is comfortable relying on a general material-breach theory or wants an express remedy.

## Issue 8 – Force Majeure: Asymmetric Sourcing Right

**Where:** Supply Agreement §16.1.

**The problem.** The last sentence of §16.1 provides: "If a force majeure event continues for more than 30 days, the unaffected Party may obtain Products from other sources during the period of the event without breach of Section 7.2, and may terminate the affected Order Form if the event continues for more than 90 days."

This is drafted from the perspective of the party that *receives* products (i.e., Zinix). If Mubart is the party hit by force majeure (e.g., a flour supply disruption, a plant fire), Zinix can source from another bakery for 30+ days and terminate the Order Form at 90 days. There is no reciprocal protection for Mubart (e.g., a right to source ingredients from alternate suppliers without breaching Specifications, or a right to extend delivery windows).

**Why it matters.** For a bakery, the most likely force majeure events are ingredient supply failures (flour, oil), utility outages, or labor disputes at Mubart's own facility. The current language gives Zinix a clean exit at 90 days with no obligation to wait for Mubart to recover, and no make-whole for Mubart's fixed costs (packaging, tooling, dedicated line capacity).

**Recommended fix.**

- **Redline §16.1** to make the sourcing right mutual in form but practical in effect: clarify that "the unaffected Party" means the party not in breach, and add: "If Supplier is the affected Party, Customer shall not terminate the affected Order Form unless the force majeure event continues for more than [120] days, and Customer shall compensate Supplier for non-cancellable packaging and tooling costs incurred in preparation for the affected period."
- **Alternatively**, add a force majeure carve-out from the 80% commitment (already partially addressed by "without breach of Section 7.2") and from the exclusivity in §7.1 (so Mubart can serve other customers if Zinix sources elsewhere during a force majeure event).
