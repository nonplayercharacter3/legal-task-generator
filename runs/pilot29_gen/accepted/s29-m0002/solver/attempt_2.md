## Summary

The matter folder contains five documents: a non-binding term sheet (Jan. 19, 2026), the executed Supply Agreement (Mar. 2, 2026), Order Form No. 1 (Apr. 6, 2026), a delivery-schedule email thread (Apr. 20–21, 2026), and Zinix's draft Amendment No. 1 (June 15, 2026). ## Issue 1 — Exclusivity: Scope Expanded Far Beyond the Term Sheet

**Where it appears:**
- Term Sheet §4.1: Mubart will not supply *private-label sandwich bread and buns* in *Delaware, Maryland and Pennsylvania* to any other grocery retailer.
- Term Sheet §4.3: "Mubart's branded products, foodservice business and other wholesale customers are outside the scope of Section 4.1."
- Supply Agreement §7.1: "During the Term, Supplier shall not, directly or indirectly, including through any Affiliate, manufacture, sell or supply **any bakery products** to **any other grocery retailer anywhere in the United States**."

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
The executed Agreement strips out all three limitations that were in the term sheet. The product scope expands from "private-label sandwich bread and buns" to "any bakery products" (presumably including Mubart's branded items, foodservice items, and any other bakery line). The geographic scope expands from three states to the entire United States. The carve-out for Mubart's branded products, foodservice business, and other wholesale customers in Term Sheet §4.3 is absent. Read literally, §7.1 prohibits Mubart from selling *any* bakery product to *any* grocery retailer in the country for the full Term (three years plus auto-renewals). If Mubart has any existing or pipeline grocery-retailer customers outside DE/MD/PA, or any branded bakery products sold to grocery channels, this is a present or imminent breach.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Confirm with Mubart:** Does Mubart currently supply any bakery products (branded or private-label, any category) to any grocery retailer outside DE/MD/PA? If yes, §7.1 is already in breach and needs immediate correction.
- **Negotiating position:** Revert §7.1 to the term-sheet scope: limit to "private-label sandwich bread and buns," limit geography to the states where Zinix operates (or at minimum DE, MD, PA), and reinstate the carve-out for Mubart's branded products, foodservice business, and other non-grocery wholesale customers.
- **Redline language for §7.1:**
  > "During the Term, Supplier shall not, directly or indirectly, including through any Affiliate, manufacture, sell or supply private-label sandwich bread or bun products to any other grocery retailer in Delaware, Maryland or Pennsylvania. Supplier's branded products, foodservice business, and other wholesale customers outside the grocery retail channel are outside the scope of this Section 7.1."

## Issue 2 — Stale Product: Expanded Definition, No Return, Self-Reported

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §1.9: "Stale Product" = Product unsold after Code Date **and returned to Supplier within 48 hours** after Code Date.
- Supply Agreement §5.4: Supplier collects Stale Product on its regular delivery route; credit at invoiced price.
- Order Form No. 1 §3.1: "Stale Product" = any Product **unsold at its Code Date OR damaged or unsaleable in store**.
- Order Form No. 1 §3.2: Credit at full invoiced price, **without return**. Quantities reported by Customer **weekly through Customer's store reporting system**.

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
Under the order of precedence (Supply Agreement §18.4(b)), the Order Form controls over the Agreement. The Order Form makes three material changes:

1. **Broader definition.** "Damaged or unsaleable in store" is not defined, not subject to inspection by Mubart, and could encompass products damaged by Zinix's own handling, display, or storage (which §10.2(c) acknowledges is Zinix's responsibility post-delivery). Mubart would be crediting Zinix for Zinix's own damage.
2. **No physical return.** Mubart cannot verify quantities, condition, or cause. It is entirely dependent on Zinix's store-level reporting system. For a product with a 9-day shelf life sold across multiple DCs and potentially stores, the financial exposure is significant and unauditable.
3. **Weekly self-reporting.** There is no reconciliation mechanism, no audit right over the stale-product data, and no cap on the credit as a percentage of invoiced volume.

At the Order Form's pricing (roughly $1.24–$1.78 per unit), even a modest over-reporting or misclassification of "unsaleable" product could generate meaningful weekly credits with no way for Mubart to verify.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Negotiating position:**
  - Limit "Stale Product" to product unsold after Code Date (delete "damaged or unsaleable in store"), consistent with the Supply Agreement definition.
  - If Zinix insists on including in-store damage, require that the product remain in original packaging and that Mubart (or a third party) have the right to inspect a sample or audit the reporting data quarterly.
  - Add a cap: stale-product credits shall not exceed [X]% of the invoiced value of Products delivered in the preceding 4-week period.
  - Require Mubart's written confirmation of credit amounts before they are applied, or at minimum a 5-business-day objection window.
- **Redline for Order Form §3.1:**
  > "For the Products ordered under this Order Form, 'Stale Product' means any Product that is unsold at its Code Date and remains in original, unopened packaging. Product damaged in store due to Customer's handling, display or storage is not Stale Product."
- **Redline for Order Form §3.2 (add):**
  > "Customer's stale-product reports shall be supported by store-level POS data or equivalent records, which Supplier may request and Customer shall make available for inspection no more than once per quarter on 10 business days' notice. Credits in excess of [15]% of the invoiced value of Products delivered in the preceding four weeks shall require Supplier's written confirmation before application."

- **Needs Mubart input:** What is Mubart's historical stale-product rate on comparable private-label bread programs? This will inform the cap percentage and help Mubart assess whether the current exposure is acceptable as a short-term concession.

## Issue 3 — Indemnification: "Regardless of Cause" and Asymmetric Cap Carve-Out in Amendment

**Where it appears:**
- Term Sheet §10.1: Mubart indemnifies for claims arising from Products that **fail to meet the product warranties**, including recall costs **caused by those failures**.
- Supply Agreement §10.1: Supplier indemnifies for all Losses arising out of or relating to the Products, including recall costs and personal injury claims, **"regardless of cause."**
- Supply Agreement §11.3(a): Excludes "each party's indemnification obligations under Section 10" from the liability cap.
- Proposed Amendment No. 1 §2: Replaces §11.3(a) with "Supplier's indemnification obligations under Section 10.1" only.

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
Two compounding problems:

1. **"Regardless of cause."** The term sheet tied Mubart's indemnity to warranty failures. The executed Agreement severs that link. If a product causes injury due to Zinix's improper storage (e.g., failure to refrigerate, expired product still on shelf), Mubart is still indemnifying Zinix. This overlaps with and arguably contradicts §10.2(c), which assigns post-delivery handling to Zinix's indemnity. As drafted, Mubart could be on the hook for injuries caused by Zinix's own negligence after delivery.

2. **Asymmetric cap carve-out in the Amendment.** The current §11.3(a) excludes *both parties'* indemnification obligations from the 12-month liability cap. The Amendment narrows this to *only Supplier's* indemnification under §10.1. This means Zinix's indemnification obligations under §10.2 (Customer Materials IP infringement, labeling non-compliance, post-delivery handling) become subject to the cap. If Zinix's labeling causes a regulatory action or IP claim that costs Mubart more than 12 months of payments, Mubart's recovery is capped. Meanwhile, Mubart's indemnity to Zinix remains uncapped.

**Recommended fix:**
- **On "regardless of cause":** Strike that phrase. Replace with:
  > "…including all costs of any recall, market withdrawal or stock recovery **to the extent caused by Supplier's breach of Section 9.2 or Supplier's negligence**, and any claim for personal injury, illness or death **to the extent caused by the Products as delivered by Supplier**."
- **On the Amendment:** Do not accept §2 of the Amendment as drafted. Either (a) strike the Amendment's change to §11.3(a) and leave the mutual carve-out intact, or (b) if Zinix insists on narrowing it, counter with language that excludes *both* parties' indemnification obligations from the cap, or at minimum excludes Customer's §10.2 indemnification from the cap as well.
- **Redline for Amendment §2, alternative (a):** Delete Amendment §2 in its entirety; no change to §11.3.
- **Redline for Amendment §2, alternative (b) if Zinix will not move:**
  > "(a) each Party's indemnification obligations under Section 10;"

## Issue 4 — Governing Law and Forum Conflict: Agreement vs. Order Form

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §18.2: Delaware law; exclusive jurisdiction in New Castle County, Delaware.
- Order Form No. 1 §6: **New Jersey law**; exclusive jurisdiction in **Bergen County, New Jersey**.

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
Under the order of precedence in Supply Agreement §18.4(b), the Order Form prevails over the Agreement "as to the Products ordered under it." This means any dispute involving the five products in Order Form No. 1 is governed by New Jersey law and must be litigated in Bergen County, NJ. New Jersey law may differ from Delaware law on issues material to this deal (e.g., enforceability of the exclusivity provision, the stale-product credit as a form of liquidated damages, or the indemnification scope). More practically, Mubart is a Delaware LLC with operations presumably in the DE/MD/PA corridor; being hauled into New Jersey adds cost and inconvenience. There is no indication in the term sheet or email thread that New Jersey was ever discussed.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Immediate:** Flag this to Mubart as likely a drafting error by Zinix. Confirm with Mubart whether any of its operations or the relevant DCs are in New Jersey (they are not — DC-1 is in Delaware, DC-2 in Maryland, DC-3 in Pennsylvania).
- **Negotiating position:** This should be corrected before any dispute arises. Propose a conforming amendment to Order Form No. 1 §6 to match the Agreement (Delaware law, New Castle County). If Zinix resists, Mubart should at minimum understand the substantive differences under NJ law before the next renewal or amendment cycle.
- **Redline for Order Form No. 1 §6:**
  > "Disputes arising under this Order Form or relating to the Products ordered under it are governed by the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to its conflict of laws rules. Each Party submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state or federal courts located in New Castle County, Delaware for any such dispute and waives any objection to venue in those courts."

## Issue 5 — No Minimum Volume Commitment Despite Broad Exclusivity

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §7.1 (exclusivity) and §7.2 (Customer Commitment: 80% of requirements).
- Term Sheet §4.1–4.3 (narrower exclusivity, same 80% commitment).

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
Section 7.2 requires Zinix to buy 80% of its *requirements* from Mubart. But "requirements" is not a fixed number — it floats with Zinix's actual sales. If Zinix's bread category shrinks (consumer trend shift, store closures, private-label delisting), Mubart's revenue shrinks proportionally, while Mubart remains locked out of selling to any other grocery retailer in the US (per the overbroad §7.1). There is no minimum purchase quantity, no take-or-pay provision, and no revenue floor.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Negotiating position:** Add a minimum annual purchase volume (in units or dollars) to the Order Form or a future amendment. For example: "Customer shall purchase no fewer than [X] units of Products in aggregate during each contract year." If Zinix's actual requirements fall below that floor, Customer pays for the difference at the Order Form price (a "buy-back" or "make-up" provision).
- **Alternative:** If a hard minimum is not achievable, add a provision that if Customer's actual requirements for a Product category fall below [X]% of the prior year's volume for two consecutive quarters, either Party may negotiate in good faith to adjust the exclusivity scope or pricing.
- **Needs Mubart input:** What volume does Mubart need from this account to justify the exclusivity and dedicated capacity? This number drives the minimum.

## Issue 6 — Force Majeure / Exclusivity Asymmetry

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §16.1: If a force majeure event continues >30 days, the unaffected Party may obtain Products from other sources without breach of §7.2.
- Supply Agreement §7.1: Supplier exclusivity (no carve-out for force majeure).

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
If a force majeure event affects Mubart (e.g., a fire at its plant, a labor dispute at Mubart's facility, a supply-chain disruption), Zinix can source from other suppliers for the duration without breaching its 80% commitment. But Mubart remains bound by §7.1 and cannot sell to any other grocery retailer to offset lost revenue. The force majeure provision does not suspend or modify the exclusivity obligation. Mubart bears 100% of the revenue loss from a force majeure event while Zinix's obligation to buy from Mubart effectively disappears.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Redline for §16.1 (add sentence):**
  > "During any period in which Supplier is unable to perform due to a force majeure event continuing for more than 30 days, Section 7.1 shall be suspended to the extent necessary to permit Supplier to allocate its available production capacity to other customers, provided that Supplier shall use commercially reasonable efforts to resume performance under this Agreement as soon as practicable."
- **Alternative:** If Zinix will not agree to suspend exclusivity, add a provision that if force majeure prevents delivery for more than 60 days, either Party may terminate the affected Order Form without penalty, and §7.1 shall not apply to the terminated Order Form's product categories.

## Issue 7 — Price Adjustment: Narrow Input List and Once-Per-Year Limit

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §4.2: One adjustment per contract year; limited to documented changes in cost of "flour, sweeteners, oils, packaging film and fuel."

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
The input list omits several significant cost drivers for a bakery: labor, energy (electricity/gas for ovens), yeast, salt, water treatment, and general inflation. If labor costs rise 10% but flour is flat, Mubart cannot pass that through until the next annual adjustment window, and even then only for the listed inputs. The "once per contract year" limit means Mubart may be locked in for up to 12 months after a sudden input spike.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Negotiating position:** Expand the input list to include "flour, sweeteners, oils, packaging film, fuel, energy, and direct labor costs." Alternatively, add a general inflation trigger: "or, if the Producer Price Index for baked goods (or a mutually agreed index) increases by more than [3]% during a contract year, Supplier may adjust prices by the amount of the increase."
- **Redline for §4.2 (expanded inputs):**
  > "Supplier may adjust prices once in each contract year, on 60 days' written notice, to reflect documented changes in the cost of flour, sweeteners, oils, packaging film, fuel, energy and direct labor. Any adjustment shall not exceed the documented change in Supplier's cost of those inputs."

## Issue 8 — Audit Rights: "For Cause" Undefined

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §8.2: One audit per contract year on 10 business days' notice, plus "additional audits for cause."

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
"For cause" is not defined. Zinix could argue that any quality complaint, any stale-product spike, or any customer complaint constitutes "cause" for an additional audit. Repeated unannounced (or short-notice) audits are operationally disruptive for a bakery and could reveal proprietary formulation details to Zinix's personnel.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Redline for §8.2 (define "for cause"):**
  > "Customer may conduct additional audits for cause, where 'cause' means a reasonable, good-faith belief, based on specific facts, that Supplier is in material breach of Section 8.1 (Food Safety Program) or that a Product recall is imminent. Audits for cause require 5 business days' notice (or such shorter notice as is reasonably necessary in an emergency) and shall be limited in scope to the specific concern giving rise to the audit."

The Initial Term ends approximately March 2, 2029. To avoid auto-renewal, Mubart must give notice by approximately **November 2, 2028**. If Mubart wants the option to walk away (particularly if the exclusivity issue in Issue 1 is not resolved), this date is critical.

## Issue 10 — Tight Production-to-Delivery Timeline

**Where it appears:**
- Order Form No. 1 §5.2: Packaging lead time is 21 days from final proof approval.
- Order Form No. 1 §1.2: First deliveries in the week beginning May 4, 2026.
- Email thread (Luis Ferrante, Apr. 20): Proofs approved Apr. 10 → packaging lands May 1 → production runs May 2–3 → delivery week of May 4.

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
The timeline leaves essentially zero buffer. If packaging is delayed by even a few days (supplier issue, quality rejection, transit problem), Mubart misses the first delivery window and is in breach of the Order Form. The Agreement has no grace period or cure period for initial delivery delays.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Operational:** Confirm with Mubart's packaging supplier that the May 1 delivery date is firm. Build in a contingency (e.g., air-freight option) if the packaging supplier flags any risk.
- **Contractual (for future Order Forms):** Add a provision that first deliveries under a new Order Form are subject to a mutual confirmation of production readiness, and that delays in Customer Materials or packaging approval beyond the dates in the Order Form extend the first-delivery date day-for-day.

## Issue 11 — Rejection at Customer's Option (No Mubart Right to Cure)

**Where it appears:**
- Supply Agreement §5.3: "Supplier shall replace or credit rejected Product **at Customer's option**."

**Why it matters to Mubart:**
Mubart cannot choose to repair, rework, or re-deliver. If a truck arrives with a minor cosmetic issue (e.g., a crushed corner of a poly bag), Zinix can demand a full credit rather than a replacement, and Mubart has no say. Combined with the 24-hour notice window, this puts all leverage on Zinix at the dock.

**Recommended fix:**
- **Redline for §5.3 (last sentence):**
  > "Supplier shall, at Supplier's option, replace rejected Product or issue a credit, provided that replacement shall be made within [5] business days."
- **Alternative (if Zinix resists):** At minimum, limit the "credit" option to Product that is genuinely unsalable (not merely cosmetically imperfect), and require that Zinix make rejected Product available for Mubart's inspection before the credit is finalized.

## Suggested Priorities for the Client Call

1. **Exclusivity (§7.1)** — Most urgent. If Mubart has any existing grocery-retailer customers for bakery products, this is a live breach. Even if not, the scope is commercially unacceptable for a three-year lock-up.
2. **Stale Product (Order Form §§3.1–3.2)** — Second most urgent financially. The "without return, self-reported, includes in-store damage" combination creates open-ended, unverifiable liability.
3. **Indemnification + Amendment** — The "regardless of cause" language plus the one-sided cap carve-out in the Amendment shift significant risk to Mubart. Address before signing the Amendment.
4. **Governing Law (Order Form §6)** — Likely a drafting error; fix now while the relationship is cooperative.
5. **Minimum Volume** — Important commercially but can be addressed in a future amendment or at the first renewal.
6. **Remaining items** (force majeure, price adjustment, audit, rejection, notice, timeline) — Lower urgency; can be bundled into a cleanup amendment.
