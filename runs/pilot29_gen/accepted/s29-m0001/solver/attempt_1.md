## 1. Working Capital Objection Deadline Likely Expired

**Documents:** Stock Purchase Agreement § 2.4(b); Gliluv Board Written Consent, Recitals B–D and Resolution 2.2

SPA § 2.4(b) requires Buyer to deliver an Objection Notice "within thirty (30) days after receipt of the Closing Statement." Seller delivered the Closing Statement on **February 9, 2026** (Board Consent, Recital B). The 30-day deadline therefore expired on **March 11, 2026**. The Board Consent authorizing the dispute is dated **March 20, 2026**—nine days after the deadline.

If no Objection Notice was delivered on or before March 11, § 2.4(b) provides that "the Closing Statement and Seller's calculation of Closing Working Capital will be final and binding on the parties," and Gliluv loses its approximately $1.9 million claim (Board Consent, Recital C).

The ratification clause in Resolution 2.7 ("all actions previously taken by any manager, officer or authorized representative…are ratified") could preserve the claim only if an officer in fact delivered the Objection Notice before March 11 without prior Board authorization. The documents do not confirm this.

**Why it matters:** This is potentially the single largest near-term dollar exposure. A $1.9M working-capital overstatement, if unchallenged, means Gliluv paid $1.9M more than the agreed target.

**Recommendation:**
- Immediately confirm with the CEO and CFO whether an Objection Notice was delivered to Seller on or before March 11, 2026, and obtain a copy.
- If it was not delivered, assess whether Seller's conduct (e.g., delay in providing books and records under § 2.4(d)) could support an equitable tolling or waiver argument, though this is weak on the current record.
- If it was delivered, file a copy with the matter record and confirm the 30-day good-faith negotiation period under § 2.4(c) is running.

## 2. Escrow Agreement Purports to Eliminate Recovery Beyond the Escrow for Environmental Claims

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 5.3; Stock Purchase Agreement §§ 9.5(b), 9.6(b), 11.8

Escrow Agreement § 5.3 states: "The Escrow Fund shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** for all claims for indemnification by the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **including claims under Section 9.2(c)** [the Environmental Indemnity]. Once the Escrow Fund has been fully disbursed…no Buyer Indemnitee shall have any further recourse against Seller or its Affiliates in respect of any such claim."

This directly conflicts with:

- **SPA § 9.5(b):** The $6.4M cap in § 9.5(a) "shall not apply to Losses arising from…Fraud, and the Environmental Indemnity under Section 9.2(c)." The cap exclusion implies environmental claims can exceed $6.4M.
- **SPA § 9.6(b):** For Losses "not subject to the cap in Section 9.5(a)," recovery is available "directly from Seller."
- **SPA § 9.1(c):** The Environmental Indemnity survives until the **fifth anniversary** of Closing (January 15, 2031), but the Escrow Fund is released 18 months after Closing (July 15, 2027) under Escrow Agreement § 3.1.

SPA § 11.8 (Order of Precedence) provides that the Escrow Agreement "shall govern with respect to recovery from the Escrow Fund." A narrow reading limits this to the mechanics of drawing on the escrow. But § 5.3 goes further: it purports to make the escrow the exclusive source of *all* recovery, not merely the first source. If § 5.3 is given its plain meaning, environmental claims arising after the escrow is released (or exceeding the $6.4M balance) have **no remedy**, effectively nullifying the 5-year survival period and the cap exclusion in § 9.5(b).

**Why it matters:** In a ship-management business, pre-closing environmental liabilities (oil spills, MARPOL violations, ballast-water discharges) can surface years later and can easily exceed $6.4M. OPA 90 liability, for example, is not subject to a cap. If Gliluv cannot recover beyond the escrow, it bears unlimited environmental risk for pre-closing events with no indemnification backstop.

**Recommendation:**
- Raise with Dreisend's counsel that Escrow Agreement § 5.3 is inconsistent with SPA §§ 9.5(b) and 9.6(b) and should be amended to provide that the Escrow Fund is the *first* source of recovery for all Article IX claims, but that for claims not subject to the § 9.5(a) cap (i.e., Fundamental Representations, Fraud, and the Environmental Indemnity), Buyer retains the right to recover directly from Seller for amounts exceeding the Escrow Fund.
- Suggested language for § 5.3: "The Escrow Fund shall be the first source of recovery for all claims for indemnification under Article IX. Notwithstanding the foregoing, for Losses that are not subject to the cap in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement, Buyer Indemnitees may recover directly from Seller any amount in excess of the balance of the Escrow Fund."
- If Dreisend resists, this should be a condition to proceeding or a negotiated risk allocation (e.g., a separate environmental insurance policy or a higher escrow for environmental claims).

## 3. Transition Services Agreement: Gliluv Indemnifies Provider for Provider's Own Negligence, Without Cap

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement §§ 8.3, 8.4

TSA § 8.3 requires Recipient (Gliluv) to "indemnify, defend and hold harmless Provider…from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to the Services or the use of the Services by Recipient Group. **This includes Losses arising from the negligence of Provider or any Provider Personnel**, except to the extent those Losses are caused by the gross negligence or willful misconduct of Provider."

The final sentence of § 8.3 states: "The obligations of Recipient under this Section 8.3 **shall not be subject to Section 8.4 or any other limitation**."

Meanwhile, TSA § 8.4 caps Provider's aggregate liability at "total Service Fees actually paid by Recipient under this Agreement" (maximum approximately $3.43M over the full term, less if services end early).

**The asymmetry:** If a Provider employee's ordinary negligence causes a third-party claim (e.g., a crewing error leads to a seafarer injury, or a data error causes a charterer loss), Gliluv must indemnify Provider for that loss—uncapped—while Provider's own liability to Gliluv for the same negligence is capped at fees paid. Gliluv is, in effect, insuring Provider against its own ordinary negligence.

**Why it matters:** The Services include crew management (manning, certification tracking, payroll, P&I crew-claims administration) and vessel-performance data (CII, EU MRV emissions reporting). Errors in these areas can generate significant third-party liability (seafarer claims, regulatory fines, charterer disputes). Gliluv would bear that risk even when caused by Provider's own staff.

**Recommendation:**
- Delete the sentence in § 8.3 that extends Recipient's indemnity to "Losses arising from the negligence of Provider or any Provider Personnel." Provider should be responsible for its own ordinary negligence, consistent with the standard of care in § 2.2.
- If Dreisend insists on retaining some form of Recipient indemnity for "use of the Services," limit it to Losses caused by Recipient's own negligence or misuse, and subject it to the same cap in § 8.4.
- At minimum, remove the sentence making § 8.3 obligations "not subject to Section 8.4 or any other limitation."

## 4. Tax Representation Survival Period Is Only Six Months

**Documents:** Stock Purchase Agreement §§ 9.1(b), 9.2(d)

SPA § 9.1(b) provides that the Tax Representations (Article IV, § 4.11) survive until **six (6) months** after the Closing Date—i.e., until **July 15, 2026**. Section 9.2(d) (Seller's indemnity for Pre-Closing Taxes) is expressly "subject to the survival period applicable to the Tax Representations under Section 9.1."

**Why it matters:** The federal statute of limitations on tax assessments is generally three years (six years for substantial omissions). State and foreign tax authorities have similar or longer periods. A six-month survival means that if the IRS, a state tax authority, or a foreign tax authority opens an examination or asserts a deficiency for a Pre-Closing period after July 15, 2026, Gliluv has no indemnification recourse against Seller. For a company that has operated since at least 2021 (per the representation look-back in § 4.11(a)), this is an unacceptably short window.

Additionally, the Escrow Fund is not released until 18 months (July 15, 2027), so the escrow remains available as security—but the *right to make a claim* against it expires at 6 months under § 9.4 ("No claim for indemnification…may be made unless notice of the claim is delivered before the expiration of the applicable survival period").

**Recommendation:**
- Negotiate an extension of the Tax Representation survival period to at least the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (consistent with the treatment of Fundamental Representations in § 9.1(a)), or a minimum of three years post-Closing.
- If Seller resists, consider a compromise: survival until the later of (i) 18 months post-Closing (aligning with escrow release) or (ii) 60 days after the close of any tax audit or proceeding that was pending or reasonably expected as of Closing.
- This is a pre-closing item if the SPA has not yet been signed; if already signed, raise it as part of any renegotiation or document it as a known risk for the client.

## 5. Escrow Release at 18 Months Leaves Environmental Claims Unsecured After That Date

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 3.1; Stock Purchase Agreement §§ 9.1(c), 9.2(c)

The Escrow Fund is released to Seller 18 months after Closing (July 15, 2027) under Escrow Agreement § 3.1, less any Pending Claim Amount. The Environmental Indemnity survives until the fifth anniversary of Closing (January 15, 2031) under SPA § 9.1(c).

Between months 18 and 60, environmental claims are unsecured by the escrow. Combined with Issue 2 above (if Escrow Agreement § 5.3 is read to make the escrow the sole source of recovery), environmental claims arising in that window may have no practical remedy.

**Why it matters:** Environmental liabilities in maritime operations (oil spills, ballast-water violations, latent contamination) often manifest with a delay. A 5-year survival period is only as good as the credit of the party obligated to pay. Dreisend is a Bermuda exempted company; enforcing a judgment against it outside the escrow may be difficult.

**Recommendation:**
- In conjunction with the fix for Issue 2, negotiate a carve-out from the escrow release for environmental claims: the Escrow Agent should retain a portion of the fund (e.g., $2M) until the fifth anniversary, or the parties should agree that the release in § 3.1 is subject to a retained environmental reserve.
- Alternatively, require Seller to maintain an environmental liability insurance policy or letter of credit for the period between escrow release and the expiration of the environmental survival period.

## 6. Migration Plan Deadline May Have Passed

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement § 3.2

TSA § 3.2 requires the parties to agree a written Migration Plan "within forty-five (45) days after the Effective Date." The Effective Date is January 15, 2026, making the deadline **March 1, 2026**. The Board Consent is dated March 20, 2026, and makes no reference to a Migration Plan having been delivered.

**Why it matters:** The Migration Plan is the roadmap for transferring crew management, vessel-performance data, ERP, and chartering systems to Gliluv or its designee. Without it, there is no agreed timeline, format, or responsibility allocation for the data transition. If Provider is not actively planning the migration, Gliluv risks being locked into the Services past the scheduled end dates without a viable exit.

**Open question:** The documents do not confirm whether a Migration Plan was agreed by March 1. If it was, obtain a copy. If it was not, this is a breach by both parties (the obligation is mutual) and should be raised immediately.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with the Company's COO (designated Recipient service manager per Schedule A) whether a Migration Plan has been agreed.
- If not, send a written notice to Provider requesting agreement on the plan within 15 days, citing § 3.2, and propose a schedule aligned with the Service Period end dates.
- If Provider resists, consider whether this constitutes a material breach supporting termination under § 7.4 (30-day cure period).

## 7. TSA Service Dependency Limits Exit Flexibility

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement, Schedule A (Dependencies); § 7.3

Schedule A states: "Service A-4 [Chartering System] depends on Service A-3 [ERP Services] and cannot continue after termination of Service A-3." Service A-4 has a 6-month period; Service A-3 has a 9-month period. Section 7.3 permits Gliluv to terminate any Service for convenience on 30 days' notice.

**Why it matters:** If Gliluv terminates the ERP Service (A-3) early—for example, because it has migrated to its own accounting system—it simultaneously loses the Chartering System (A-4), even if A-4 is still needed. This couples two services with different natural end dates and reduces Gliluv's ability to manage the transition on a service-by-service basis.

**Recommendation:**
- Negotiate deletion of the dependency, or restructure it so that A-4 can continue independently of A-3 for its remaining term (or a short tail period) upon payment of the applicable fee.
- If the technical dependency is real (i.e., the chartering system literally runs on the ERP instance), require Provider to confirm in writing the specific technical dependency and agree to a reasonable transition period.

**Documents:** Saltreach Office Lease §§ 17.1, 20(b)

## 9. TSA: Provider Liability Cap Is Low Relative to Risk

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement §§ 8.4, 8.1

Provider's aggregate liability is capped at "total Service Fees actually paid" (§ 8.4). Maximum total fees over the full term are approximately $3.43M (calculated from Schedule A). In practice, if services are terminated early, the cap will be lower. Combined with the broad disclaimer in § 8.1 (no implied warranties of merchantability or fitness), Gliluv's recovery for a Provider failure (e.g., loss of vessel-performance data, ERP outage during month-end close, crew certification error) is limited to a relatively small amount.

**Why it matters:** The Systems hold crew records, seafarer certificates, voyage data, and financial records. A data loss or prolonged outage could disrupt operations across the entire managed fleet and trigger regulatory non-compliance (ISM Code, ML Convention). The cap does not reflect the magnitude of potential harm.

**Recommendation:**
- Negotiate to raise the cap to at least 2× total Service Fees, or a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $5M), for claims arising from breach of Article 5 (Systems Access and Data) or Article 6 (Confidentiality), which are already excluded from the § 8.5 consequential-damages waiver.
- Alternatively, require Provider to maintain technology errors-and-omissions and cyber-liability insurance with limits adequate to cover the cap, naming Gliluv as an additional insured or loss payee.
