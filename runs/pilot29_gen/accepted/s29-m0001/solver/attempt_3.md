## 1. Escrow Agreement Limits Recovery for the Environmental Indemnity to $6.4 Million, Contradicting the SPA

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 5.3; Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA) §§ 9.1(c), 9.2(c), 9.5(b), 9.6(b), 11.8

SPA § 9.5(b) expressly carves the Environmental Indemnity (SPA § 9.2(c)) out of the cap, and SPA § 9.6(b) provides that losses "not subject to the cap in Section 9.5(a)" are recoverable "directly from Seller." The Environmental Indemnity also survives five years (SPA § 9.1(c)).

Escrow Agreement § 5.3, however, states: "The Escrow Fund shall be the **sole and exclusive source of recovery** for all claims for indemnification by the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **including claims under Section 9.2(c)** of the Purchase Agreement. Once the Escrow Fund has been fully disbursed under this Agreement, no Buyer Indemnitee shall have any further recourse against Seller or its Affiliates in respect of any such claim."

Read together, these provisions create a direct conflict. If § 5.3 controls, Gliluv's maximum recovery for a pre-Closing environmental liability—potentially an OPA 90 oil-spill claim running into the hundreds of millions—is $6.4 million, and after the escrow is released at 18 months (Escrow Agreement § 3.1), Gliluv has **no recourse at all** for environmental claims, even though the SPA contemplates a five-year survival period.

SPA § 11.8 (Order of Precedence) provides that the SPA prevails over Ancillary Agreements "except that the Escrow Agreement shall govern with respect to recovery from the Escrow Fund." Whether § 5.3 falls within that carve-out is genuinely ambiguous: it is framed as a limitation on recovery *from the Escrow Fund*, but its operative effect is to eliminate recovery *from Seller* entirely. A court could read it either way.

**Why it matters:** This is the single largest potential exposure in the transaction. Maritime environmental liabilities (OPA 90, MARPOL, ballast water) can be catastrophic and are precisely the kind of risk that an uncapped, five-year environmental indemnity is designed to address. If § 5.3 is enforced as written, Gliluv bears all environmental risk above $6.4 million.

**Recommendation:**
- Raise immediately with Dreisend's counsel. Seek a conforming amendment to Escrow Agreement § 5.3 to provide that the "sole and exclusive source" language applies only to claims subject to the cap in SPA § 9.5(a), and that for claims excluded from the cap (Fundamental Representations, Fraud, and the Environmental Indemnity), the Escrow Fund is the *first* source of recovery but not the *exclusive* source, with recourse against Seller preserved in accordance with SPA § 9.6(b).
- If Dreisend resists, consider whether the purchase price or escrow amount should be adjusted to reflect the limited environmental protection Gliluv is actually receiving.
- As a fallback, confirm that Gliluv's insurance program (pollution legal liability, etc.) covers the gap.

## 2. Possible Missed Deadline to Object to the Closing Statement

**Documents:** SPA § 2.4(b); Gliluv Board Written Consent (Recitals B–D; Resolution 2.7)

SPA § 2.4(b) requires Buyer to deliver an Objection Notice "within thirty (30) days after receipt of the Closing Statement." Seller delivered the Closing Statement on **February 9, 2026** (Board Consent, Recital B). The 30-day deadline therefore expired on **March 11, 2026**.

The Board Written Consent is dated **March 20, 2026**—nine days after the deadline. Resolution 2.7 ratifies "all actions previously taken by any manager, officer or authorized representative of the Company in connection with the matters contemplated by the foregoing resolutions." If the Objection Notice was in fact delivered on or before March 11, the ratification cures the authorization gap. If it was not, SPA § 2.4(b) provides that the Closing Statement "will be final and binding on the parties," and the $1.9 million working-capital dispute is lost.

**Why it matters:** The Board is authorizing a $1.9 million claim (Recital C; Resolution 2.1) that may no longer be available. If the deadline was missed, Gliluv has overpaid the Purchase Price by that amount with no contractual remedy.

**Recommendation:**
- **Open question:** Confirm with the CFO whether and when the Objection Notice was actually delivered to Seller. If it was delivered on or before March 11, document the delivery date and method (per SPA § 11.2) in the file. If it was not delivered, assess whether there is any equitable argument (e.g., Seller's course of performance, waiver) or whether the matter must be written off.
- If the notice was timely, no further action is needed on this point, but calendar the 30-day good-faith negotiation period under SPA § 2.4(c) and the potential referral to the Independent Accountant.

## 3. TSA: Gliluv Indemnifies Provider for Provider's Own Negligence, Without Cap

**Documents:** Transition Services Agreement (TSA) § 8.3

TSA § 8.3 provides: "Recipient shall indemnify, defend and hold harmless Provider… from and against all Losses arising out of or relating to the Services or the use of the Services by Recipient Group. **This includes Losses arising from the negligence of Provider or any Provider Personnel**, except to the extent those Losses are caused by the gross negligence or willful misconduct of Provider. The obligations of Recipient under this Section 8.3 **shall not be subject to Section 8.4 or any other limitation**."

This means that if a Provider employee is merely negligent (e.g., misprocesses seafarer payroll, fails to track a certification expiry, makes an error in a noon report) and that negligence gives rise to a third-party claim, Gliluv must indemnify Dreisend. The only protection is for gross negligence or willful misconduct. And Gliluv's exposure is uncapped.

By contrast, Provider's own indemnity to Gliluv (TSA § 8.2) is limited to gross negligence/willful misconduct and breaches of the data/confidentiality articles, and Provider's aggregate liability is capped at fees paid (TSA § 8.4).

**Why it matters:** Crew management (Service A-1, $185,000/month) involves high-risk activities: seafarer recruitment, certification, payroll, and P&I crew-claims administration. A simple negligence error in crewing could lead to a vessel being detained, a seafarer injury claim, or a regulatory fine. Under the current drafting, Gliluv would bear the cost of indemnifying Dreisend for those losses while having no reciprocal claim against Dreisend (because ordinary negligence does not trigger § 8.2).

**Recommendation:**
- Seek to delete the sentence "This includes Losses arising from the negligence of Provider or any Provider Personnel" from § 8.3. At minimum, limit Gliluv's indemnity to Losses caused by the negligence of *Recipient Group* (i.e., Gliluv's own acts or omissions in using the Services), not Provider's negligence.
- If Dreisend insists on retaining some version, cap Gliluv's § 8.3 exposure (e.g., at 2× annual Service Fees) and make it subject to the same consequential-damages exclusion in § 8.5.
- Flag the asymmetry: Provider's liability is capped at fees paid (§ 8.4), but Gliluv's liability to Provider is unlimited. This is not a market-standard TSA structure.

## 4. Tax Representations Survive Only Six Months

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1(b); SPA § 9.2(d)

SPA § 9.1(b) provides that the Tax Representations (SPA § 4.11) survive only "until six (6) months after the Closing Date"—i.e., until **July 15, 2026**. SPA § 9.2(d) makes indemnification for Pre-Closing Taxes "subject to the survival period applicable to the Tax Representations under Section 9.1."

Six months is unusually short for tax representations in a maritime transaction. The Company is subject to U.S. gross transportation tax (IRC § 887), tonnage taxes, payroll taxes for seafarers in multiple jurisdictions, and potentially VAT/GST on international services. Tax audits and assessments commonly arise more than six months after a closing, particularly for international shipping entities with complex multi-jurisdictional tax footprints.

**Why it matters:** If a tax authority assesses additional tax for a Pre-Closing period after July 15, 2026, Gliluv has no indemnification claim against Seller, even though the tax obligation accrued before Closing and Seller represented (in § 4.11) that all taxes had been paid.

**Recommendation:**
- If the deal has not yet closed (per the Board Consent, it closed January 15, 2026, so this point may be moot), this would have been a negotiating item. Given that the Closing has occurred, the practical question is whether Gliluv has adequate tax reserves and whether the insurance program covers pre-Closing tax exposures.
- Confirm with the tax advisors whether any open tax periods (e.g., gross transportation tax filings, foreign withholding) are likely to generate assessments after July 15, 2026. If so, quantify the exposure and consider whether it is reflected in the purchase price.
- Note that the Escrow Fund remains available until 18 months (July 15, 2027), but because the Tax Representations expire at 6 months, no tax claim can be made against the escrow after July 15, 2026.

## 5. Escrow Released at 18 Months, but Environmental Indemnity Survives Five Years

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 3.1; SPA § 9.1(c); SPA § 9.2(c)

The escrow is released 18 months after Closing (July 15, 2027), less pending claims. The Environmental Indemnity survives five years (through January 15, 2031). For the 3-year gap between escrow release and expiration of the environmental survival period, Gliluv's only recourse is a direct claim against Seller.

This gap is not inherently problematic *if* SPA § 9.6(b) is enforceable (i.e., if Escrow Agreement § 5.3 does not, as discussed in Issue 1, eliminate direct recourse against Seller). But it compounds the risk in Issue 1: if § 5.3 is read to make the escrow the exclusive remedy, then after 18 months Gliluv has zero security for environmental claims for the remaining three years of the survival period.

**Recommendation:** Addressed under Issue 1. Additionally, consider whether Seller's creditworthiness (a Bermuda exempted company) supports an unsecured claim of potentially unlimited magnitude three to five years post-closing. If not, the amendment to § 5.3 becomes even more critical.

## 6. TSA: Provider's Liability Cap Is Low Relative to the Risk

**Documents:** TSA § 8.4; Schedule A

Provider's aggregate liability under the TSA is capped at "the total Service Fees actually paid by Recipient under this Agreement." The maximum fees over the full service periods are approximately:

| Service | Monthly Fee | Duration | Total |
|---------|------------|----------|-------|
| A-1 Crew Management | $185,000 | 12 months | $2,220,000 |
| A-2 Vessel-Performance Data | $62,000 | 9 months | $558,000 |
| A-3 ERP Services | $48,000 | 9 months | $432,000 |
| A-4 Chartering System | $36,000 | 6 months | $216,000 |
| **Total (max)** | | | **$3,426,000** |

In practice, the cap will be lower because Gliluv may terminate services early for convenience (§ 7.3) and because the cap is based on fees *actually paid*, not the maximum possible.

**Why it matters:** A single serious crewing error (e.g., sending an unqualified master, failing to maintain mandatory certifications) could result in vessel detention, off-hire losses, or personal injury claims well in excess of $3.4 million. The cap means Provider's maximum exposure is a fraction of the potential loss.

**Recommendation:**
- Seek to raise the cap to at least 2× annual Service Fees or a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $5–10 million).
- Carve out data breaches (Article 5) and confidentiality breaches (Article 6) from the cap, as is market-standard. Currently, § 8.5 excludes breaches of Article 6 from the *consequential damages* waiver, but § 8.4's cap still applies to them.
- Confirm that Gliluv's own insurance (professional liability, employer's liability for seafarers) covers the gap.

## 7. TSA Service Dependencies Could Create Operational Lock-In

**Documents:** TSA Schedule A (Dependencies); TSA § 7.3

Schedule A states: "Service A-4 [Chartering System] depends on Service A-3 [ERP Services] and cannot continue after termination of Service A-3." Section 7.3 permits Gliluv to terminate any Service for convenience on 30 days' notice, but "Schedule A identifies any Service that cannot be terminated without also terminating a dependent Service."

This means if Gliluv wants to terminate the ERP service (A-3) before its 9-month period ends—perhaps because it has migrated to its own accounting system—it must also terminate the Chartering System (A-4), even if A-4's 6-month period has not yet expired and Gliluv still needs it.

**Why it matters:** The chartering system holds fixture records, laytime/demurrage calculations, and voyage estimates. Losing access mid-voyage could disrupt commercial operations and create disputes with charterers over laytime.

**Recommendation:**
- Before Closing (or as soon as possible post-Closing), confirm the migration timeline for both A-3 and A-4 and ensure they are aligned. If Gliluv plans to replace the ERP before 9 months, it must also have a replacement chartering system in place by that date.
- Consider negotiating a decoupling of the dependency or a short bridge period for A-4 after A-3 terminates.

## 8. Lease Renewal Option – Calendar the Notice Window

**Documents:** Saltreach Office Lease § 3.3

The lease expires March 31, 2029. The renewal option (5 additional years) requires written notice "at least twelve (12) months, and not more than eighteen (18) months, before the Expiration Date." The notice window therefore opens **September 30, 2027** and closes **March 31, 2028**. The option is available only if no Event of Default exists when notice is given.

**Why it matters:** If Gliluv wants the renewal option, it must decide and act within that 6-month window. Missing the deadline forfeits the option permanently. Additionally, the option requires no default to exist at the time of notice, so any lease default (even a minor one) must be cured before the notice is given.

**Recommendation:**
- Calendar the September 30, 2027 opening date for internal discussion of whether to exercise.
- Ensure lease compliance (rent payments, insurance certificates, etc.) is monitored so that no Event of Default exists during the notice window.
- Note that renewal base rent is 95% of fair market rent, which could be significantly above the current $46/SF given the 3% annual escalations already built in.

## 9. SPA Section 9.7 – Exclusive Remedy Limits Common Law Claims

**Documents:** SPA § 9.7

"Except for claims for Fraud, claims for specific performance or injunctive relief, and the post-closing adjustment under Section 2.4, the indemnification provisions of this Article IX are the **exclusive remedy** of the parties for any breach of this Agreement."

**Why it matters:** If Seller breaches a covenant (e.g., the confidentiality obligation in § 6.4 or the non-solicitation in § 6.5) and the breach does not also constitute a breach of a representation, Gliluv's only monetary remedy is the indemnification claim, subject to the cap, basket, and survival periods in Article IX. Gliluv cannot bring a separate common law damages claim for the covenant breach.

This is not unusual in M&A, but it means the survival periods and cap in Article IX effectively govern all post-Closing monetary disputes (other than fraud and the working-capital adjustment). Combined with Issue 1 (escrow as sole source of recovery), this narrows Gliluv's practical remedies significantly.

**Recommendation:** No drafting fix available post-Closing. Be aware of this limitation when evaluating any post-Closing dispute. If a covenant breach occurs, the claim must be framed within the Article IX indemnification structure.
