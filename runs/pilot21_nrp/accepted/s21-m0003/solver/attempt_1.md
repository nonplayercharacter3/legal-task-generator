## Issues in Order of Priority

### 1. Disputed Non-Renewal Notice – Potential Five-Year Lock-In

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, Recital B; MSA § 12.2.

**Explanation:** MSA § 12.2 provides that the Agreement auto-renews for a five-year term unless either party gives written notice of non-renewal "at least 120 days before expiration of the then-current term." The Initial Term ends February 28, 2026. Counting back 120 days from February 28, 2026 yields a deadline of **October 31, 2025**. Negel delivered its notice of non-renewal on **November 14, 2025** — fourteen days late. Zaigath has advised that it considers the notice untimely and that the Agreement renewed through February 28, 2031 (Amendment No. 1, Recital B). Negel disputes this position and reserved its rights in § 4.1 of the Amendment.

**Why it matters:** If Zaigath is correct, Negel is contractually bound for another five years (through February 28, 2031) at all five Covered Buildings, with annual fee escalators and no exit right short of termination for cause. This would materially constrain Negel's ability to re-bid the work or respond to building dispositions. Even if Negel ultimately prevails, the dispute creates uncertainty that could complicate any pending or contemplated building sale (a buyer will want to know whether the elevator contract runs through 2026 or 2031).

**Recommended fix:** This is not a drafting fix — it is a litigation/negotiation strategy question. We should (a) confirm the exact delivery date and method of Negel's November 14 notice against MSA § 15.1 (hand, overnight courier, or certified mail), (b) assess whether any equitable tolling, waiver, or course-of-dealing argument is available, and (c) evaluate the commercial cost of being locked in versus the cost of litigating. If Negel wants out, the practical path may be to negotiate a buyout or early-termination provision with Zaigath as part of a broader amendment, using the assignment rights in Amendment § 3 as leverage.

> **⚠ FLAG FOR NEGEL:** Before we raise anything with Zaigath, we need Negel to confirm: (1) the exact date and method of delivery of the November 14, 2025 notice; (2) whether Negel actually wants to exit at the end of the Initial Term or is open to a shorter renewal; and (3) whether any building sales are currently in progress or expected within the next 12 months, because the answer changes our negotiation posture on the renewal dispute.

### 2. Overly Broad Exclusivity Obligation

**Where:** MSA § 3.4; MSA § 1.6 (definition of "Customer Property").

**Explanation:** MSA § 3.4 requires Negel to engage Zaigath as its "exclusive provider of Vertical Transportation Services at **every Customer Property**," and prohibits Negel or any Affiliate from engaging any other provider "at any Customer Property, except as Provider may agree in writing." "Customer Property" is defined in § 1.6 as "any building, structure or real property owned, leased, managed or hereafter acquired by Customer or any of its Affiliates, **wherever located**." This is not limited to the five Covered Buildings in SOW Schedule A.

**Why it matters:** If Negel acquires, leases, or begins managing any other building — anywhere in the world — it would need Zaigath's written consent to use any other elevator or escalator company there. This is a significant strategic constraint that goes well beyond the scope of the actual engagement. It also creates a mismatch with the press release (see Issue 8 below), which describes the exclusivity as applying only to "the five buildings."

**Recommended fix:** Amend § 3.4 to limit exclusivity to the Covered Buildings identified in a SOW (or to properties in the Borough of Manhattan that Negel owns or manages). Alternatively, add a carve-out for properties acquired after the Effective Date that are not Covered Buildings under any SOW.

### 3. Customer Indemnity Is Overly Broad

**Where:** MSA § 10.2.

**Explanation:** Negel indemnifies Zaigath for third-party claims "arising out of or relating to the condition of the Premises or the Equipment, **whether or not caused in part by Provider**." This means that if an elevator entrapment or mechanical failure injures a tenant and the failure was caused by Zaigath's own negligent maintenance, Negel still owes an indemnity to Zaigath for that claim. The only carve-out is for claims arising from the negligence of "Customer or its employees, tenants or contractors (other than Provider)."

**Why it matters:** This inverts the normal risk allocation. A building owner should not be indemnifying its elevator contractor for the contractor's own negligence. In a portfolio with 64 passenger elevators carrying heavy daily traffic, the probability of an injury claim is non-trivial, and the indemnity could expose Negel to six- or seven-figure liabilities that Zaigath's own negligence caused.

**Recommended fix:** Delete the phrase "whether or not caused in part by Provider" from § 10.2. Negel should indemnify only for claims to the extent caused by the negligence or willful misconduct of Customer, its employees, tenants, or contractors (other than Provider). The condition-of-the-Premises language should be limited to conditions that existed before the SOW Effective Date and were identified in the takeover inspection report (SOW § 2.3), or that arise from Customer's use or alteration of the Equipment in violation of MSA § 6.3.

### 4. No Remedy for Uptime Shortfalls

**Where:** SOW § 4.4.

**Explanation:** SOW § 4.4 requires Zaigath to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to keep each passenger elevator in service at least 98% of the time in each calendar month. There is no service credit, liquidated damage, or other remedy specified for failure to meet this target. Compare SOW § 4.3, which provides specific dollar credits for late response times.

**Why it matters:** The 98% uptime target is the metric that most directly affects tenant experience and rent collectability. Without a remedy, the provision is aspirational only. If a building's elevators are down 10% of the month (roughly 7.2 hours of downtime per day per unit across the portfolio), Negel has no contractual recourse beyond a termination-for-cause claim, which requires a material breach and a 30-day cure period (MSA § 12.3).

**Recommended fix:** Add a service credit schedule for uptime shortfalls, mirroring the structure in § 4.3. For example: a credit of $X per unit per percentage point below 98%, capped at a stated percentage of the Monthly Maintenance Fee. Alternatively, provide that chronic failure to meet the 98% target (e.g., two or more consecutive months) constitutes a material breach giving rise to termination rights under MSA § 12.3 without the need to prove a separate material breach.

### 5. Service Credit Cap Is Low Relative to Portfolio Size

**Where:** SOW § 4.3 (last sentence).

**Explanation:** Service credits for late response times are capped at 15% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee per calendar month. At $38,500/month, the maximum monthly credit is **$5,775** across all five buildings and 64 passenger elevators.

**Why it matters:** If multiple elevators across multiple buildings experience late responses in a single month, the cap is reached quickly and provides no further incentive for performance. In a portfolio this size, the cap is effectively a small percentage of the total fee and may not deter chronic underperformance.

**Recommended fix:** Increase the cap to 25% or 30% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee, or remove the cap entirely for Entrapment-related response failures (which are the most safety-critical). At minimum, the cap should be per-building rather than portfolio-wide, so that poor performance at one building does not consume the credit available for another.

### 6. Consequential Damages Waiver Excludes Lost Rents

**Where:** MSA § 11.2.

**Explanation:** Neither party is liable for "lost profits or **lost rents**." For Negel, this means that if Zaigath's failure to maintain the Equipment causes extended elevator outages that give tenants a basis for rent abatement or that contribute to tenant non-renewal, Negel bears that loss with no recourse against Zaigath.

**Why it matters:** In Manhattan office buildings, elevator reliability is a primary tenant decision factor. A pattern of downtime could drive tenant attrition or support rent-abatement claims. The lost-rents exclusion shields Zaigath from the most economically significant harm its underperformance could cause to Negel.

**Recommended fix:** Carve "lost rents attributable to elevator downtime exceeding [X] hours in any 30-day period" out of the consequential damages exclusion in § 11.2, or add a specific liquidated damages provision in the SOW for extended outages (e.g., any passenger elevator out of service for more than 24 consecutive hours). If Zaigath will not agree to a lost-rents carve-out, the service credit and uptime remedies (Issues 4 and 5) become more important as the primary performance levers.

### 7. Annual Fee Escalator Has No Ceiling Other Than CBA Wage Increases

**Where:** MSA § 5.5; SOW § 5.1 and § 5.3.

**Explanation:** MSA § 5.5 permits Zaigath to adjust the Monthly Maintenance Fee once per contract year by "the percentage increase in the wage rates payable to elevator mechanics under the collective bargaining agreement then in effect for the New York City area." SOW § 5.3 extends the same adjustment to the labor rates. There is no cap on the percentage, no floor, and no mechanism for Negel to challenge the adjustment.

**Why it matters:** In a period of significant wage inflation (as has occurred in the NYC elevator trade), the escalator could produce double-digit annual increases on a $38,500/month base fee (approximately $462,000/year), compounding over the term. If the renewal dispute resolves in Zaigath's favor (Issue 1), Negel would be subject to this escalator for five years.

**Recommended fix:** Add a cap on the annual adjustment (e.g., no more than 5% per year, or no more than the lesser of the CBA wage increase or CPI). Alternatively, require Zaigath to provide supporting documentation of the CBA wage increase and give Negel a right to object before the adjustment takes effect.

### 8. Press Release Understates the Exclusivity Obligation

**Where:** Press Release, § 1; MSA § 3.4 and § 1.6.

**Explanation:** The press release states that "Zaigath will serve as Negel's exclusive vertical transportation provider **at the five buildings**." The MSA, however, makes Zaigath exclusive at "every Customer Property" (MSA § 3.4), which is defined to include any property "owned, leased, managed or hereafter acquired by Customer or any of its Affiliates, wherever located" (MSA § 1.6). The press release is therefore inaccurate — it understates the scope of Negel's exclusivity obligation.

**Why it matters:** If Negel is in the process of selling a Covered Building (as contemplated in Amendment No. 1, Recital C), a buyer's diligence team will read the press release and assume the exclusivity is limited to the five buildings. When they find the broader MSA language, it could create a surprise that complicates the transaction or gives the buyer leverage to demand a price reduction or an assignment condition. Additionally, if the press release is relied upon by tenants or third parties, the discrepancy could create expectations that are difficult to manage.

**Recommended fix:** This is not something to fix in the press release (it is already published). Instead, when negotiating the amendment to narrow § 3.4 (Issue 2), ensure the revised language aligns with what was publicly stated. In any building sale process, disclose the actual scope of the exclusivity obligation in the disclosure letter and negotiate the assignment provision (Amendment § 3.1) to ensure the buyer steps into a clean, building-specific contract.

> **⚠ FLAG FOR NEGEL:** If any building sale is active or expected, we should coordinate with the deal team to ensure the exclusivity issue (MSA § 3.4) is addressed in the PSA or disclosure schedule before the buyer's elevator contractor diligence surfaces it.

### 9. No Cap on After-Hours Callback Charges

**Where:** MSA § 5.4; SOW § 5.3.

**Explanation:** After-Hours Callbacks are billed at overtime rates ($525/team hour on business days and Saturdays; $640/team hour on Sundays and holidays) including travel time (SOW § 5.3; MSA § 5.4). There is no monthly or annual cap on these charges, and the Monthly Maintenance Fee expressly excludes them (MSA § 5.4, last sentence).

**Why it matters:** In a portfolio with 64 passenger elevators and 8 escalators, after-hours calls (entrapped passengers, overnight breakdowns, holiday outages) are a recurring cost. Without a cap, a bad month could produce a significant unbudgeted expense. The rates are also subject to the annual CBA escalator (SOW § 5.3, last sentence), so they will increase over time.

**Recommended fix:** Add a monthly cap on After-Hours Callback charges (e.g., 10% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee), with charges above the cap billed at a reduced rate or credited against the following month's fee. Alternatively, include a stated number of after-hours callbacks per month in the Monthly Maintenance Fee, with additional calls billed at the overtime rates.

### 10. Risk of Estoppel or Waiver Argument from Executing Amendment No. 1

**Where:** Amendment No. 1, §§ 4.1–4.2; MSA § 12.2.

**Explanation:** Amendment § 4.1 expressly states that nothing in the Amendment constitutes an acceptance by Negel of any renewal or a waiver of its rights. However, Zaigath could argue in the alternative that Negel's voluntary execution of the Amendment — and its continued performance under the Agreement after the Initial Term expiration date — constitutes conduct inconsistent with a non-renewal position, giving rise to estoppel or implied waiver. The fact that the Amendment is dated January 20, 2026 (before the February 28, 2026 expiration) and contemplates continued performance (e.g., the assignment provisions) strengthens this argument.

**Why it matters:** If a court or arbitrator finds that Negel's conduct estopped it from asserting the non-renewal, the five-year renewal would be confirmed despite the 14-day-late notice. The express reservation in § 4.1 helps but is not a guarantee.

**Recommended fix:** This is primarily a litigation-risk item. To mitigate, we should (a) ensure all communications with Zaigath going forward reference the pending renewal dispute and Negel's reservation of rights, (b) avoid any language in future correspondence or documents that could be read as accepting the renewed term, and (c) consider whether a short, targeted letter to Zaigath reaffirming Negel's position on the non-renewal notice would be prudent before the February 28, 2026 date passes.

> **⚠ FLAG FOR NEGEL:** We need to confirm with Negel whether it has continued to pay invoices and receive services after the Initial Term expiration date of February 28, 2026, and if so, whether those payments were made under a express reservation of rights. If Negel is paying without protest, that conduct undermines the non-renewal position.
