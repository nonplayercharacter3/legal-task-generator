## Executive Summary

Negel's contract with Zaigath presents one existential issue and a series of structural problems that, taken together, leave Negel with limited leverage and significant exposure. The existential issue is the live dispute over whether the MSA auto-renewed for five years on March 1, 2026. If Zaigath is correct, Negel is locked in through February 28, 2031 with no right to terminate for convenience. Every other issue below should be evaluated against that backdrop.

## I. Items to Resolve with Negel Before Engaging Zaigath

### A. The Renewal Dispute Is the Controlling Issue

**Where:** MSA §12.1–12.2; Amendment No. 1, Recital B and §§4.1–4.2.

**Facts.** The Initial Term ended February 28, 2026. MSA §12.2 requires written notice of non-renewal "at least 120 days before expiration." One hundred twenty days before February 28, 2026 is **October 31, 2025**. Negel's notice was delivered **November 14, 2025** — 14 days late. Zaigath has asserted the agreement renewed for a five-year term through February 28, 2031. Both parties signed Amendment No. 1 on January 20, 2026 (during the Initial Term, while the dispute was pending), and each expressly reserved its position (Amendment §§4.1, 4.2).

**Why it matters.** If the renewal is effective, Negel is committed for five additional years at five buildings with no termination-for-convenience right (the MSA contains only termination for cause and for insolvency, §§12.3–12.4). The annual fee adjustment (§5.5) and after-hours callback charges (§5.4) will compound over that period. The 14-day gap is a real problem: the contractual language is unambiguous ("at least 120 days"), and New York courts generally enforce notice deadlines strictly.

**Arguments available to Negel (for internal assessment):**
- *Substantial compliance / waiver:* Negel gave notice well before the term ended and within the same calendar quarter. Zaigath did not dispute the notice until after the term had already expired. There is a colorable argument that Zaigath, having received and not challenged the notice for over a month, waived strict compliance. This is fact-dependent and not strong.
- *Estoppel / course of dealing:* If Zaigath continued to perform after the Initial Term ended without asserting the renewal until after the Amendment was negotiated, Negel could argue Zaigath's conduct induced reliance. Again, fact-dependent.
- *The press release:* Zaigath's own March 2023 press release states "The initial term of the agreement is three years" and does not mention auto-renewal. This is weak evidence of the parties' understanding but worth preserving.

**Recommended action.** Before raising any other point with Zaigath, Negel should decide its litigation posture. If Negel believes the renewal is effective, the strategy shifts from "how do I get out?" to "how do I negotiate the best terms for the next five years?" If Negel believes it can litigate successfully, it should preserve the record and consider a demand for a meeting under MSA §14.3 (senior executive escalation) before filing anything. **This decision drives everything else.**

### B. Exclusivity Extends Beyond the Five Buildings

**Where:** MSA §3.4; MSA §1.6 (definition of "Customer Property"); SOW §2.1; MSA §2.3 (order of precedence).

**The conflict.** MSA §3.4 obligates Customer to engage Provider as "its exclusive provider of Vertical Transportation Services at **every Customer Property**." "Customer Property" (§1.6) means "any building, structure or real property owned, leased, managed or **hereafter acquired** by Customer or any of its Affiliates, **wherever located**." SOW §2.1, by contrast, limits exclusivity to "the five Covered Buildings in Manhattan listed in SOW Schedule A."

Under the order of precedence (MSA §2.3), the MSA controls over the SOW. The broader MSA language therefore governs.

**Why it matters.** If Negel (or any Affiliate) acquires, leases, or manages any other building — anywhere in the world — during the Term, it must use Zaigath for vertical transportation at that property. This is an extraordinary restriction on a real estate company's operational flexibility, and it was likely not the commercial understanding when the deal was struck (the press release and SOW both frame the engagement around five buildings).

**Recommended fix.** Amend MSA §3.4 to limit exclusivity to the Covered Buildings identified in the applicable SOW, or to properties that Customer specifically designates in writing as subject to exclusivity. If the renewal is in effect, this should be a priority negotiation item.

**Flag for Negel:** Confirm whether Negel or any Affiliate currently owns, leases, or manages any other property with vertical transportation equipment, and whether any acquisitions are planned. If so, this is an active compliance issue, not just a future risk.

### C. No Termination for Convenience

**Where:** MSA §12 (Term and Termination) — no such right exists.

**Why it matters.** Negel's only exit rights are termination for material breach (with a 30-day cure period, §12.3) and for insolvency (§12.4). If Zaigath's performance is mediocre but not a "material breach," or if Negel's business needs change (e.g., a building is converted to a use that doesn't need the same service levels), Negel has no clean exit. This is especially acute if the five-year renewal is in effect.

**Recommended fix.** Negotiate a termination-for-convenience right, exercisable on 180 days' notice, with a declining fee (e.g., 50% of remaining Monthly Maintenance Fees in year one of the notice period, 25% in year two). Alternatively, if the renewal dispute is resolved in Negel's favor, this becomes less urgent but should still be addressed for the next renewal cycle.

## II. Other Issues in Priority Order

### D. Indemnification Is One-Way in Effect

**Where:** MSA §10.1 (Provider's indemnity) vs. §10.2 (Customer's indemnity).

**The problem.** Provider's indemnity (§10.1) covers third-party claims "to the extent caused by the negligent acts or omissions or willful misconduct of Provider." Customer's indemnity (§10.2) covers claims "arising out of or relating to the condition of the Premises or the Equipment, **whether or not caused in part by Provider**." The italicized phrase means Customer indemnifies Provider even when Provider's own negligence contributed to the equipment condition.

**Why it matters.** Elevator failures are often the result of a combination of factors (wear, maintenance quality, building systems). Under §10.2, if a tenant is injured in an elevator and the condition was partly caused by Provider's inadequate maintenance, Negel still indemnifies Zaigath for Provider's share. This effectively guttes the allocation of risk.

**Recommended fix.** Delete "whether or not caused in part by Provider" from §10.2, or replace with "to the extent not caused by Provider or its personnel." At minimum, add language making the indemnities several rather than joint, so each party covers only its own percentage of fault.

---

### E. Liability Cap and Consequential Damages Exclusion Are Inadequate for the Risk Profile

**Where:** MSA §11.1 (cap), §11.2 (consequential damages exclusion), §11.3 (exclusions from the cap).

**The problem.** The cap is 12 months of fees: $38,500 × 12 = **$462,000**. The consequential damages exclusion bars recovery for "lost profits or lost rents." The exclusions from the cap (§11.3) cover only (a) Customer's indemnity obligations, (b) Customer's payment obligations, and (c) fraud. Provider's indemnity to Customer (§10.1) is **not** excluded from the cap.

**Why it matters.** A single serious entrainment with injury, or a cable failure causing property damage, could generate claims well in excess of $462,000. If a prolonged elevator outage at a major building (e.g., Negel Tower at Madison, with 18 destination-dispatch elevators) causes tenant relocation or loss of rent, that recovery is barred by §11.2. The $462,000 cap is less than one month of the Monthly Maintenance Fee for a single large building's worth of potential harm.

**Recommended fix.** (1) Increase the cap to at least 24 months of fees or a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $2 million), whichever is greater. (2) Carve Provider's indemnification obligations (§10.1) out of the cap, as is already done for Customer's indemnification (§10.2). (3) Narrow the consequential damages exclusion to exclude "lost rents" or at least make it mutual and subject to a higher "super-cap" (e.g., 2× the general cap) for claims arising from Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct.

### F. Order of Precedence Favors the MSA Over the SOW

**Where:** MSA §2.3.

**The problem.** The stated order is: Amendments → MSA → SOW. This means the MSA always prevails over the SOW in case of conflict.

**Why it matters.** The SOW contains the specific service levels, response times, and fee structures that are most important to Negel's day-to-day operations. If any MSA provision is ambiguous or could be read to override a SOW protection (e.g., the exclusivity issue in Part B above, or the standard of performance in §3.2 being read to limit the specific service levels in SOW §4), the MSA wins. This is the reverse of the market-standard approach, where the more specific document (SOW) controls over the general framework (MSA).

**Recommended fix.** Reverse the order to: Amendments → SOW → MSA. Alternatively, add a savings clause: "Where a Statement of Work sets out specific service levels, fees, or obligations that are more detailed than a provision of this Agreement, the Statement of Work shall control with respect to the Services described therein."

### G. Service Credit Cap Is Too Low for the Portfolio Size

**Where:** SOW §4.3.

**The problem.** Service credits are capped at 15% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee per calendar month = 15% × $38,500 = **$5,775/month**. The credit for a response exceeding 120 minutes is $1,500 per call. Negel would hit the cap after just **four** late responses in a month.

**Why it matters.** With 82 units across 5 buildings, multiple late responses in a month are not unusual during periods of high demand (e.g., heat waves, major events in Midtown). The cap effectively means that after four late calls, additional failures carry zero financial consequence. The service credit is the **only** remedy for missed response times — there is no termination right for chronic failure to meet the 30-minute standard (unless it rises to "material breach," which is a high bar).

**Recommended fix.** Increase the monthly cap to 25–30% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee, or set a per-building cap rather than a portfolio-wide cap. Add a "chronic failure" provision: if Provider misses the 30-minute response time on more than X% of Service Calls in any two consecutive months, Customer may terminate the affected SOW for cause without a cure period.

### H. Uptime Target Has No Teeth

**Where:** SOW §4.4.

**The problem.** The 98% monthly uptime target for passenger elevators is stated as a "commercially reasonable efforts" obligation. There is no service credit, no liquidated damage, and no termination trigger tied to missing the uptime target.

**Why it matters.** "Commercially reasonable efforts" is a very low standard. In a dispute, Zaigath could argue it made reasonable efforts (e.g., it dispatched a mechanic, but the part wasn't available) and would not be in breach even if uptime fell to 90%. For a portfolio where tenant experience and lease compliance (many office leases require a minimum number of functioning elevators) depend on uptime, this is a significant gap.

**Recommended fix.** Convert the uptime target to a firm obligation with a service credit schedule (e.g., 1% below target = $X per building per month; 3% below = $Y; 5% below = right to terminate for cause). At minimum, add a provision that chronic failure to meet the uptime target (e.g., below 95% for three consecutive months at any single building) constitutes a material breach.

### I. After-Hours Callback Costs Are Uncapped

**Where:** MSA §5.4; SOW §5.3 (rates).

**The problem.** After-Hours Callbacks are billed at overtime rates ($525/team-hour on business-day evenings and Saturdays; $640 on Sundays and holidays), including travel time. There is no cap, no monthly aggregate limit, and no requirement that the callback be "necessary" or "reasonably related to a malfunction." The SOW requires 24/7/365 response (SOW §4.1), which means every after-hours call — whether it's a genuine emergency or a minor issue that could wait until morning — generates a billable callback.

**Why it matters.** For 82 units in 5 buildings, after-hours calls will occur regularly. At $525–$640 per team-hour plus travel, a single 3-hour callback on a Sunday costs $1,920–$2,400 plus travel. If Negel is paying these on top of the $38,500 monthly fee, the true cost of the engagement could be significantly higher than the headline number. There is no reporting requirement specifically for after-hours callbacks (the quarterly report in SOW §4.5 covers "Service Calls received and Response Times" but doesn't require a separate after-hours cost breakdown).

**Recommended fix.** (1) Add a monthly cap on after-hours callback charges (e.g., 20% of the Monthly Maintenance Fee), with Provider absorbing costs above the cap. (2) Require a written justification for each after-hours callback that exceeds a de minimis threshold (e.g., $500). (3) Add after-hours callback costs and frequency to the quarterly report (SOW §4.5). (4) Clarify that a Service Call that could reasonably be deferred to Business Hours without safety risk is not an After-Hours Callback.

### J. Annual Fee Adjustment Has No Cap

**Where:** MSA §5.5; SOW §5.1 and §5.3 (cross-referencing §5.5).

**The problem.** The Monthly Maintenance Fee and all labor rates may be adjusted annually by "the percentage increase in the wage rates payable to elevator mechanics under the collective bargaining agreement then in effect for the New York City area." There is no ceiling on the percentage.

**Why it matters.** CBA wage increases for NYC elevator mechanics have historically been in the 4–8% range, but in a period of significant inflation or a contentious contract negotiation, the increase could be higher. Over a five-year renewal term, compounding uncapped increases could add 25–40% to the base fee. Combined with the after-hours callback exposure (Part I), the total cost of the engagement is difficult to forecast.

**Recommended fix.** Cap the annual adjustment at a fixed percentage (e.g., 5%) or at the lesser of the CBA increase and CPI. If the CBA increase exceeds the cap, the excess is not payable.

### K. Takeover Inspection Report Is Missing from the File

**Where:** SOW §2.3; SOW Schedule B (last paragraph).

**The problem.** SOW §2.3 required Provider to complete a takeover inspection of every Unit within 45 days of the SOW Effective Date (i.e., by April 15, 2023) and deliver a written report. The report was to be "incorporated into Schedule B on delivery." We do not have this report in the matter folder.

**Why it matters.** The takeover inspection report is the baseline for allocating repair responsibility. Under §2.3, Provider "shall not charge Customer for repairing any condition that Provider does not identify in that report and that existed on the SOW Effective Date." Without the report, Negel cannot verify whether Zaigath has been billing for pre-existing conditions that should have been identified at takeover. This is a potential source of overbilling that has been ongoing since 2023.

**Recommended fix.** Request the takeover inspection report from Zaigath immediately. Review it against time-and-material invoices since March 2023 to identify any charges for conditions that were (or should have been) identified at takeover. If Zaigath cannot produce the report, that is itself a breach of SOW §2.3 and a potential basis for a claim.

### L. Press Release Characterization

**Where:** Zaigath Press Release, §5 ("Transition"); MSA §9.4 (Publicity).

**The problem.** The press release (issued March 6, 2023, with Negel's written approval per its footnote) states: "The initial term of the agreement is three years." It does not mention the five-year auto-renewal. While §9.4 was satisfied (Negel approved the content), the public characterization of the deal as a three-year engagement is inconsistent with the actual contractual structure (three-year initial term + automatic five-year renewals).

**Why it matters.** In the renewal dispute, this is a minor but useful data point. It shows that at the time of signing, the publicly communicated understanding was a three-year engagement. It does not change the contractual analysis, but it undercuts any argument by Zaigath that the renewal was a central, well-understood feature of the deal that Negel should have appreciated.

**Recommended fix.** No contractual fix needed. Preserve the press release as evidence in the renewal dispute.

### M. No Performance Security

**Where:** MSA §13 (Insurance) — no bond or security requirement.

**The problem.** The MSA requires insurance but does not require a performance bond, letter of credit, or other security.

**Why it matters.** Elevator service is critical infrastructure. If Zaigath were to walk away (e.g., due to financial distress, a labor dispute, or a strategic decision to exit the market), Negel would have no security to fund emergency replacement services. The insurance requirements (§13.1) protect against third-party claims, not against Provider's failure to perform.

**Recommended fix.** If the renewal is in effect, negotiate a requirement for a performance bond or letter of credit in an amount equal to 3–6 months of the Monthly Maintenance Fee, drawable upon Provider's material breach or insolvency.
