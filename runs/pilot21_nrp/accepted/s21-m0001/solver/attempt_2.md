## Overview

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement (the "SPA"), the Letter of Intent dated March 18, 2026 (the "LOI"), the Seller Note at Exhibit B to the SPA (the "Final Note"), and the buyer's earlier draft of the Seller Note dated June 2, 2026 (the "Buyer Draft"). Several provisions in the Final Note and the SPA depart materially from the positions reflected in the LOI and the Buyer Draft in ways that increase Bougairt's exposure as both buyer and maker of the note. The issues are ranked by severity below.

## Issue 1 – Set-Off Cap in the Final Note Overrides the SPA's Indemnification Carve-Outs

**Where:** Final Note § 4.2; SPA § 9.5(a)–(c); SPA § 11.9; LOI § 6(d); Buyer Draft § 4.3.

**What the documents say:**

- SPA § 9.5(a) sets a $540,000 deductible and a $7,200,000 General Cap for Seller's liability under § 9.2(a) (breach of non-fundamental reps).
- SPA § 9.5(c) provides that the deductible and General Cap do **not** apply to Fundamental Representation breaches or Product Recall Losses; those are capped only at the full Purchase Price ($72,000,000).
- LOI § 6(d) confirms this structure: "Claims for breach of fundamental representations and recall-related losses will not be subject to the deductible or that cap. These claims will instead be limited to the Purchase Price."
- Buyer Draft § 4.3 correctly tracks the SPA: set-off is "subject to the deductible and the limitations on liability in Section 9.5 of the Purchase Agreement, **as applicable to the claim in question**."
- Final Note § 4.2 states: "The aggregate Losses recoverable by the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **whether by set-off under this Note or otherwise**, shall not exceed the General Cap, **including Losses described in Section 9.5(c) of the Purchase Agreement**."

- SPA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence) provides that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to set-off and recovery of indemnifiable Losses."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

Final Note § 4.2 collapses the SPA's tiered indemnification structure into a single $7.2M ceiling for *all* claims, explicitly sweeping in the very categories (Fundamental Reps and Product Recall Losses) that the SPA and LOI carve out from the General Cap. Because SPA § 11.9 gives the Note control on set-off and recovery, the Note's cap would override the SPA's higher ceiling. In a pet-food business where a single recall event could generate tens of millions in Product Recall Losses (defined in SPA § 1.1 to include notification, retrieval, destruction, replacement, customer credits, and chargebacks), Bougairt's recovery by set-off would be capped at $7.2M rather than $72M. The practical effect is that the Seller Note becomes the primary recovery vehicle (per SPA § 9.7), and that vehicle is capped far below the SPA's intended protection.

**Recommended fix:**

Strike Final Note § 4.2 in its entirety and replace with language tracking Buyer Draft § 4.3: set-off is subject to the deductible and caps in SPA § 9.5 "as applicable to the claim in question." Alternatively, amend § 4.2 to state that Losses described in SPA § 9.5(c) (Fundamental Reps and Product Recall Losses) are recoverable by set-off up to the Purchase Price, consistent with SPA § 9.5(c). Confirm that SPA § 11.9's order-of-precedence carve-out for the Note does not swallow the SPA's indemnification structure.

## Issue 2 – Free Transferability of the Seller Note

**Where:** Final Note § 8; LOI § 3(f); Buyer Draft § 8.1.

**What the documents say:**

- LOI § 3(f): "Not transferable by Seller without Buyer's prior written consent."
- Buyer Draft § 8.1: "Holder may not sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, or any interest in it, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of Maker. Any purported transfer without that consent is void."
- Final Note § 8: "Holder may sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, in whole or in part, to **any Person without the consent of Maker**."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The Final Note permits Drigask to sell or pledge the note to any third party—potentially a distressed-debt fund, a competitor, or an entity with no business relationship to Bougairt—without Bougairt's approval. This is problematic for several reasons:

1. **Information exposure.** Combined with the expanded reporting rights in Final Note § 5 (see Issue 3), Bougairt's monthly financials and customer-level sales data could flow to unknown transferees.
2. **Enforcement risk.** A hostile or aggressive noteholder may be more likely to declare an Event of Default or pursue acceleration, and Bougairt loses the ability to vet who sits across from it as a creditor.
3. **Negotiating leverage.** A third-party holder with no ongoing relationship to the transaction may be less willing to accept set-off, extend maturities, or accommodate operational realities.
4. **Non-compete interplay.** If the note is transferred to an entity in the pet-food space, the customer-level data delivered under § 5(b) could facilitate competitive harm that the five-year non-compete (SPA § 6.10) was designed to prevent.

**Recommended fix:**

Restore Buyer Draft § 8.1: Holder may not transfer without Maker's prior written consent, and any purported transfer without consent is void. If the other side resists an absolute bar, propose a middle ground: transfer permitted only to an Affiliate of Holder or to a financial institution, in each case subject to (a) prior written notice to Maker, (b) the transferee's written assumption of the set-off, subordination, and (if added) confidentiality obligations, and (c) Maker's right to object if the transferee is a competitor of the Company.

## Issue 3 – Dramatically Expanded Information Rights Without Confidentiality Protection

**Where:** Final Note § 5; Buyer Draft § 5; LOI (silent on reporting specifics).

**What the documents say:**

| Element | Buyer Draft § 5 | Final Note § 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Periodic financials | Quarterly (45 days after quarter-end) | **Monthly** (30 days after month-end) |
| Customer-level data | None | **Customer-level sales, volumes, gross margin by customer and product line, monthly** |
| Annual audited | 120 days | 120 days (same) |
| Open-ended requests | None | **"Promptly after Holder's reasonable request, such other information… as Holder may reasonably request"** |
| Confidentiality on Holder | Yes – detailed restrictions | **None** |

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

- **Monthly consolidated financials** of Bougairt and all its subsidiaries (not just the Company) are a significant burden and a competitive sensitivity. The Buyer Draft's quarterly cadence was already generous for a seller note.
- **Customer-level gross margin data** is the type of information a competitor or a lender to a competitor would pay for. Given that Fenwold is the Company's largest customer (SPA § 4.9(b)) and that the note is freely transferable (Issue 2), this data could end up in the hands of an unknown party with no confidentiality obligation.
- **The open-ended request right in § 5(d)** has no scope limitation, no response deadline defined beyond "promptly," and no standard beyond "reasonably request." It effectively gives Holder a continuing audit right over Bougairt's entire enterprise.
- **Absence of confidentiality.** The Buyer Draft imposed express confidentiality obligations on Holder. The Final Note contains none. If the note is transferred (per Issue 2), there is no contractual restriction on the transferee's use or disclosure of the information.

**Recommended fix:**

1. Revert to quarterly (not monthly) consolidated financial reporting, consistent with the Buyer Draft.
2. Delete customer-level sales reporting (§ 5(b)) in its entirety, or limit it to aggregate revenue by product line without customer identification.
3. Delete the open-ended request right (§ 5(d)) or narrow it to information reasonably necessary for Holder to assess Maker's ability to pay under the Note, with a 15-business-day response period.
4. Restore the confidentiality provisions from Buyer Draft § 5 and make them run to any transferee.
5. If the other side insists on monthly reporting, limit it to the Company (not Bougairt and all subsidiaries) and add a confidentiality obligation as a condition.

## Issue 4 – Fenwold Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Closing Conditions

**Where:** SPA § 7.2(c); SPA § 6.4; SPA § 4.9(b); LOI § 7(d).

**What the documents say:**

- LOI § 7(d) made receipt of Fenwold's consent to the change of control "in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer" an express closing condition.
- SPA § 4.9(b) acknowledges that the Fenwold Supply Agreement "requires the prior written consent of Fenwold" for a change of control and "may terminate upon a change of control of the Company effected without that consent."
- SPA § 7.2(c) lists only two consents as closing conditions: the Plant Lease landlord and the Equipment Financing Agreement lender. **Fenwold is absent.**
- SPA § 6.4 (covenant to obtain consents) refers only to "the third-party consents listed in Section 7.2(c)"—again, Fenwold is not included.

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The Fenwold Supply Agreement is the Company's largest customer contract by revenue (SPA § 4.9(b)). If Fenwold does not consent, the agreement may terminate automatically upon closing. Yet Bougairt has no contractual right to refuse to close if Fenwold consent is not obtained, because it is not a condition under § 7.2. There is also no affirmative covenant requiring Seller to obtain it (§ 6.4 covers only § 7.2(c) consents). The result: Bougairt could be forced to close a $72M acquisition of a pet-food manufacturer whose largest customer contract is terminable at the customer's option, with no remedy short of a post-closing indemnification claim (itself subject to the caps and survival issues noted above).

**Recommended fix:**

Add Fenwold's consent to SPA § 7.2(c) as item (iii), in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer. Correspondingly, the consent will fall within the scope of the § 6.4 covenant. If the other side argues that making it a hard condition gives Fenwold too much leverage, an alternative is to add it as a condition subject to a long-stop date extension of 30 days, or to add a specific covenant in § 6.4 requiring Seller to use best efforts (not merely commercially reasonable efforts) to obtain Fenwold's consent before Closing.

**Fact needed from client:** Has Fenwold been approached? Is there any indication of their posture? If Fenwold is already informally supportive, the other side may resist making it a formal condition on the theory that it is a "sure thing."

## Issue 5 – Survival Period for General Representations Cut from 18 Months to 9 Months

**Where:** SPA § 9.1; LOI § 6(b); SPA § 4.14(c).

**What the documents say:**

- LOI § 6(b): "Seller's general representations and warranties, **including the product safety and recall representations**, will survive the Closing until **18 months** after Closing."
- SPA § 9.1: "All other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement, **including the representations and warranties in Section 4.14**, shall survive the Closing until **nine (9) months** following the Closing Date."
- SPA § 4.14(c): The Company's dry products carry a shelf life of up to **eighteen (18) months** from the date of manufacture.

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The 9-month survival period for the product safety and recall representations (§ 4.14) is less than half the product shelf life. A lot manufactured just before Closing could remain in the channel (distributors, retail customers) for up to 18 months post-Closing. If a contamination issue (e.g., aflatoxin, per the specific reference in § 4.14(a)) manifests in month 10 or 12, the representation has already expired and Bougairt cannot make a claim "in respect of" that representation under § 9.1's notice requirement.

While Product Recall Losses under § 9.2(c) are a standalone indemnity (not strictly "in respect of a representation or warranty"), the practical difficulty is that most recall claims will be framed as, or intertwined with, a breach of the § 4.14 reps (e.g., the product was adulterated at the time of manufacture, in violation of § 4.14(a)). If the survival period has lapsed, the other side will argue the claim is time-barred. The ambiguity itself is a risk.

**Recommended fix:**

Extend the survival period for § 4.14 (Product Safety; Recalls) to 18 months post-Closing, consistent with the LOI and the product shelf life. Alternatively, extend all general rep survival to 18 months as the LOI contemplated. At a minimum, add clarifying language to § 9.1 stating that the survival period does not limit claims under § 9.2(c) (Product Recall Losses) as a standalone indemnity, regardless of whether the underlying facts also constitute a rep breach.

## Issue 6 – No Material Adverse Change Closing Condition

**Where:** SPA § 7.2; LOI § 7(g).

**What the documents say:**

- LOI § 7(g): Closing subject to "the absence of any material adverse change in the business, assets, condition or results of operations of the Company."
- SPA § 7.2: Lists five conditions (reps true, covenants performed, consents obtained, officer's certificate, no recall). **No MAC condition.**

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

SPA § 4.7 (Absence of Changes) is a representation, not a closing condition. If a material adverse event occurs between signing and closing that does not rise to the level of a recall (which is covered by § 7.2(e)) or a rep breach (which is covered by § 7.2(a) only if it renders the rep untrue "in all material respects"), Bougairt must still close. For example, if the Company loses a key ingredient supplier, suffers a major equipment failure, or experiences a 30% revenue decline in the month before Closing, none of these would necessarily trigger a failure of a § 7.2 condition, yet they would materially impair the asset Bougairt is purchasing.

**Recommended fix:**

Add a MAC condition to SPA § 7.2: "Since the date of this Agreement, there has not occurred any Material Adverse Change." Define "Material Adverse Change" in § 1.1 with appropriate carve-outs (general economic conditions, industry-wide trends, the pendency of the transaction itself). This was in the LOI and should be restored.

## Issue 7 – Buyer's Indemnity for Seller Guarantees Is Broadly Defined

**Where:** SPA § 9.3(c); SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Seller Guarantees"); SPA § 6.8.

**What the documents say:**

- SPA § 1.1 defines "Seller Guarantees" to include "any other guaranty, keep-well, letter of credit, surety bond or other credit support provided by Seller or any of its Affiliates for any obligation of the Company, **whether arising before or after the Closing**."
- SPA § 6.8 requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain release of the guaranties listed in the Disclosure Schedule, but does not guarantee release.
- SPA § 9.3(c): Buyer indemnifies Seller for "Losses arising under any Seller Guarantee."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The definition of "Seller Guarantees" captures post-Closing obligations. If the Company incurs new debt after Closing that Seller had guaranteed pre-Closing (or if a pre-Closing guaranty is triggered by a post-Closing event), Buyer indemnifies Seller for the resulting losses. The "commercially reasonable efforts" standard in § 6.8 means Buyer is not obligated to pay a release fee, but if the guarantor (e.g., a bank) demands one, Buyer may be stuck: it cannot force the release, and if the guaranty is eventually called, Buyer indemnifies Seller under § 9.3(c). This creates a potential uncapped (subject only to the $7.2M Buyer Cap in § 9.5(b)) exposure for an obligation that is, in economic substance, the Company's (and thus now Buyer's) own debt.

**Recommended fix:**

Narrow the "Seller Guarantees" definition to exclude obligations arising after the Closing Date. Alternatively, clarify that § 9.3(c) applies only to Seller Guarantees that were in effect immediately prior to Closing and that Buyer has failed to use commercially reasonable efforts to release. At a minimum, confirm with the client what specific guaranties are listed in Section 6.8 of the Disclosure Schedule and whether any are likely to be difficult to release.

**Fact needed from client:** What guaranties are listed in Section 6.8 of the Disclosure Schedule? Are any in favor of the Equipment Financing Agreement lender (which would be addressed by the § 7.2(c) consent and payoff)? Are any in favor of trade creditors or tax authorities?

## Issue 8 – Order of Precedence Elevates the Note Over the SPA on Recovery

**Where:** SPA § 11.9; SPA § 9.7.

**What the documents say:**

SPA § 11.9: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any other Transaction Document, this Agreement shall prevail, **except that the Seller Note shall control with respect to set-off and recovery of indemnifiable Losses**."

SPA § 9.7: Recovery is "first by set-off against the principal and accrued interest outstanding under the Seller Note, **in accordance with Section 4 of the Seller Note**."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

This provision is the mechanism that makes Issue 1 operative. Even if the SPA's indemnification article provides for higher caps on certain claims, the Note's § 4.2 controls the actual recovery. The § 11.9 carve-out was presumably added to give the Note's set-off mechanics (application order, notice, withholding for pending claims) primacy, but as drafted it sweeps in the *substantive cap* in Note § 4.2, not just the *procedural* mechanics.

**Recommended fix:**

Narrow SPA § 11.9 to provide that the Seller Note controls only with respect to the *procedural mechanics* of set-off (application order, notice requirements, withholding for pending claims, treatment as payment), and not with respect to the *amounts* recoverable under Article IX. The amounts should be governed exclusively by SPA § 9.5. This fix is interdependent with Issue 1: if Note § 4.2 is corrected, the § 11.9 issue becomes less acute, but the clarification is still advisable to prevent future ambiguity.

## Issue 9 – Final Note Adds "Successors and Assigns" to Holder Definition

**Where:** Final Note, preamble; Buyer Draft, preamble.

**What the documents say:**

- Buyer Draft: "promises to pay to the order of Drigask Holdings, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company ('**Holder**')."
- Final Note: "promises to pay to the order of Drigask Holdings, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company (**together with its successors and assigns**, '**Holder**')."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

This is a small textual change with significant consequences when read alongside the free-transferability provision (Issue 2). By defining Holder to include "successors and assigns," the Note makes explicit that any transferee is a "Holder" entitled to all rights under the Note (information, payment, acceleration, etc.) without any further action. It also means that if Drigask merges, is acquired, or otherwise transfers its assets, the note automatically follows to the successor without notice to or consent of Bougairt.

**Recommended fix:**

If Issue 2 is resolved by restoring transfer restrictions, this becomes less critical. If free transferability is accepted as a compromise, at minimum require that the "successors and assigns" language be coupled with a notice obligation (already in § 8) and that the transferee expressly assume the set-off, subordination, and confidentiality obligations.

## Issue 10 – No Express Cross-Default Between the SPA and the Note

**Where:** Final Note § 9.1; SPA (no corresponding provision).

**What the documents say:**

Final Note § 9.1 lists Events of Default under the Note (payment default, covenant breach, bankruptcy). The SPA does not provide that a material breach of the SPA constitutes an Event of Default under the Note, nor does the Note provide that a termination of the SPA accelerates the Note.

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

This is actually *favorable* to Bougairt as Maker (it limits the circumstances in which the Note can be accelerated). However, it cuts both ways: if Seller wants to argue that a Bougairt breach of the SPA (e.g., failure to obtain Fenwold consent, if that were a covenant) should accelerate the Note, there is no textual hook. More practically, the absence of a cross-default means that if the SPA is terminated pre-Closing (e.g., under § 8.1(b) for failure to close by December 31, 2026), the Note is never issued and there is no issue. But if the SPA is terminated post-Closing for some reason (which § 8.1 does not contemplate—termination is only "before the Closing"), the Note would continue unaffected. This is not a pressing issue but worth noting for completeness.

**No action recommended** unless the other side raises it.

Confirm whether Bougairt's accounting function can support this cadence without material additional cost, as this affects the practicality of negotiating a revert to quarterly.
