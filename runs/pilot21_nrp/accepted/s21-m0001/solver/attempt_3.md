## Issue 1: Seller Note § 4.2 Caps All Indemnification Recovery at the General Cap, Overriding SPA Carve-Outs

**Where:** Seller Note (Exhibit B), Section 4.2 ("Limitation on Recovery"); compare SPA Section 9.5(c) and Section 11.9.

**What the documents say:**

Final Exhibit B, § 4.2 provides:

> "The aggregate Losses recoverable by the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **whether by set-off under this Note or otherwise**, shall not exceed the General Cap, **including Losses described in Section 9.5(c) of the Purchase Agreement**."

The General Cap is $7,200,000 (SPA § 9.5(a)).

SPA § 9.5(c), by contrast, provides that the General Cap does **not** apply to Fundamental Representation breaches or Product Recall Losses; those are instead capped at the full Purchase Price ($72,000,000).

SPA § 11.9 (Order of Precedence) states: "the Seller Note shall control with respect to set-off and recovery of indemnifiable Losses."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

Because § 11.9 gives the Note control over recovery of indemnifiable Losses, § 4.2 as drafted would override the SPA's carve-outs. If a pre-Closing product recall resulted in $40 million of Product Recall Losses, Bougairt's recovery would be capped at $7.2 million rather than $72 million. The same applies to Fundamental Rep breaches (e.g., a title defect in the Shares). The phrase "whether by set-off under this Note or otherwise" makes clear the cap applies to direct recovery from Seller as well, not just the set-off mechanism.

The buyer's own June 2 draft (Section 4.3) correctly tracked the SPA: "Set-off under this Section 4 is subject to the deductible and the limitations on liability in Section 9.5 of the Purchase Agreement, **as applicable to the claim in question**." The final note departed from this in a way that is materially adverse.

**Recommended fix:**

Restore the buyer draft's approach. Replace § 4.2 with language providing that set-off and recovery are subject to the limitations in SPA § 9.5 "as applicable to the claim in question," so that Fundamental Rep and Product Recall Losses remain subject to the Purchase Price cap (and not the General Cap). Alternatively, delete the phrase "including Losses described in Section 9.5(c)" and add: "provided that Losses described in Section 9.5(c) of the Purchase Agreement shall be subject to the limitations set forth in that Section."

## Issue 2: Fenwold Change-of-Control Consent Omitted from Closing Conditions

**Where:** SPA Section 7.2(c); compare LOI Section 7(d) and SPA Section 4.9(b).

**What the documents say:**

SPA § 7.2(c) conditions Buyer's closing obligation on delivery of only two consents: (i) the Plant Lease landlord and (ii) the Equipment Financing Agreement lender.

LOI § 7(d) specifically listed as a closing condition: "receipt of the consent of Fenwold Pet Supply Co. ('Fenwold') to the change of control of the Company under the Company's private-label supply agreement with Fenwold, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer."

SPA § 4.9(b) confirms that the Fenwold Supply Agreement "is the Company's largest customer contract by revenue" and that "a change of control of the Company requires the prior written consent of Fenwold, and the Fenwold Supply Agreement may terminate upon a change of control of the Company effected without that consent."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

Fenwold is the Company's largest customer. If the transaction closes without Fenwold's consent, the supply agreement is terminable at Fenwold's option. Bougairt would have paid $72 million for a business whose top revenue stream can be pulled out from under it. The absence of this condition means Seller could walk Bougairt into closing without having secured the consent, and Bougairt would have no contractual right to refuse to close on that ground (short of arguing the § 4.9(b) rep is inaccurate, which would be a narrow and fact-specific argument).

**Recommended fix:**

Add to SPA § 7.2(c): "(iii) the consent of Fenwold under the Fenwold Supply Agreement to the change of control of the Company, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer." This restores the LOI term and protects the single most important commercial relationship in the deal.

## Issue 3: Seller Note § 8 Permits Free Transfer Without Maker Consent

**Where:** Seller Note (Exhibit B), Section 8; compare LOI Section 3(f) and Buyer Draft Section 8.1.

**What the documents say:**

Final Exhibit B, § 8: "Holder may sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, in whole or in part, **to any Person without the consent of Maker**."

LOI § 3(f): "Not transferable by Seller without Buyer's prior written consent."

Buyer Draft § 8.1: "Holder may not sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, or any interest in it, in whole or in part, **without the prior written consent of Maker**. Any purported transfer without that consent is void."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

Bougairt is the Maker. Free transferability means Drigask could sell or pledge the $14 million note to any third party—a competitor, a distressed-debt fund, or an entity with no business relationship to Bougairt—without Bougairt's approval. Combined with the monthly customer-level reporting in § 5 (see Issue 4) and the absence of any confidentiality obligation on the Holder in the final note, Bougairt's competitively sensitive financial data could end up in the hands of an unknown party. The buyer draft's requirement of written transferee confirmation (Draft § 8.2) is also absent from the final note.

**Recommended fix:**

Restore the buyer draft's § 8.1 transfer restriction (no transfer without Maker's prior written consent; purported transfers void). At minimum, require that any transferee (a) be an Affiliate of Drigask or an entity Drigask controls, (b) confirm in writing that it is bound by the set-off, subordination, and any confidentiality provisions, and (c) not be a competitor of the Company. If the other side resists a full consent requirement, propose a limited list of permitted transferees or a right of first refusal.

## Issue 4: Seller Note § 5 Imposes Monthly Customer-Level Reporting Without Confidentiality Protections

**Where:** Seller Note (Exhibit B), Section 5; compare Buyer Draft Section 5.

**What the documents say:**

Final Exhibit B, § 5 requires Maker to deliver:

- (a) **Monthly** unaudited consolidated balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement (within 30 days of month-end);
- (b) **Monthly customer-level sales reports** for the Company, "showing net sales, volumes and **gross margin by customer and by product line**";
- (c) Annual audited consolidated financial statements (within 120 days); and
- (d) "promptly after Holder's reasonable request, **such other information** about the financial condition and operations of Maker and the Company **as Holder may reasonably request**."

There is **no confidentiality obligation** on Holder.

Buyer Draft, § 5 required only:

- (a) **Quarterly** unaudited consolidated financials (within 45 days, first three quarters only);
- (b) Annual audited financials (within 120 days);
- And included an express confidentiality clause: "Holder shall hold all information delivered under this Section 5 in confidence. Holder shall use that information only to monitor its investment in this Note."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The monthly customer-level gross margin data reveals Bougairt's pricing structure and margin profile for every customer, including Fenwold (the largest). This is competitively sensitive information that a former owner (or, given Issue 3, an unknown transferee) could use to the Company's detriment. The open-ended request right in § 5(d) has no scope limitation. The absence of a confidentiality clause means there is no contractual restriction on how the Holder uses or discloses the information. The reporting burden (monthly rather than quarterly) also creates ongoing administrative cost and exposure.

**Recommended fix:**

- Reduce reporting frequency to quarterly (matching the buyer draft).
- Delete § 5(b) (customer-level sales reports) in its entirety, or limit it to aggregate Company revenue by product line without customer identification or margin data.
- Delete or narrowly tailor § 5(d) to requests that are "reasonably necessary for Holder to assess its credit exposure under this Note."
- Restore the confidentiality clause from the buyer draft, binding on Holder and any permitted transferee.

## Issue 5: Product Safety and Recall Representation Survival Reduced from 18 Months to 9 Months

**Where:** SPA Section 9.1; compare LOI Section 6(b) and SPA Section 4.14(c).

**What the documents say:**

SPA § 9.1: "All other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement, **including the representations and warranties in Section 4.14**, shall survive the Closing until **nine (9) months** following the Closing Date."

LOI § 6(b): "Seller's general representations and warranties, **including the product safety and recall representations**, will survive the Closing until **18 months** after Closing."

SPA § 4.14(c): "The Company's dry products carry a shelf life of up to **eighteen (18) months** from the date of manufacture."

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

The Company's products remain in the market for up to 18 months after manufacture. A lot manufactured in June 2026 could be consumed by a customer as late as December 2027. If a product safety issue (e.g., aflatoxin contamination, pathogen presence) manifests in month 10 or 12 post-Closing, the § 4.14 representation will have already expired under the 9-month survival period, and Bougairt will have no indemnification claim—despite the fact that the LOI expressly negotiated 18 months for these reps. The 9-month period also means the set-off window under the Seller Note closes before the full shelf life has elapsed.

**Recommended fix:**

Carve Section 4.14 out of the general 9-month survival period and provide that it survives for 18 months post-Closing (matching the LOI and the product shelf life). Alternatively, tie survival to the expiration of the longest shelf life of any product lot in the Disclosure Schedule inventory as of Closing.

## Issue 6: Material Adverse Change Closing Condition Present in LOI but Absent from SPA

**Where:** LOI Section 7(g); SPA Article VII (no corresponding condition).

**What the documents say:**

LOI § 7(g): "the absence of any material adverse change in the business, assets, condition or results of operations of the Company."

SPA § 7.2 contains five conditions (reps, covenants, consents, officer's certificate, no recall) but **no MAC condition**.

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

Without a MAC closing condition, Bougairt is obligated to close even if the business deteriorates materially between signing and closing (e.g., loss of a key supplier, a significant customer other than Fenwold reducing orders, a regulatory sanction that doesn't rise to the level of a "recall"). The only protection is the § 4.7 representation (no material adverse change since December 31, 2025), but a rep breach gives rise only to indemnification after closing—it does not give Bougairt the right to walk away before closing (unless the breach is so severe that § 7.2(a) is not satisfied, which requires the rep to be untrue "in all material respects").

**Recommended fix:**

Add to SPA § 7.2: "(f) since the date of this Agreement, there shall not have occurred a Material Adverse Change." Define "Material Adverse Change" in Article I with appropriate carve-outs (general economic conditions, effects of the pandemic if still relevant, etc.).

## Issue 7: Open-Ended Information Request Right in Seller Note § 5(d)

**Where:** Seller Note (Exhibit B), Section 5(d).

**What the documents say:**

> "promptly after Holder's reasonable request, such other information about the financial condition and operations of Maker and the Company as Holder may reasonably request."

This provision is absent from the buyer draft entirely.

**Why it matters to Bougairt:**

"Reasonably request" is an extremely low bar. Combined with the free-transferability in § 8 (Issue 3) and the absence of confidentiality (Issue 4), this gives Drigask—or any transferee—a perpetual right to demand any operational or financial information about Bougairt and the Company for as long as the note is outstanding (30 months). This could be used to extract competitive intelligence well beyond what is needed to monitor credit risk on a $14 million note.

**Recommended fix:**

Delete § 5(d), or limit it to: "such additional financial information as is reasonably necessary for Holder to assess its exposure under this Note, provided that no request shall require disclosure of customer-level pricing, margin, or formulation data."
