## Critical Issues

### 1. Note §4.2 Caps Set-Off at the General Cap, Contradicting the SPA Carve-Out for Recall Losses and Fundamental Representations

**Where:** Final Note §4.2; SPA §9.5(a)–(c); SPA §11.9.

**The problem.** SPA §9.5(c) expressly provides that the $7.2 million General Cap does *not* apply to (i) breaches of Fundamental Representations or (ii) Product Recall Losses. For those categories, Seller's aggregate liability is capped only at the full Purchase Price ($72 million). However, Final Note §4.2 states:

> "The aggregate Losses recoverable by the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **whether by set-off under this Note or otherwise**, shall not exceed the General Cap, **including Losses described in Section 9.5(c)** of the Purchase Agreement."

Read plainly, this subordinates the SPA's carve-out to the note's $7.2 million cap for *all* indemnification recovery, including the very losses the SPA says are excluded from the General Cap. SPA §11.9 then compounds the problem by providing that "the Seller Note shall control with respect to set-off and recovery of indemnifiable Losses." The result: Bougairt's maximum set-off against the $14 million note for a catastrophic product recall or a fundamental representation breach (e.g., Seller never owned the shares) is $7.2 million, not up to $72 million.

The Buyer Draft did not contain this limitation. Its §4.3 provided that set-off is "subject to the deductible and the limitations on liability in Section 9.5 of the Purchase Agreement, **as applicable to the claim in question**"—i.e., the correct SPA limitations applied claim-by-claim, preserving the carve-out.

**Why it matters.** Product Recall Losses are the single largest potential exposure in a pet food acquisition. The SPA's own definition (§1.1) contemplates "costs of notification, retrieval, destruction, replacement, customer credits and chargebacks." A single mycotoxin or pathogen event (the exact risks flagged in SPA §4.14(a)) could generate recall costs well in excess of $7.2 million. Capping set-off at that level forces Bougairt to chase Seller directly for the excess—after Seller has already received $58 million in cash at closing and may have distributed it.

**Recommended fix.** Delete Final Note §4.2 in its entirety and replace with language tracking Buyer Draft §4.3: set-off is subject to the deductible and limitations in SPA §9.5 *as applicable to the claim in question*, so that the §9.5(c) carve-out operates as intended. Alternatively, amend §4.2 to state that the General Cap in §4.2 does not apply to Losses described in SPA §9.5(c), consistent with the SPA.

### 2. Fenwold Consent Dropped as a Closing Condition

**Where:** LOI §7(d); SPA §7.2(c); SPA §4.9(b).

**The problem.** The LOI made receipt of Fenwold's change-of-control consent "in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer" an express condition to closing (LOI §7(d)). The SPA's closing conditions in §7.2(c) list only two consents: the Plant Lease landlord and the Equipment Financing Agreement lender. Fenwold is absent.

Meanwhile, SPA §4.9(b) concedes that the Fenwold Supply Agreement "is the Company's largest customer contract by revenue" and that it "may terminate upon a change of control of the Company effected without that consent." The only remaining protection is the representation in §4.9(b) that Fenwold "has not given notice of any intention to terminate, not renew or materially reduce its purchases"—a snapshot in time that provides no forward-looking protection and survives only nine months (see Issue 4 below).

**Why it matters.** If Bougairt closes without Fenwold's written consent, the Company's largest revenue contract is terminable at Fenwold's option. There is no closing condition to walk, no specific indemnity for loss of the Fenwold contract, and the general rep survival period (nine months) may expire before Fenwold exercises its termination right. Bougairt would be paying $72 million for a business whose top customer can walk the day after closing.

**Recommended fix.** Add Fenwold's consent to SPA §7.2(c) as a third item, in form and substance reasonably satisfactory to Buyer, restoring the LOI position. If Seller resists (e.g., Fenwold is unresponsive), at minimum add a specific indemnity in SPA §9.2 for Losses arising from termination or material reduction of the Fenwold Supply Agreement resulting from the change of control, with a survival period no shorter than the contract term.

### 3. Seller Note Is Freely Transferable Without Buyer Consent

**Where:** LOI §3(f); Buyer Draft §8.1; Final Note §8; Final Note preamble.

**The problem.** The LOI stated the note would be "[n]ot transferable by Seller without Buyer's prior written consent" (LOI §3(f)). The Buyer Draft implemented this: "Holder may not sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, or any interest in it, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of Maker. Any purported transfer without that consent is void" (Buyer Draft §8.1). The Buyer Draft also required any permitted transferee to confirm the set-off, subordination, and confidentiality terms in writing before the transfer became effective (Buyer Draft §8.2).

The Final Note reverses all of this. Section 8 now provides: "Holder may sell, assign, pledge or otherwise transfer this Note, in whole or in part, to any Person **without the consent of Maker**." The only obligation on Holder is to give notice of the transfer within five Business Days *after* it occurs. The preamble's transfer restriction ("IT MAY NOT BE SOLD, ASSIGNED, PLEDGED OR OTHERWISE TRANSFERRED EXCEPT AS PROVIDED IN SECTION 8") was also deleted. The definition of "Holder" was expanded to include "successors and assigns."

**Why it matters.** Free transferability creates three compounding risks:

- *Set-off enforcement.* Although Final Note §8 states each transferee "takes this Note subject to the right of set-off under Section 4," exercising set-off against a stranger (a fund, an individual, an entity in another jurisdiction) is far more difficult than against a known counterparty with whom Bougairt has an ongoing contractual relationship.
- *Information leakage.* Combined with the absence of any confidentiality obligation on Holder (see Issue 5), monthly customer-level financial data could end up in the hands of an unknown third party with no contractual duty to protect it.
- *Loss of leverage.* In any dispute, Bougairt currently has a single known counterparty. A transferred note could be held by a party with no relationship to the transaction and no incentive to negotiate.

**Recommended fix.** Restore Buyer Draft §8.1 (transfer only with Maker's prior written consent; purported transfer without consent is void) and §8.2 (transferee must confirm set-off, subordination, and confidentiality terms in writing before the transfer is effective). If Seller insists on some transferability, limit it to Affiliates of Seller and require the same written confirmation.

### 4. Survival Period for Product Safety and Recall Representations Cut from 18 Months to 9 Months

**Where:** LOI §6(b); SPA §9.1; SPA §4.14(c).

**The problem.** The LOI provided that "Seller's general representations and warranties, **including the product safety and recall representations**, will survive the Closing until **18 months** after Closing" (LOI §6(b)). The SPA reduces this to nine months: "All other representations and warranties of Seller and Buyer contained in this Agreement, **including the representations and warranties in Section 4.14**, shall survive the Closing until **nine (9) months** following the Closing Date" (SPA §9.1).

SPA §4.14(c) confirms that the Company's dry products carry a shelf life of up to eighteen months from manufacture. A latent contamination issue (e.g., aflatoxin, salmonella) in a product manufactured near the Closing Date could manifest as a recall at month ten, eleven, or beyond—after the nine-month survival period has expired.

**Why it matters.** Although Product Recall Losses are covered by a standalone indemnity in SPA §9.2(c) (not a rep), the Final Note's set-off provision (§4.1) limits set-off to claims "for which written notice was delivered to Holder under Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement **before the expiration of the applicable survival period in Section 9.1**." If the "applicable survival period" for a recall claim is read as the nine-month period governing §4.14 reps, Bougairt could lose the ability to set off against the note for a recall that occurs after month nine—even though the underlying indemnity in §9.2(c) has no stated expiration. This ambiguity, combined with the shortened survival period, creates a real risk that the note (Bougairt's primary security) becomes unavailable for the most likely large loss scenario.

**Recommended fix.** Extend survival for Section 4.14 (Product Safety; Recalls) to 18 months, consistent with the LOI and the product shelf life. Additionally, amend Final Note §4.1 to clarify that the "applicable survival period" for claims under SPA §9.2(c) (Product Recall Losses) and §9.2(d) (pre-Closing Taxes) is the longer of (i) the survival period for the related representation, or (ii) the period during which the underlying indemnity remains available.

### 5. Reporting Obligations Significantly Expanded; Confidentiality Protection Deleted

**Where:** Buyer Draft §5; Final Note §5.

**The problem.** The Buyer Draft required quarterly reporting (unaudited consolidated financials within 45 days after each of the first three fiscal quarters, plus annual audited financials within 120 days) and included an express confidentiality obligation: "Holder shall hold all information delivered under this Section 5 in confidence. Holder shall use that information only to monitor its investment in this Note."

The Final Note replaces this with:

- **Monthly** unaudited consolidated financials (30 days after each calendar month) — Final Note §5(a);
- **Monthly customer-level sales reports** showing "net sales, volumes and gross margin by customer and by product line" — Final Note §5(b);
- Annual audited financials (120 days) — Final Note §5(c);
- A **catch-all** right for Holder to request "such other information about the financial condition and operations of Maker and the Company as Holder may reasonably request" — Final Note §5(d);
- **No confidentiality obligation whatsoever.**

**Why it matters.**

- *Operational burden.* Monthly consolidated reporting for a public or near-public company is unusual for a seller note and imposes significant accounting and disclosure costs on Bougairt for the life of the note (30 months).
- *Competitive sensitivity.* Customer-level gross margin data reveals Bougairt's pricing strategy, margin structure, and customer concentration at the Company level. In the pet food industry, this information is highly competitively sensitive.
- *No confidentiality + free transferability.* Because Final Note §8 permits transfer to "any Person" without consent, and §5 imposes no duty of confidence, Bougairt could be delivering monthly customer-level margin data to an unknown transferee (potentially a competitor or a fund with no obligation to protect it).
- *Catch-all.* The "reasonably request" standard in §5(d) is open-ended and could be used to extract information well beyond financial condition (e.g., pipeline products, formulation data, strategic plans).

**Recommended fix.**

- Revert to quarterly reporting per Buyer Draft §5(a)–(b).
- Delete Final Note §5(b) (customer-level sales reports) in its entirety.
- Delete Final Note §5(d) (catch-all), or limit it to information reasonably necessary for Holder to assess its credit exposure under the Note.
- Restore the confidentiality provision from Buyer Draft §5 in full.
- If any reporting is permitted, add: "Holder shall hold all information delivered under this Section 5 in strict confidence and shall not disclose it except to its professional advisors bound by confidentiality or as required by Law."

## Significant Issues

### 6. No Material Adverse Change Closing Condition

**Where:** LOI §7(g); SPA §7.2.

**The problem.** The LOI included "the absence of any material adverse change in the business, assets, condition or results of operations of the Company" as a closing condition (LOI §7(g)). The SPA's buyer conditions (§7.2) include bring-down of reps, covenant compliance, third-party consents, an officer's certificate, and no recall—but no standalone MAC condition.

**Why it matters.** If the Company suffers a significant business deterioration between signing (July 15, 2026) and closing (potentially up to December 31, 2026 under the outside date in §8.1(b))—for example, loss of a major distributor, a plant shutdown, or a sudden drop in orders—Bougairt has no express right to walk unless the event also constitutes a breach of a representation or covenant. The §4.7 "Absence of Changes" rep covers only changes "since December 31, 2025" and is subject to the materiality qualifier, which is scraped for indemnification purposes (§9.6) but not for the bring-down condition (§7.2(a) uses "true and correct in all material respects").

**Recommended fix.** Add to SPA §7.2: "(f) Since the date of this Agreement, there shall not have occurred any Material Adverse Change." Define "Material Adverse Change" in §1.1 with appropriate carve-outs (general economic conditions, effects of the pandemic if still relevant, etc.).

### 7. "Seller Guarantees" Definition Captures Post-Closing Guaranties, Creating Indemnity Exposure

**Where:** SPA §1.1 (definition of "Seller Guarantees"); SPA §9.3(c).

**The problem.** "Seller Guarantees" is defined to include "any other guaranty, keep-well, letter of credit, surety bond or other credit support provided by Seller or any of its Affiliates for any obligation of the Company, **whether arising before or after the Closing**" (SPA §1.1). SPA §9.3(c) then provides that Buyer indemnifies Seller for "Losses arising under any Seller Guarantee."

**Why it matters.** If, after closing, Seller voluntarily provides a guaranty or keep-well for a Company obligation (e.g., to support the Company in obtaining new financing during transition), and the Company later defaults, Seller pays under the guaranty and then turns to Bougairt for indemnification under §9.3(c). This creates an open-ended, uncapped-by-the-General-Cap indemnity obligation for Bougairt (the §9.5(b) Buyer Cap applies only to §9.3(a), not §9.3(c)). There is no stated limit on this exposure.

**Recommended fix.** Amend the definition of "Seller Guarantees" to cover only guaranties and credit support "provided by Seller or any of its Affiliates for any obligation of the Company **arising before the Closing**." Alternatively, add §9.3(c) to the scope of the Buyer Cap in §9.5(b).

### 8. Ambiguity in Note §4.1: "Applicable Survival Period" for Standalone Indemnities

**Where:** Final Note §4.1; SPA §9.1; SPA §9.2(c)–(d).

**The problem.** Final Note §4.1 limits set-off to claims "for which written notice was delivered to Holder under Section 9.4 of the Purchase Agreement before the expiration of the **applicable survival period in Section 9.1**." SPA §9.1 specifies survival periods for (i) Fundamental Representations (60 days after statute of limitations), (ii) all other reps and warranties (9 months), and (iii) covenants (in accordance with their terms). It does *not* specify a survival period for the standalone indemnities in SPA §9.2(c) (Product Recall Losses) or §9.2(d) (pre-Closing Taxes).

**Why it matters.** If there is no "applicable survival period" for a standalone indemnity, a literal reading of Note §4.1 could be argued to mean set-off is *never* available for those claims (because there is no period before which notice can be given). Alternatively, it could mean the limitation doesn't apply (because there's no period to expire). Either interpretation creates uncertainty at the moment Bougairt most needs clarity—when a recall occurs and it must decide whether to set off or pay the note and sue separately.

**Recommended fix.** Add to Final Note §4.1: "For purposes of this Section 4.1, the 'applicable survival period' for claims under Section 9.2(c) or 9.2(d) of the Purchase Agreement shall be [18 months / the full term of the Note], whichever is longer." This removes the ambiguity and aligns with the fix recommended in Issue 4.

### 9. Order of Precedence Entrenches the Note's Cap Over the SPA

**Where:** SPA §11.9; Final Note §4.2.

**The problem.** SPA §11.9 provides: "In the event of any conflict between this Agreement and any other Transaction Document, this Agreement shall prevail, **except that the Seller Note shall control with respect to set-off and recovery of indemnifiable Losses**."

This means that even if Bougairt succeeds in arguing that SPA §9.5(c) carves out recall losses from the General Cap, the Final Note's §4.2 (which explicitly subjects those losses to the General Cap for set-off purposes) would control. The SPA's own order-of-precedence clause hands Seller the argument.

**Why it matters.** This is the structural mechanism that makes Issue 1 so damaging. Fixing §4.2 of the note is necessary but may not be sufficient if the broader §11.9 language is left in place and other conflicts arise.

**Recommended fix.** In conjunction with the fix to Note §4.2 (Issue 1), amend SPA §11.9 to provide: "the Seller Note shall control with respect to the mechanics of set-off and payment under the Seller Note, **provided that the limitations on liability set forth in Section 9.5 of this Agreement shall apply to all indemnification claims regardless of the method of recovery**." This preserves the note's operational terms while ensuring the SPA's cap structure governs the substantive limits.

### 10. No Escrow, Holdback, or Other Credit Support for Indemnification

**Where:** SPA Article IX generally; Final Note §7.1 (unsecured).

**The problem.** The Seller Note is the sole security for indemnification claims. It is unsecured (Final Note §7.1), subordinated to senior acquisition debt, and subject to a payment blockage while senior debt is in default (Final Note §7.2). There is no escrow, no holdback from the purchase price, and no requirement that Seller maintain minimum net worth or provide a letter of credit.

**Why it matters.** At closing, Seller receives $58 million in cash. If Seller distributes that cash to its members (it is an LLC, so this is straightforward), Bougairt's recovery for indemnification claims exceeding the note balance (or for claims where set-off is unavailable due to the issues above) depends on Seller's post-closing solvency. The subordination and payment blockage further reduce the note's value as security during the period when Bougairt is most likely to need it (while senior debt is outstanding).

**Recommended fix.** Consider proposing a $3–5 million escrow or holdback from the Closing Cash Payment, held for 18 months (aligned with the recommended survival period for product safety reps). Alternatively, require Seller to maintain a minimum tangible net worth for the term of the note, with a breach constituting an Event of Default. If Seller resists both, at minimum confirm that the senior acquisition debt is expected to be paid off well before the 30-month maturity, so the subordination and payment blockage are not a practical constraint.
