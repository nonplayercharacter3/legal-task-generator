## Issue 1: "Covered Services" Definition Conflict Affects the MAC and Service Levels

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §1.4 (defines "Covered Services" as DCP mastering, KDM generation, hard-drive duplication and shipment, *and* electronic delivery)
- Order Form §1.1 (redefines "Covered Services" as "physical DCP duplication and hard-drive shipment *only*")
- Order Form §1.3 (states the services ordered "include DCP mastering, KDM generation, hard-drive duplication and shipment, and related quality-control services")
- Order Form §3.1–3.3 (MAC measured against "Covered Services fees")
- Supply Agreement §14.3 (Order Form controls with respect to the Minimum Annual Commitment)

**Why it matters:**
The Order Form narrows "Covered Services" to physical duplication and shipment only, excluding mastering, KDM generation, and electronic delivery. Because Supply Agreement §14.3 gives the Order Form control over the MAC, the $850,000 annual commitment is measured solely against the fees for hard-drive duplication and shipment. At the rate card (Order Form §4.1: $95 per feature drive, $38–$85 per shipment), Breimourt would need roughly 5,000–9,000 drive duplications per year just to hit the MAC from that fee stream alone—before counting a single dollar of mastering, KDM, or electronic delivery fees.

In practical terms, Breimourt could spend well over $850,000 in total fees (e.g., $400,000 on mastering + $300,000 on KDMs + $300,000 on drives) and still owe a six-figure shortfall because only the drive-and-shipment fees count.

The internal inconsistency within the Order Form itself (§1.1 vs. §1.3) makes this worse: §1.3 describes the full scope of services ordered, but §1.1 redefines the term used in the MAC calculation. A court could resolve the ambiguity either way, but the plain text of §1.1 is difficult to ignore.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend Order Form §1.1 to conform to Supply Agreement §1.4, so that "Covered Services" encompasses all four service types (mastering, KDM generation, hard-drive duplication and shipment, and electronic delivery). Alternatively, if the parties intended the MAC to apply only to physical delivery, the MAC dollar amount should be recalibrated downward to reflect the narrower fee base. This should be resolved before the second Contract Year closes (February 28, 2027), when the first MAC true-up will occur.

## Issue 2: Worldwide Exclusivity with No Commercial Terms Outside the US and Canada

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §4.1 (exclusivity obligation is "throughout the world")
- Supply Agreement §1.7 ("Exclusive Services" includes "mastering, encoding and delivery services for any Content")
- Order Form §1.2 (Territory is "the United States and Canada")
- Order Form §1.3 (services ordered are for releases "in the Territory")

**Why it matters:**
Breimourt is contractually barred from using any other provider—or performing the work itself—for Exclusive Services anywhere in the world. But the Order Form, which contains all pricing, service levels, and delivery terms, covers only the US and Canada. If Breimourt releases a film in Europe, Asia, or Latin America, it must use Padisk, but there is no agreed rate, no service level, no delivery commitment, and no KDM reissue obligation for those territories.

This creates a trap: Breimourt cannot shop for a local provider in a foreign territory without breaching §4.1–4.2, yet Padisk has no contractual obligation to perform there on any defined terms. The practical effect is that Breimourt's worldwide distribution is held hostage to a supplier whose performance obligations are geographically limited.

**Recommended fix:**
Either (a) narrow the exclusivity in Supply Agreement §4.1 to the Territory defined in the Order Form (US and Canada), or (b) add a mechanism for Breimourt to open new territories by amending the Order Form on agreed terms (with a fallback that if the parties cannot agree, the exclusivity does not apply to that territory). Option (a) is simpler and more protective of Breimourt's flexibility.

## Issue 3: MAC Reduction Notice Was Untimely—Client Is Likely in the Wrong

**Where:**
- Order Form §3.4 (notice required "no later than 45 days before the first day of the second Contract Year")
- Order Form §2.1 (first Contract Year begins March 1, 2025; second begins March 1, 2026)
- Side Letter §3.1 (Customer delivered notice on February 2, 2026)
- Side Letter §3.2 (Supplier rejected as untimely)

**Why it matters:**
The second Contract Year begins March 1, 2026. Forty-five days before that date is January 15, 2026. Breimourt's notice was delivered on February 2, 2026—seventeen days late. Supplier's rejection appears correct on the plain language. The financial exposure is $212,500 (the difference between $850,000 and $637,500) for the second Contract Year.

The Side Letter records the dispute but resolves nothing. Section 3.5 routes it to Supply Agreement §15 (New York courts, with the 15-day executive escalation first). Litigating a 17-day lateness issue is unlikely to produce a favorable result and would damage the commercial relationship.

**Recommended fix:**
Advise the client that the notice was late and that Supplier's position is textually correct. Explore a commercial resolution: for example, ask Supplier to accept a partial reduction (e.g., 10–15%) as a gesture, or to apply the $212,500 delta as a credit against future invoices rather than a lump-sum shortfall payment. If no commercial resolution is reached, the client should budget for the full $212,500 shortfall at the end of the second Contract Year (February 2027). Internally, ensure the client's contract management calendar flags the non-renewal notice deadline (180 days before term expiry) and any future option exercise dates well in advance.

## Issue 4: Parent Guaranty Covers Only a Narrow Slice of Supplier's Obligations

**Where:**
- Parent Guaranty §2.1 (Guaranteed Obligations limited to: (a) service credits under Supply Agreement §6.5, (b) refunds on termination under §13.3(b), (c) indemnification under §10.1, and (d) enforcement costs)
- Supply Agreement §6.6 (re-performance obligation—not guaranteed)
- Supply Agreement §13.3(d) (transition assistance—not guaranteed)
- Supply Agreement §3.5–3.6 (KDM reissue and 24/7 operations desk—not guaranteed unless they give rise to a service credit or indemnity claim)

**Why it matters:**
The Guarantor (Padisk Holdings Ltd.) stands behind only service credits, termination refunds, indemnification payments, and enforcement costs. It does *not* guarantee:
- Supplier's obligation to re-perform defective work (§6.6);
- Supplier's obligation to provide transition assistance (§13.3(d));
- Any general damages for breach of contract that don't fit within the four categories above;
- Supplier's operational commitments (24/7 desk, KDM reissue within 2 hours).

If the subsidiary (Padisk Digital Cinema Services, Inc.) fails to perform and Breimourt's remedy is ordinary contract damages (not a service credit or an indemnification claim), the parent guaranty provides no recourse. Given that the subsidiary is a single-asset Delaware corporation, this is a meaningful credit risk.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate an expansion of the Guaranteed Obligations to include (a) all amounts payable by Supplier under the Agreement (i.e., a full payment guaranty), or at minimum (b) Supplier's re-performance and transition assistance obligations. If the parent resists, consider requiring the subsidiary to maintain a letter of credit or escrow for a defined period. At a minimum, the client should understand that its recourse against the parent is limited and should factor that into its risk assessment.

## Issue 5: No Agreed Electronic Delivery Rates for the Entire First Contract Year

**Where:**
- Order Form §4.1 (electronic delivery rate: "As separately agreed in writing by the Parties")
- Side Letter §1.1 (acknowledges the gap)
- Side Letter §2.2 (rates apply from March 1, 2026; prior deliveries invoiced at "interim rates notified by Supplier," confirmed as final)

**Why it matters:**
For the full first Contract Year (March 1, 2025 – February 28, 2026), electronic delivery was performed without any contractually agreed rate. Supplier notified "interim rates" unilaterally, and the Side Letter now confirms those invoices as final. This means:
- Breimourt had no benchmark to assess whether the interim rates were reasonable.
- The confirmation in Side Letter §2.2 waives any right to dispute those charges under Supply Agreement §5.5.
- The interim rates are not in the folder, so we cannot assess whether they were consistent with the now-agreed rates in Side Letter §2.1 or significantly higher.

**Recommended fix:**
Request from the client a copy of all interim-rate invoices for electronic delivery during the first Contract Year. Compare the amounts to the Side Letter §2.1 rates. If the interim rates were materially higher, the client should raise this with Supplier as a goodwill item (even though the Side Letter technically closes the issue). Going forward, ensure that no service is performed without a stated rate in the Order Form or a side letter before work begins.

## Issue 6: Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction Between the Supply Agreement and the Parent Guaranty

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §15.1–15.2 (New York law; exclusive jurisdiction in New York County)
- Parent Guaranty §12.1–12.2 (Ontario/Canadian law; exclusive jurisdiction in Ontario Superior Court, Toronto)

**Why it matters:**
If Breimourt needs to enforce both the Supply Agreement (against the subsidiary) and the Parent Guaranty (against the parent), it must litigate in two forums under two different legal systems. This creates:
- Increased cost (New York and Ontario counsel);
- Risk of inconsistent interpretations (e.g., what constitutes a "service credit" under NY law vs. what is a "Guaranteed Obligation" under Ontario law);
- Delay (a New York judgment establishing the subsidiary's liability may need to be recognized in Ontario before the guaranty can be enforced, or vice versa).

The "guaranty of payment, not collection" language (Parent Guaranty §2.3) and "primary obligation" language (§3.1) help by allowing Breimourt to sue the parent directly without first exhausting remedies against the subsidiary. But Breimourt still needs to establish that a Guaranteed Obligation is due, which requires reference to the Supply Agreement governed by New York law.

**Recommended fix:**
Conform the Parent Guaranty's governing law and jurisdiction to New York (matching the Supply Agreement). If the parent insists on Ontario law (likely, given it is an Ontario corporation), at minimum add a provision that the Guarantor consents to the application of New York law to the interpretation of the underlying Supply Agreement provisions that define the Guaranteed Obligations. This reduces the risk of divergent interpretations.

## Issue 7: Service Level Remedies Are Inadequate for a Major Release Failure

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §6.4 (99.5% DCP / 99.8% KDM on-time, measured monthly)
- Supply Agreement §6.5 (service credit: 5% of title fees per percentage point short; $1,500 per missed screening)
- Supply Agreement §11.1 (exclusion of indirect, consequential, and lost-profits damages)
- Supply Agreement §11.2 (liability cap: fees paid in prior 12 months)
- Supply Agreement §11.3 (carve-outs do not include service level failures)

**Why it matters:**
Consider a worst-case scenario: Breimourt's largest release of the year, booked at 2,000 screens. Supplier misses the on-time delivery threshold by 2 percentage points (97.5% on-time). That means roughly 50 screens get their DCP late. If those 50 screens miss their opening night:
- Service credit: 5% × 2 × (title fees, say $100,000) = $10,000
- Missed screening credits: 50 × $1,500 = $75,000
- Total recovery: $85,000

The actual harm to Breimourt—lost opening-weekend revenue, exhibitor relationships, marketing waste—could be in the millions. But §11.1 bars recovery of lost profits and consequential damages, and §11.2 caps total liability at the prior 12 months' fees. Service level failures are not among the §11.3 carve-outs.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate one or more of the following:
- Increase the per-missed-screening credit (e.g., to $5,000–$10,000 for wide releases);
- Add a super-cap or uncapped liability for failures that cause a missed opening on more than a defined threshold of screens (e.g., 100+ screens);
- Carve service level failures affecting a "Priority Release" (defined as a title booked at 500+ screens) out of the §11.1 indirect damages exclusion;
- Add a termination right for chronic service level failure (e.g., missing the threshold in 3 consecutive months), which would at least let Breimourt exit and recover the MAC shortfall.

## Issue 8: 7% Rate Increases on Renewal with a Fixed MAC

**Where:**
- Order Form §4.3 (all rates increase 7% at the start of each Renewal Term)
- Order Form §3.1 (MAC is a fixed $850,000 per Contract Year, including Renewal Terms)
- Side Letter §2.3 (confirms §4.3 applies to electronic delivery rates)
- Supply Agreement §2.2 (Renewal Terms are 3 years each; auto-renew unless 180-day notice)

**Why it matters:**
The MAC does not adjust for inflation or rate increases. At 7% compounding, after one Renewal Term rates are 7% higher; after two, approximately 14.5% higher. Because the MAC is fixed in dollar terms, Breimourt's $850,000 commitment buys progressively less service. Meanwhile, the auto-renewal (180-day notice) means Breimourt could inadvertently lock into a Renewal Term at higher rates.

The initial term ends February 29, 2028. The non-renewal notice deadline is approximately September 2, 2027. The client needs to be aware of this date well in advance.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add the non-renewal notice deadline (September 2, 2027) to the client's contract management calendar with a 6-month advance internal alert.
- Negotiate either (a) a corresponding increase in the MAC on renewal (so the real-value commitment stays constant), or (b) a cap on the cumulative rate increase over the life of the agreement (e.g., no more than 10% total).
- Consider negotiating a right to exit at the end of the Initial Term without penalty (i.e., no MAC shortfall for the final Contract Year if Breimourt gives non-renewal notice).

## Issue 9: Press Release Issued Without Documented Written Approval

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §16.7 ("Neither Party may issue any press release or public statement about this Agreement without the other Party's prior written approval of its content")
- Press Release (dated March 4, 2025; includes quotes from both parties' executives)

**Why it matters:**
The press release was issued three days after the Effective Date. While the inclusion of quotes from both Celia Anwander (Breimourt COO) and Gordon Halvey (Padisk CEO) strongly implies mutual consent, Supply Agreement §16.7 requires "prior written approval of its content." If no written approval (email, signed document) is in the file, there is a technical breach.

The practical risk is low—neither party is likely to enforce this against the other for a mutually beneficial announcement. However, the press release publicly describes the full scope of services (mastering, KDM, drives, electronic delivery), which could be cited as evidence of the parties' intent regarding the "Covered Services" definition (relevant to Issue 1).

**Recommended fix:**
Confirm with the client whether written approval was exchanged (email suffices under most interpretations of "written"). If so, file it. If not, the issue is likely moot given the passage of time and mutual benefit, but it should be noted. Going forward, ensure any public statement about the agreement is approved in writing (email is adequate) before release.

## Issue 10: Force Majeure Does Not Suspend Exclusivity for the First 30 Days

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §16.4 (if force majeure prevents performance for more than 30 consecutive days, Customer may obtain services from a third party)
- Supply Agreement §4.1–4.2 (exclusivity obligation)

**Why it matters:**
If a force majeure event (e.g., a major network outage, natural disaster at Supplier's facility) prevents Padisk from performing for 1–30 days, Breimourt is still bound by the exclusivity clause. It cannot engage a backup provider to protect its release dates. For a theatrical distributor, a 30-day delay on a major release can be catastrophic (missed holiday window, etc.), yet the contract provides no right to use an alternative during that period.

Additionally, §16.4 does not address who bears the cost of third-party services after day 30, or whether those services count toward the MAC.

**Recommended fix:**
Shorten the 30-day threshold to 5 or 7 days for the right to procure from a third party. Add language clarifying that (a) third-party costs during force majeure are for Supplier's account (or shared), and (b) services procured from a third party during force majeure count toward the MAC so Breimourt is not double-penalized.

## Issue 11: Transition Assistance Is Unspecified and Not Guaranteed

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §13.3(d) ("reasonable transition assistance, at its then-current rates, for up to ninety (90) days")
- Parent Guaranty §2.1 (transition assistance is not a Guaranteed Obligation)

**Why it matters:**
"Reasonable" is undefined. "Then-current rates" is ambiguous (rate card rates? negotiated rates? a markup?). Ninety days may be insufficient to re-master an entire slate, re-establish KDM relationships with exhibitors, and migrate electronic delivery infrastructure. If the relationship ends badly (e.g., termination for cause), Supplier has little incentive to provide helpful transition assistance, and Breimourt has no guaranty backstop.

**Recommended fix:**
Define "transition assistance" with specificity: e.g., Supplier shall provide (a) all DCPs, KDMs, and metadata in standard formats; (b) access to Supplier's ordering portal and exhibitor delivery lists for 90 days; (c) cooperation with a successor provider for up to 60 days; and (d) a transition fee schedule agreed in advance (not "then-current rates"). Extend the period to 120 days. If possible, add transition assistance to the Parent Guaranty.

## Issue 12: KDM Reissue and 24/7 Operations Desk Commitments Lack a Dedicated Remedy

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement §3.5 (KDM reissue within 2 hours during release weekends)
- Supply Agreement §3.6 (24/7/365 operations desk from Thursday before through Monday after each release)
- Supply Agreement §6.4–6.5 (service levels and credits)
- Supply Agreement §11.1–11.2 (indirect damages exclusion and cap)

**Why it matters:**
The 2-hour KDM reissue window and the 24/7 desk are operational commitments critical to a theatrical release. If Supplier fails to reissue a defective KDM within 2 hours and a screen goes dark, the only monetary remedy is the $1,500 missed-screening credit (if the screening is actually missed). If the KDM is reissued in 3 hours and the screening is saved but delayed, there is no service credit at all—the 99.8% KDM on-time metric (§6.4) measures delivery, not reissue speed.

The 24/7 desk commitment is similarly unenforceable in practice: if the desk is understaffed but still answers, there's no breach. If it doesn't answer and no screening is missed, the only remedy is a general damages claim subject to the cap and indirect damages exclusion.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a specific service level for KDM reissue (e.g., "100% of defective KDMs reissued within 2 hours during release weekends") with a dedicated credit if missed. Add a service level for operations desk responsiveness (e.g., "answer within 15 minutes, 24/7 during release windows"). These would plug the gap between the operational commitments in §3.5–3.6 and the measurement framework in §6.
