## Summary

I have reviewed all five documents in the matter folder. The package consists of a Supply Agreement, an Order Form, a Parent Guaranty from Padisk Holdings Ltd., a Side Letter (February 2026) addressing electronic delivery rates and a disputed MAC reduction, and a joint press release (March 2025). The documents contain several material conflicts and gaps that create significant risk for Breimourt. The most pressing issues are: (1) a worldwide exclusivity obligation that is not limited to the US/Canada Territory stated in the Order Form; (2) a live dispute over the second-year Minimum Annual Commitment in which Supplier's rejection appears correct on the face of the Order Form; and (3) a direct contradiction in the definition of "Covered Services" that creates uncertainty about what counts toward the MAC and what services are actually contracted.

## Issues (Ranked by Significance)

### 1. Worldwide Exclusivity Obligation Not Conformed to Territory

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 4.1: "Customer shall, and shall cause its Affiliates to, obtain all of their requirements for Exclusive Services **throughout the world** solely from Supplier."
- Supply Agreement § 1.7: "Exclusive Services" means "mastering, encoding and delivery services for any Content, including the Covered Services."
- Order Form § 1.2: "The Territory is the United States and Canada."
- Supply Agreement § 14.3 (Order of Precedence): The Supply Agreement prevails over the Order Form except for "pricing, the Initial Term and the Minimum Annual Commitment." Territory and Exclusivity are not in that carve-out.

**Why it matters:**
On the face of the documents, Breimourt is contractually locked into using Padisk as its sole worldwide provider of "mastering, encoding and delivery services for any Content." This is an extraordinarily broad restriction for an independent distributor. It would prevent Breimourt from using any other vendor for DCP mastering, encoding, or delivery for any content it distributes or licenses anywhere in the world, even though the commercial deal (pricing, MAC, service levels) is structured around US/Canada theatrical releases only. The "Affiliates" language in § 4.1 extends the restriction to all entities under common control. If Breimourt has any international distribution activity, streaming deliverables, or airline/home-entertainment content (all of which fall within the definition of "Content" in § 1.5), it cannot source those services from anyone else.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate an amendment to § 4.1 to limit exclusivity to the Territory (US and Canada) and to theatrical release services only. Alternatively, if the commercial intent was worldwide exclusivity, confirm that the pricing, MAC, and service levels are structured to reflect that broader scope, and consider whether the MAC is adequate for worldwide volume. At minimum, add a carve-out for non-theatrical deliverables (streaming, airline, home entertainment) and for Affiliates operating outside the Territory.

### 2. MAC Reduction Notice Was Late – Supplier's Rejection Appears Correct

**Where:**
- Order Form § 3.4: Notice must be "delivered no later than 45 days before the first day of the second Contract Year."
- Order Form § 2.1: First Contract Year begins March 1, 2025. Second Contract Year therefore begins March 1, 2026.
- 45 days before March 1, 2026 = **January 15, 2026**.
- Side Letter § 3.1: Customer delivered the Reduction Notice on **February 2, 2026** (27 days before the deadline).
- Side Letter § 3.2: Supplier rejected the notice as untimely on February 9, 2026.
- Side Letter § 3.3–3.4: Customer disputes; both parties reserve rights.

**Why it matters:**
The arithmetic is straightforward: February 2 is not 45 days before March 1, 2026. Supplier's rejection is correct on the face of the Order Form. If the dispute is resolved against Breimourt, the second-year MAC remains $850,000 rather than the intended $637,500 – an exposure of **$212,500** for that Contract Year alone. The Side Letter preserves the dispute but does not resolve it, and § 3.5 routes it to Supply Agreement § 15 (New York courts, jury waiver, escalation). Breimourt should understand that its position is weak unless it can argue that the 45-day period should be measured differently (e.g., from the Order Form Effective Date rather than the start of the second Contract Year) or that Supplier waived the deadline by some conduct – neither of which is supported by the documents in the folder.

**Recommended fix:**
Assess whether to settle the dispute (e.g., agree to the $850,000 MAC in exchange for a concession elsewhere) or to litigate. If Breimourt believes it has a viable argument, it should be developed promptly because the second Contract Year is already in progress (began March 1, 2026) and the shortfall will be invoiced within 30 days after year-end (Order Form § 3.2). In any event, internal processes should be reviewed to ensure future time-sensitive notices are delivered well in advance of deadlines.

### 3. Contradictory Definitions of "Covered Services"

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 1.4: "Covered Services" means "DCP mastering, KDM generation, hard-drive duplication and shipment, **and electronic delivery to exhibitors**."
- Order Form § 1.1: "As used in this Order Form, Covered Services means **physical DCP duplication and hard-drive shipment only**."
- Order Form § 1.3: "Supplier shall provide theatrical DCP services … These include **DCP mastering, KDM generation, hard-drive duplication and shipment**, and related quality-control services." (Note: this section of the same Order Form lists mastering and KDM generation, contradicting § 1.1.)
- Supply Agreement § 14.3: The Supply Agreement prevails over the Order Form except for pricing, Initial Term, and MAC.

**Why it matters:**
There are three different statements of what "Covered Services" means across two documents, and one of them (Order Form § 1.1) directly contradicts the other two. The practical consequences are significant:

- **MAC calculation (Order Form § 3.3):** "Covered Services fees count toward the Minimum Annual Commitment." If the Supply Agreement's broader definition governs, then mastering, KDM, and electronic delivery fees all count toward the $850,000. If the Order Form's narrow definition governs, only duplication and shipment fees count – meaning Breimourt could easily fall short of the MAC even if it spends well over $850,000 total, because mastering and KDM fees (which are substantial at $2,400–$3,600 per version and $6.50 per KDM) would not count.
- **Scope of Supplier's obligations:** The narrow definition could be read to mean Supplier is only contractually obligated to perform duplication and shipment, with mastering and KDM generation being extra-contractual.
- **Service levels (Order Form § 1.4):** The Order Form says Supply Agreement § 6 service levels apply to "all services ordered under this Order Form." If "Covered Services" is narrow, it's unclear whether service levels apply to mastering or KDM generation.

Under the order of precedence, the Supply Agreement's definition likely prevails (because "Covered Services" is not pricing, Initial Term, or MAC). But the internal contradiction within the Order Form itself ( §§ 1.1 vs. 1.3) creates interpretive risk that a court might resolve against the drafter.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend Order Form § 1.1 to conform to Supply Agreement § 1.4, or delete it and rely on the Supply Agreement definition. Clarify in Order Form § 3.3 exactly which fee categories count toward the MAC. This should be done before the end of the current Contract Year to avoid a disputed shortfall calculation.

### 4. Parent Guaranty Does Not Cover Supplier's Performance Obligations

**Where:**
- Parent Guaranty § 2.1: "Guaranteed Obligations" are limited to: (a) service credits (§ 6.5); (b) refunds on termination (§ 13.3(b)); (c) indemnification payments (§ 10.1); and (d) enforcement costs.
- Supply Agreement § 6.6: Supplier must re-perform non-conforming services at no charge.
- Supply Agreement § 11.2: Liability cap (fees paid in prior 12 months).

**Why it matters:**
The Guaranty is a backstop for specific monetary obligations only. It does not guarantee:
- Supplier's obligation to actually perform the services (deliver DCPs, generate KDMs, etc.);
- Supplier's obligation to re-perform defective work (§ 6.6);
- Any damages Breimourt might recover for Supplier's breach beyond service credits (e.g., if a missed delivery causes Breimourt to lose an exhibitor booking, and the $1,500-per-missed-screening credit in § 6.5 is inadequate);
- Supplier's transition assistance obligation (§ 13.3(d)).

If Padisk Digital Cinema Services, Inc. (the operating subsidiary) becomes insolvent or simply fails to perform, Breimourt's recourse against Padisk Holdings Ltd. is limited to the narrow categories in § 2.1. The Guaranty is useful for service credits and indemnification, but it is not a performance bond.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate an expansion of the Guaranteed Obligations to include (i) Supplier's re-performance obligations, (ii) any damages awarded in a judgment or settlement for Supplier's breach, and (iii) transition assistance costs. Alternatively, if the commercial risk is acceptable given the insurance requirements in Supply Agreement § 16.5 ($5M per occurrence), document that decision.

### 5. Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction (New York vs. Ontario)

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 15.1–15.2: New York law; exclusive jurisdiction in New York County state and federal courts.
- Parent Guaranty § 12.1–12.2: Ontario law; exclusive jurisdiction in Ontario Superior Court of Justice, Toronto.
- Side Letter § 4.4: Incorporates Supply Agreement §§ 15.1–15.2 (New York).

**Why it matters:**
If Breimourt needs to enforce the Guaranty (e.g., for service credits or indemnification), it must litigate in Ontario under Ontario law, while any claim against Supplier for breach of the Supply Agreement must be litigated in New York under New York law. This creates:
- Practical difficulty in coordinating parallel proceedings;
- Risk of inconsistent outcomes (e.g., a New York court finds Supplier liable for indemnification, but an Ontario court applies a different standard to the Guaranty);
- Increased cost (two sets of counsel, two jurisdictions);
- The Guaranty's 15-business-day payment window (§ 4.2) may be harder to enforce if a dispute requires Ontario litigation first.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate a conforming amendment to Parent Guaranty § 12 to align with New York law and jurisdiction, or at minimum add a provision allowing the Beneficiary to bring a Guaranty claim in the same proceeding as the underlying Supply Agreement claim. If the Guarantor resists, consider whether the Guaranty's value justifies the enforcement friction.

### 6. Liability Cap May Be Inadequate Early in the Term

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 11.2: Cap is "total fees paid and payable under this Agreement in the twelve (12) months **before** the event giving rise to the claim."
- Supply Agreement § 11.3: Exceptions (no cap) for indemnification, breach of § 7 or § 12, gross negligence/fraud/willful misconduct, and Customer's payment obligations.

**Why it matters:**
If a claim arises in the first 12 months of the term (i.e., before March 1, 2026), there are no prior fees to measure the cap against. The cap could be zero or nominal. Even in later years, if Breimourt's actual spend is well below the $850,000 MAC (which is possible given the Covered Services definition ambiguity in Issue 3), the cap will be low. The exceptions in § 11.3 help for security breaches and indemnification, but a simple failure to perform (e.g., chronic late deliveries that don't rise to gross negligence) would be subject to the cap.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate a minimum cap floor (e.g., "in no event less than the Minimum Annual Commitment for the applicable Contract Year") or a fixed-dollar cap that is meaningful relative to Breimourt's exposure. Alternatively, add Supplier's service level obligations and re-performance obligations to the § 11.3 exceptions.

### 7. No Evidence of Facilities Disclosure in the Folder

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 7.2: "Supplier shall perform the Covered Services **only at facilities that it has disclosed to Customer in writing**."
- Press Release § 4.2: Padisk's CEO references "our teams in **Los Angeles and Toronto**."

**Why it matters:**
Section 7.2 is a content security safeguard: it limits where Breimourt's unencrypted content can be handled. If Supplier has not provided written disclosure of its facilities, then either (a) no facilities have been disclosed and Supplier is in breach of § 7.2 by performing any service, or (b) disclosure was made informally (e.g., in the press release) but not in the required written form. The press release reference to Toronto is notable because Padisk Holdings Ltd. is an Ontario corporation, but the operating entity (Padisk Digital Cinema Services, Inc.) is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in Los Angeles. If content is being handled in Toronto, that facility must be disclosed in writing.

**Recommended fix:**
Request from Supplier a written list of all facilities at which Covered Services are or will be performed, as required by § 7.2. Confirm whether any content has been or will be handled in Toronto. If so, ensure the Parent Guaranty and any data protection obligations account for cross-border transfer.

### 8. Press Release Announced Electronic Delivery Before Rates Were Contracted

**Where:**
- Press Release § 3.4 (dated March 4, 2025): Announces "Electronic delivery. Delivery of DCPs to exhibitors through satellite and terrestrial networks, with confirmation of successful ingest at each location."
- Order Form § 4.1 (effective March 1, 2025): Electronic delivery rate is "As separately agreed in writing by the Parties."
- Side Letter § 2.2 (dated February 20, 2026): "Electronic deliveries completed before March 1, 2026 have been invoiced at the interim rates notified by Supplier, and the Parties confirm those invoices as final."

**Why it matters:**
For nearly 11 months (March 2025 – February 2026), electronic delivery was being performed and invoiced at "interim rates" that were never formally agreed in a contract document. The Side Letter now confirms those invoices as final, which means Breimourt has accepted payment at rates it cannot verify were fair or reasonable. The press release created a public commitment to the service that outpaced the contractual framework.

**Recommended fix:**
Review the interim invoices for electronic delivery from March 2025 through February 2026. Compare the interim rates to the rates now set in Side Letter § 2.1. If the interim rates were higher, the "confirmation as final" in Side Letter § 2.2 may have cost Breimourt money. This is likely too late to remedy (the Side Letter is signed), but it should be noted in the file and considered in any future negotiation leverage.

### 9. Auto-Renewal with 180-Day Notice Period and 7% Rate Escalation

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 2.2: Auto-renews for 3-year terms unless notice given 180 days before expiry.
- Order Form § 4.3: All rates increase by 7% at the start of each Renewal Term.
- Order Form § 2.1: Initial Term ends February 29, 2028.

**Why it matters:**
The first renewal decision point is **September 3, 2027** (180 days before February 29, 2028). If Breimourt misses that date, it is locked in for another 3 years at 7% higher rates. Combined with the worldwide exclusivity (Issue 1), a missed renewal notice could trap Breimourt in an expensive, broad exclusivity arrangement for an additional 3 years. The 7% increase compounds: after two renewal terms, rates would be ~14.5% above the Initial Term.

**Recommended fix:**
Calendar the 180-day non-renewal notice deadline (September 3, 2027) immediately. Assign internal ownership. Consider whether the 7% escalation is market-rate and whether to negotiate a cap or CPI-linked adjustment in any renewal.

### 10. Force Majeure Third-Party Right Only Available After 30 Consecutive Days

**Where:**
- Supply Agreement § 16.4: "If a force majeure event prevents Supplier from performing for more than thirty (30) consecutive days, Customer may obtain the affected services from a third party for the duration of the event."

**Why it matters:**
For the first 30 days of a force majeure event, Breimourt is stuck: it cannot terminate (no termination right for force majeure in § 13), it cannot source from a third party (the § 16.4 right hasn't triggered), and the exclusivity in § 4.1 arguably still applies. For a theatrical distributor, 30 days without delivery capability could mean missed release windows and exhibitor penalties.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate a shorter trigger (e.g., 5 or 7 consecutive days) for the right to source from a third party, or add a termination right if force majeure prevents performance for more than a stated period. Also clarify whether the exclusivity obligation is suspended during a force majeure event.

### 11. Order Form Internal Contact Fields Are Incomplete

**Where:**
- Order Form, header table: "Customer Operations Contact" is listed as "VP, Theatrical Distribution Operations" (title only, no name or email). "Supplier Account Representative" is listed as "Designated by Supplier under Supply Agreement Section 3.6" (no actual name).

**Why it matters:**
Section 3.6 of the Supply Agreement requires Supplier to assign a dedicated account representative. The Order Form does not identify who that person is. For day-to-day operations (work orders, release weekend coordination, KDM reissues within 2 hours), having a named contact is critical. The absence of a named Customer Operations Contact also creates ambiguity about who at Breimourt has authority to submit work orders or receive notices.

**Recommended fix:**
Complete the Order Form contact fields with named individuals and direct contact information. Confirm in writing who Supplier's designated account representative is.
