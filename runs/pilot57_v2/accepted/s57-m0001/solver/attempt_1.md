## I. URGENT ISSUES

### Issue 1: Orlund Change-of-Control Consent Is Not a Closing Condition

**Documents:** SPA Schedule 7.2(d); Disclosure Schedule § 2.1; LOI § 7(c)

**Description.** Disclosure Schedule § 2.1 discloses that Section 14.3 of the Exclusive License Agreement with Orlund BioSciences GmbH prohibits a change of control (defined as sale of more than 50% of voting securities) without Orlund's prior written consent, and that Orlund may terminate the license if a change of control occurs without consent. The SPA defines "Required Consents" (SPA § 1.1) as those "listed on Schedule 7.2(d)." Schedule 7.2(d) lists only four state boards of pharmacy (NJ, CA, TX, FL). Orlund's consent is not included.

The LOI § 7(c) contemplated that closing would be conditioned on "receipt of required governmental approvals and **third-party consents** to be identified in the Definitive Agreement." The SPA narrowed this to governmental approvals only.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** Velnarix is the Company's lead product and the principal asset justifying the $186 million purchase price. The Company does not own the underlying patent rights; it holds an exclusive license from Orlund. If Orlund withholds consent or terminates the license post-closing, Peitort loses the right to develop, manufacture, and commercialize Velnarix in the U.S. and Canada. Because Orlund's consent is not a Required Consent, Peitort has no contractual right to walk away from the deal if Orlund refuses. Peitort would close, pay the full purchase price, and bear the loss with no indemnification trigger (the rep in § 4.4 is qualified by the Disclosure Schedule, so the Orlund consent requirement is a disclosed exception, not a breach).

**Recommended Fix.** Add Orlund's written consent to the Exclusive License Agreement as a fifth item on Schedule 7.2(d), making it a Required Consent under SPA § 7.2(d). Alternatively, add a specific closing condition in § 7.2 requiring that Orlund have delivered its consent and that the Exclusive License Agreement remain in full force and effect. The notice to Orlund was delivered August 27, 2026; Peitort should confirm the status of Orlund's response before closing.

**Urgency:** **CRITICAL.** This should be raised with opposing counsel immediately. If Orlund has not yet consented, closing without that consent would be a severe error.

### Issue 2: Seller Side Letter Deletes Regulatory Compliance Carve-Out from the General Cap

**Documents:** Seller Side Letter Draft § 2.2; SPA § 9.6(c); LOI § 5(d)

**Description.** SPA § 9.6(c) as drafted provides that the General Cap ($18.6 million) "shall not apply to Losses arising from Fraud or any breach of a Fundamental Representation **or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)**, and the aggregate liability of Seller for those Losses shall not exceed the Purchase Price." The Seller Side Letter Draft § 2.2 amends § 9.6(c) by deleting the words "or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)."

The LOI § 5(d) expressly contemplated that the General Cap would not apply to "breaches of the regulatory compliance representation."

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** The Company is an FDA-regulated manufacturer of sterile injectables. The Disclosure Schedule already reveals a 2023 FDA Form 483 (VAI classification) and a voluntary market withdrawal of Lorvexa. If a post-closing FDA enforcement action, warning letter, or loss of manufacturing registration occurs that traces to a pre-closing condition, Peitort's recovery would be capped at $18.6 million instead of the full $186 million purchase price. For a company whose entire value proposition depends on regulatory compliance, this is a material reduction in protection.

**Recommended Fix.** Reject the side letter amendment. If Seller is pressing for this change, consider a compromise: retain the regulatory compliance carve-out but reduce the uncapped exposure to a middle figure (e.g., 50% of the Purchase Price = $93 million), or extend the survival period for § 4.12 to match the Fundamental Reps (six years). Do not sign the side letter as drafted.

**Urgency:** **HIGH.** The side letter is in draft form and not yet executed. Peitort should not agree to this deletion.

### Issue 3: Seller Note Set-Off Right Is Limited to Product Liability Claims Only

**Documents:** Seller Note § 4.1; SPA § 9.9; SPA § 11.6; LOI § 3(f)

**Description.** SPA § 9.9 provides: "Buyer may set off **any amount** to which any Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled under this Article IX against any amount otherwise payable by Buyer under the Seller Note, subject to the terms of the Seller Note." SPA § 11.6 then provides that in the event of conflict, "the Seller Note shall govern with respect to its payment, set-off and dispute resolution terms."

Seller Note § 4.1 narrows the set-off right to "Losses arising from **Product Liability Claims** relating to Company Products" only. This means Peitort cannot set off against the Note:

- Tax indemnity claims (§ 9.2(c))
- Regulatory compliance claims (§ 9.2(a) for breach of § 4.12)
- General representation and warranty claims (§ 9.2(a))
- Covenant breach claims (§ 9.2(b))
- Seller Guarantee payment claims (§ 9.3(c) – though this runs in Seller's favor)

The LOI § 3(f) contemplated a broad set-off: "Buyer would have a right of set-off against amounts payable under the Seller Note for amounts owed to Buyer under **the indemnification provisions** of the Definitive Agreement."

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** The Seller Note is $24 million, payable in a single bullet at 36 months with quarterly interest. If a material indemnification claim arises (e.g., a regulatory breach or tax liability) before the Note matures, Peitort must continue making interest payments to Seller while separately pursuing its indemnification claim. Peitort loses the leverage of withholding note payments. In a worst-case scenario where Seller becomes judgment-proof or dissolves after closing, the set-off right is Peitort's primary self-help remedy against the Note balance.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend Seller Note § 4.1 to track SPA § 9.9: "The Maker may set off against any amount of principal or interest otherwise payable under this Note the amount of any Losses to which any Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement." If Seller resists, accept a compromise that at minimum includes set-off for (a) Product Liability Claims, (b) Tax indemnity claims, and (c) Regulatory Compliance claims.

**Urgency:** **HIGH.** The Note is in form (Exhibit B) and has not been executed. This must be negotiated before signing.

### Issue 4: Tax Indemnity Survival Period Is Only 12 Months

**Documents:** SPA § 9.2(c); SPA § 9.1; SPA § 4.11

**Description.** SPA § 9.2(c) provides that Seller indemnifies Buyer for "any Taxes of the Company for any Pre-Closing Tax Period," but adds: "provided that claims under this Section 9.2(c) may be made only during the survival period applicable to the representations and warranties in Section 4.11." Section 4.11 (Taxes) is a general representation, not a Fundamental Representation. Under § 9.1, general representations survive for 12 months after the Closing Date.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** The federal statute of limitations on tax assessments is generally three years (26 U.S.C. § 6501(a)). State limitations periods are similar. A 12-month survival period means Peitort could be exposed to pre-closing tax liabilities (e.g., unfiled returns, transfer pricing adjustments, sales tax on out-of-state distributions) that are discovered or assessed after 12 months, with no indemnification recourse against Seller. For a specialty pharma company with multi-state operations (NJ, CA, TX, FL per the Required Consents), the tax exposure is non-trivial.

**Recommended Fix.** Amend § 9.2(c) to provide that the tax indemnity survives until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (including extensions) for the relevant tax period, or at minimum for three to four years post-closing. This is a standard market term for tax-specific indemnities.

**Urgency:** **HIGH.** Should be raised in the next round of SPA negotiations or as a condition to signing the side letter.

## II. OTHER ISSUES

### Issue 5: Key Employee Agreement – Ambiguous RSU Grant Language

**Document:** Sallow Key Employee Agreement § 3.3

**Description.** Section 3.3 states: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort's compensation committee **will be recommended to grant** the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000."

The passive construction "will be recommended to grant" is unclear. It does not state who makes the recommendation, whether the recommendation is binding, or whether the compensation committee is obligated to follow it. If the committee declines or delays, it is unclear whether Peitort is in breach.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** Dr. Sallow's continued employment is a closing condition (SPA § 7.2(e)). If a dispute arises over the RSU grant within the first 60 days post-closing, it could undermine the retention purpose of the agreement and potentially trigger a Good Reason claim under § 5.4(d) (material breach of the agreement).

**Recommended Fix.** Rewrite § 3.3 as: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort shall grant the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000, made under and subject to the terms of Peitort's equity incentive plan and a form of award agreement reasonably acceptable to the parties." This makes the grant obligatory on Peitort rather than subject to a committee recommendation.

**Urgency:** Moderate. Should be corrected before the Key Employee Agreement is finalized for closing.

### Issue 6: Split Governing Law Between SPA and Seller Note

**Documents:** SPA § 11.10 (New York); Seller Note § 10.1 (Delaware); SPA § 11.6

**Description.** The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The Seller Note is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Superior Court. SPA § 11.6 confirms that the Note governs its own "dispute resolution terms."

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** If a dispute arises that implicates both the SPA indemnification provisions and the Note (e.g., a set-off dispute), Peitort could face the need to litigate in two jurisdictions under two bodies of law. While § 11.6 allocates Note-specific disputes to the Note's forum, the boundary between a "set-off" dispute and an "indemnification" dispute may be unclear, creating forum and choice-of-law complications.

**Recommended Fix.** Consider conforming the Note's governing law and jurisdiction to New York to match the SPA. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps for consistency with its own organizational documents), add a provision to the Note stating that any dispute arising from the interaction between the Note's set-off provisions and the SPA's indemnification provisions shall be resolved in the SPA's forum (New York) under the SPA's governing law.

**Urgency:** Low to moderate. Negotiate if convenient; not a deal-breaker.

### Issue 7: Orlund Milestone Payment – Imminent Contingent Liability

**Document:** Disclosure Schedule § 4.2

**Description.** The Company has a contingent obligation to pay Orlund $2,500,000 under § 6.4 of the Exclusive License Agreement when cumulative U.S. net sales of Velnarix first reach $250,000,000. Cumulative net sales were approximately $198,400,000 as of June 30, 2026. The remaining gap is approximately $51.6 million.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** Depending on the sales trajectory, this milestone could be triggered within 6–18 months post-closing. It is a real, quantified liability that will reduce post-closing cash flow. It is properly disclosed, so it does not give rise to an indemnification claim, but Peitort should ensure it is reflected in its post-closing financial planning and that the working capital target ($14.2 million) is calculated consistently with respect to this accrual.

**Recommended Fix.** No contractual fix required (it is disclosed). However, confirm with the Company's finance team the projected timing of the milestone trigger and ensure the closing working capital calculation does not inappropriately exclude or include the accrual. Consider whether the milestone should be treated as an indebtedness for working capital purposes (it should not be, as it is not yet due, but the treatment should be agreed).

**Urgency:** Low. Planning item.

### Issue 8: Retention Bonuses – $575,000 Payable at Closing

**Document:** Disclosure Schedule § 11.5

**Description.** Seven employees received retention bonus letters dated August 1, 2026. The aggregate amount is $1,150,000, of which 50% ($575,000) is payable at the Closing and 50% is payable six months after the Closing, in each case subject to continued employment.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** The $575,000 payable at Closing should be reflected as a liability in the closing working capital calculation. If it is not properly accrued on the Company's books as of the Closing Date, the working capital true-up could be affected. Additionally, the second 50% ($575,000) is a post-closing cash outflow that Peitort should model.

**Recommended Fix.** Confirm with the Company's finance team that the at-closing retention bonus is accrued as a liability on the closing balance sheet. If it is not, it will reduce net working capital dollar-for-dollar, increasing the cash payment to Seller under § 2.3(a). Ensure the working capital definition in the SPA (or the parties' agreed calculation methodology) treats this correctly.

**Urgency:** Moderate. Address in the working capital estimate review (due 3 Business Days before Closing per § 2.3(a)).

### Issue 9: Mutual Confidentiality Agreement – One-Way Non-Solicitation

**Document:** MCA § 5.1

**Description.** The MCA imposes an 18-month non-solicitation restriction on Peitort (only) with respect to Company officers and director-level employees. The restriction runs from the Effective Date (May 4, 2026) and therefore expires on November 4, 2027. There is no reciprocal restriction on Bratousk.

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** While post-closing, Peitort owns the Company and its employees are Peitort's employees, so the restriction is largely moot for existing employees. However, if Peitort wishes to hire any Company employee into a role outside the Company (e.g., into Peitort's corporate functions) before November 4, 2027, the MCA could technically restrict that. The carve-out in § 5.2(c) for "any employment arrangement entered into with the Company's consent or in connection with a definitive agreement" should cover most post-closing scenarios, but it is worth noting.

**Recommended Fix.** No action required. The restriction is standard and the carve-outs are adequate. Be aware of the November 4, 2027 expiration if planning any internal transfers.

**Urgency:** Low.

### Issue 12: Seller Note – "Company Products" Defined by Exhibit A Excludes Lorvexa

**Documents:** Seller Note § 1.1 ("Company Products" definition); Seller Note Exhibit A; SPA § 1.1 ("Company Products" definition)

**Description.** The SPA defines "Company Products" broadly: "all products manufactured, sold or distributed by or on behalf of the Company at any time prior to the Closing." This includes Lorvexa (discontinued November 2024 – February 2025).

The Seller Note defines "Company Products" as "the products listed on Exhibit A to this Note." Exhibit A lists only Velnarix and Ostrevan. Lorvexa is not listed.

The set-off right in Note § 4.1 is limited to "Losses arising from Product Liability Claims relating to Company Products." Because the Note's definition of "Company Products" excludes Lorvexa, the set-off right does not extend to the pending and threatened Lorvexa product liability claims (Disclosure Schedule §§ 10.2–10.3).

**Why It Matters for Peitort.** The Lorvexa claims are the most active product liability exposure (three pending cases, two threatened claims). If Peitort were to rely on the Note's set-off right for these claims, it would fail because Lorvexa is not a "Company Product" under the Note. Peitort would need to pursue the indemnification claim under SPA § 9.2(d) separately, without the ability to offset against Note payments.

**Recommended Fix.** Add Lorvexa to Exhibit A of the Seller Note, or amend the Note's definition of "Company Products" to track the SPA definition ("all products manufactured, sold or distributed by or on behalf of the Company at any time prior to the Closing"). This is critical if Issue 3 (broadening the set-off) is resolved in Peitort's favor.

**Urgency:** **HIGH** (in conjunction with Issue 3). If the set-off is broadened to cover all Article IX claims, the "Company Products" definition in the Note must still be corrected to include Lorvexa for the product liability set-off to work as intended.
