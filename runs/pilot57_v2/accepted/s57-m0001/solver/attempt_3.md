## URGENT ISSUES

### 1. Orlund Consent Not a Required Consent to Closing

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule ¶ 2.1; SPA Schedule 7.2(d); SPA § 7.2(d)

**Issue.** The Exclusive License Agreement with Orlund BioSciences GmbH (the license covering Velnarix, the Company's lead product) prohibits a change of control without Orlund's prior written consent. The Disclosure Schedule (¶ 2.1) confirms that the transaction "will constitute a change of control" and that the Company delivered notice to Orlund on August 27, 2026. However, no consent from Orlund has been received, and Orlund's consent is **not listed on Schedule 7.2(d)** (Required Consents), which lists only four state board of pharmacy approvals.

Because SPA § 7.2(d) conditions Closing only on "each Required Consent" being obtained, Peitort could be obligated to close even if Orlund withholds or refuses consent. If Orlund then terminates the Exclusive License Agreement under § 14.3 for the unauthorized change of control, Peitort would lose the Velnarix license—the core asset of the acquisition.

**Why it matters.** Velnarix is the Company's primary revenue driver. Loss of the license would destroy substantially all of the $186 million purchase price value.

**Recommended fix.** Add Orlund's written consent to Schedule 7.2(d) as a Required Consent, or add a new condition in § 7.2 (e.g., § 7.2(g)) that requires Orlund's consent to be in full force and effect as a condition to Buyer's Closing obligation. Alternatively, require delivery of Orlund's consent as a Seller Closing deliverable under § 3.2. Given that the SPA is already signed, this will require an amendment under § 11.8.

**Urgency.** The parties are targeting the week of December 7 for Closing (closing logistics email, Celia Marchetti, Nov. 17). If Orlund has not yet responded to the August 27 notice, there is a risk of closing without consent. Confirm immediately whether Orlund has granted or is expected to grant consent.

### 2. Seller Note Set-Off Excludes Lorvexa Product Liability Claims

**Documents:** Seller Note § 1.1 (definition of "Company Products") and Exhibit A; SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Company Products"); SPA § 9.9; SPA § 11.6; Disclosure Schedule ¶¶ 10.2–10.3

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Company Products" broadly as "all products manufactured, sold or distributed by or on behalf of the Company at any time prior to the Closing" (§ 1.1). Under that definition, Lorvexa is a Company Product. The SPA's set-off provision (§ 9.9) allows Buyer to set off "any amount to which any Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled under this Article IX" against the Seller Note.

However, the Seller Note defines "Company Products" narrowly as "the products listed on Exhibit A to this Note" (§ 1.1), and Exhibit A lists **only Velnarix and Ostrevan**—Lorvexa is omitted. The Seller Note's set-off (§ 4.1) is limited to "Losses arising from Product Liability Claims relating to Company Products." Because SPA § 11.6 provides that "the Seller Note shall govern with respect to its payment, set-off and dispute resolution terms," the Note's narrower definition controls.

All three pending product liability claims and both threatened claims disclosed on Schedule 4.15 relate to **Lorvexa** (a discontinued product withdrawn from the market in early 2025). Peitort therefore **cannot set off any Lorvexa-related losses against the $24 million Seller Note**, even though those are the actual claims creating exposure.

**Why it matters.** The Lorvexa claims are the Company's principal product liability exposure. Without set-off, Peitort must (a) pay the full Seller Note installments to Bratousk and (b) separately pursue an indemnification claim under SPA § 9.2(d) against Bratousk, creating credit risk if Bratousk is unwilling or unable to pay. The LOI (§ 3(f)) contemplated a broader set-off right: "Buyer would have a right of set-off against amounts payable under the Seller Note for amounts owed to Buyer under the indemnification provisions of the Definitive Agreement."

**Recommended fix.** Amend Exhibit A to the Seller Note to include Lorvexa (or to use the SPA's broader definition of "Company Products"). Alternatively, amend Seller Note § 4.1 to permit set-off for any Losses under Article IX of the SPA, not just Product Liability Claims relating to the listed products. This should be addressed before the Note is executed at Closing.

**Urgency.** The Note has not yet been executed (it is a Closing deliverable under SPA § 3.3(b)). This must be fixed before signing the execution version.

### 3. Seller Side Letter Deletes Regulatory Compliance Carve-Out from Indemnification Cap

**Documents:** Seller Side Letter § 2.2; SPA § 9.6(c); LOI § 5(d)

**Issue.** SPA § 9.6(c) as signed provides that the General Cap ($18.6 million) does not apply to Losses arising from "Fraud or any breach of a Fundamental Representation **or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)**," with liability for those matters capped at the full Purchase Price ($186 million). The LOI (§ 5(d)) expressly contemplated this carve-out.

The Seller's side letter (§ 2.2) amends § 9.6(c) by **deleting "or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)."** If Peitort signs the side letter, breaches of the regulatory compliance representation (FDA/CGMP compliance, permits, Form 483s, etc.) would be subject to the $18.6 million General Cap rather than the $186 million Purchase Price cap.

**Why it matters.** For a specialty pharmaceutical manufacturer, regulatory risk is among the most significant post-closing exposures. The Company already has a history of FDA observations (Form 483, May 2023, VAI classification—Disclosure Schedule ¶ 8.1) and a voluntary market withdrawal (Lorvexa—¶ 8.2). A future FDA enforcement action, warning letter, or consent decree could generate losses far exceeding $18.6 million. Capping Peitort's recovery at $18.6 million for regulatory breaches would leave it bearing the majority of that risk.

**Recommended fix.** Do not sign the side letter as drafted. If Seller insists on the deletion, negotiate a middle ground: (a) retain the carve-out for FDA warning letters, consent decrees, or injunctions (i.e., the most severe regulatory outcomes), while accepting the General Cap for lesser regulatory matters; or (b) increase the General Cap specifically for § 4.12 breaches to a level between $18.6 million and $186 million (e.g., 25% of the Purchase Price).

**Urgency.** The side letter is undated and unsigned. Peitort should not execute it without resolving this point.

## SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 4. Seller Note § 6.2 Contains a Reference to a Non-Existent "Clause (ii)"

**Documents:** Seller Note § 6.2

**Issue.** Section 6.2 (Permitted Payments) states: "A payment block under clause (ii) above shall last no more than 179 days in any 360-day period." There is no "clause (ii)" in § 6.2 or anywhere else in the Note. The section contains only a single unnumbered paragraph. This appears to be a drafting error, likely a remnant of a prior version that had multiple sub-clauses.

**Why it matters.** The payment block provision is critical to Peitort as Maker: it determines when Senior Lenders can block interest and principal payments on the Note. Ambiguity in this provision could lead to disputes about the maximum duration of a payment block, potentially allowing Senior Lenders to block payments indefinitely (if the 179-day limit is read as inapplicable due to the broken cross-reference) or creating uncertainty about whether the limit applies at all.

**Recommended fix.** Delete "under clause (ii) above" so the sentence reads: "A payment block shall last no more than 179 days in any 360-day period." Confirm with Seller's counsel that this was the intended meaning.

### 5. Key Employee Agreement § 3.3 – Ambiguous Equity Grant Obligation

**Documents:** Sallow Key Employee Agreement § 3.3

**Issue.** The provision states: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort's compensation committee **will be recommended to grant** the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000." The phrase "will be recommended to grant" is grammatically incoherent and creates uncertainty about whether the grant is mandatory or merely advisory. Read literally, it could mean the committee "will be recommended" (i.e., someone will recommend to the committee) to grant the RSUs, leaving the actual grant to the committee's discretion.

**Why it matters.** SPA § 7.2(e) makes the Key Employee Agreement a condition to Closing. Dr. Sallow is the Chief Scientific Officer and is critical to the Velnarix program (including her role as scientific liaison with Orlund—§ 2.2). If the equity grant is not made (or is made at a lower value), Dr. Sallow may have a claim for breach of the agreement, potentially triggering a Good Reason resignation (§ 5.4(d)) and jeopardizing the transaction's key-person assumption.

**Recommended fix.** Redraft § 3.3 to state: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort shall grant the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000, made under Peitort's equity incentive plan and subject to the terms of the applicable award agreement. The grant shall vest in three equal annual installments beginning on the first anniversary of the Effective Date, subject to continued employment."

### 6. Board Consent May Not Cover the Side Letter

**Documents:** Peitort Board Written Consent §§ 2, 3; Seller Side Letter § 2.2

**Issue.** The Board consent (effective September 12, 2026) authorizes the officers to execute the Purchase Agreement "substantially in the form presented to the Board" and to make changes "not materially adverse to the Corporation." The side letter deletes the regulatory compliance carve-out from the indemnification cap—a change that materially increases Peitort's risk exposure. If the side letter was not presented to the Board, the officers may lack authority to sign it under the existing consent.

**Why it matters.** If the side letter is executed without proper Board authorization, there is a risk that the amendment is ultra vires or that a stockholder could challenge it. More practically, it creates an internal governance issue that could complicate enforcement.

**Recommended fix.** If Peitort agrees to the side letter (in whole or in modified form), obtain a supplemental Board consent or confirm that the existing consent's "not materially adverse" standard is satisfied. Given Issue 3 above, the safer course is to resolve the substance first and then confirm authority.

## MODERATE ISSUES

### 7. Wire Verification Procedure in Closing Emails Is Less Secure Than the Side Letter Requires

**Documents:** Closing Logistics Email (Dan Rourke, Nov. 16, item: "We will use the general switchboard number for Bratousk at 75 Rockefeller Plaza"); Seller Side Letter § 4.1

**Issue.** The side letter requires that wire instructions be verified "by telephone with Buyer's Chief Financial Officer at a number known to Buyer, and not at a number provided in the instructions themselves." The closing email thread indicates Peitort's CFO will call Bratousk's "general switchboard number." A switchboard is not the same as a direct number to a known individual, and it provides less protection against social engineering or wire fraud.

**Why it matters.** The cash payment at Closing is $162 million. Wire fraud targeting large payments is a well-known risk. Using a general switchboard (where any employee could answer and provide instructions) increases the risk that fraudulent instructions could be relayed.

**Recommended fix.** Before Closing, Peitort's CFO should obtain and record a direct telephone number for a specific named individual at Bratousk (e.g., the Managing Partner or CFO) who is authorized to confirm wire instructions. Use that direct number for the verification call. Do not rely on the switchboard.

### 8. Split Governing Law and Venue Across Transaction Documents

**Documents:** SPA § 11.10–11.11 (New York law; New York venue); Seller Note § 10.1–10.2 (Delaware law; Delaware venue); Key Employee Agreement § 11.8 (New Jersey law; New Jersey venue); Side Letter § 6.2 (New York law; New York venue); MCA § 10.5–10.6 (New York law; New York venue)

**Issue.** The transaction documents are governed by three different state laws and provide for three different venues. While SPA § 11.6 attempts to manage the interface (the Note governs for "payment, set-off and dispute resolution terms"), the split creates complexity.

**Why it matters.** In the event of a dispute involving both the SPA and the Seller Note (e.g., a set-off claim), the parties could face threshold litigation over which law and forum applies. The Key Employee Agreement's New Jersey venue is particularly awkward because Peitort is a New Jersey-based company but the SPA disputes go to New York.

**Recommended fix.** This is a lower-priority item given the SPA is already signed. However, if the Seller Note is being negotiated (see Issues 2 and 4), consider conforming its governing law to New York for consistency. If not, ensure that any cross-document dispute mechanism is clear.

## MINOR / INFORMATIONAL ISSUES

### 9. SPA § 6.1(e) – Confusing Dividend Restriction Language

**Document:** SPA § 6.1(e)

**Issue.** The conduct-of-business covenant provides that Seller shall not "declare or pay any dividend other than cash dividends paid before the Closing." The "other than" clause is redundant or confusing: if a cash dividend was already paid before the Closing, it cannot be "declared or paid" again. The likely intent is simply that no dividends may be declared or paid during the interim period.

**Recommended fix.** In any amendment or side letter, restate as: "declare or pay any dividend or make any distribution."

### 10. Orlund Sales Milestone – Near-Term Contingent Liability

**Document:** Disclosure Schedule ¶ 4.2

**Issue.** The Company owes Orlund a $2.5 million sales milestone when cumulative US net sales of Velnarix reach $250 million. As of June 30, 2026, cumulative sales are approximately $198.4 million—roughly $51.6 million short. At the Company's current run rate, this milestone is likely to be triggered within 12–18 months after Closing.

**Why it matters.** This is a known, disclosed contingent liability, but Peitort should ensure it is reflected in the working capital target or otherwise accounted for in the purchase price allocation. It is not currently listed as a current liability on the balance sheet (being contingent), so it would not affect the NWC calculation unless it becomes probable and estimable before Closing.

**Recommended fix.** No contractual fix needed, but flag for Peitort's finance team in the post-closing integration plan and tax allocation.

### 11. Retention Bonuses – Confirm Inclusion in Working Capital

**Document:** Disclosure Schedule ¶ 11.5; SPA § 2.3

**Issue.** Seven employees have retention bonuses totaling $1,150,000, of which 50% ($575,000) is payable at Closing. This amount should be included as a current liability in the closing net working capital calculation.

**Why it matters.** If the $575,000 is omitted from the NWC estimate, the cash payment to Seller would be overstated by that amount.

### 12. Product Liability Insurance – Reservation of Rights

**Document:** Disclosure Schedule ¶ 10.4

**Issue.** The Company's product liability insurer (Sterling Specialty, $25 million aggregate limit, $250,000 retention—¶ 12.1(b)) has accepted coverage for the Lorvexa claims "subject to a reservation of rights." This means the insurer may later deny coverage in whole or in part.

**Why it matters.** If the reservation of rights matures into a coverage denial, the full cost of the Lorvexa claims (defense costs already accrued at $410,000 per ¶ 4.1, plus any judgments or settlements) would fall on the Company/Peitort. Peitort's indemnification claim against Seller under § 9.2(d) would be the primary recourse, but the set-off limitation (Issue 2) means Peitort cannot net those losses against the Seller Note.

**Recommended fix.** In addition to fixing Issue 2, consider requiring Seller to confirm in writing at or before Closing that it is aware of the reservation of rights and that the § 9.2(d) indemnity applies regardless of the insurance outcome. Also confirm that SPA § 9.6(d) (netting of insurance proceeds) operates as intended: if the insurer ultimately pays, that amount reduces the indemnifiable Losses.

### 13. MCA Non-Solicitation – Post-Closing Hiring

**Document:** Mutual Confidentiality Agreement § 5.1

**Issue.** The MCA prohibits Peitort from soliciting or hiring Company officers or director-level employees for 18 months after the Effective Date (May 4, 2026), i.e., until approximately November 4, 2027. The carve-out in § 5.2(c) covers "any employment arrangement entered into with the Company's consent or in connection with a definitive agreement for the Proposed Transaction," which should cover Dr. Sallow under the Key Employee Agreement.

**Why it matters.** If Peitort wishes to hire other Company employees (beyond Dr. Sallow) who leave voluntarily after Closing, the non-solicit could restrict that ability until November 2027. This is a modest constraint but worth noting for the integration team.

**Recommended fix.** No action required, but flag for the HR/integration team.
