### 1. Orlund Change-of-Control Consent Missing from Required Consents

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.4, item 2.1; SPA, Schedule 7.2(d); SPA, Section 7.2(d)

The Disclosure Schedule (item 2.1) confirms that Section 14.3 of the Exclusive License Agreement with Orlund BioSciences GmbH prohibits any change of control of the Company without Orlund's prior written consent, and that the transaction constitutes such a change of control. If consent is not obtained, Orlund may terminate the Exclusive License Agreement.

However, Schedule 7.2(d) lists only four state board of pharmacy approvals as Required Consents. Orlund's consent is absent. Consequently:

- Orlund's consent is **not** a condition to Closing under SPA Section 7.2(d).
- Seller is **not** obligated under SPA Section 6.4 to use "reasonable best efforts" to obtain it (that covenant covers only Required Consents).
- Peitort could close on December 7 (the target date per the closing logistics email of November 17) without Orlund having consented, and then face termination of the license covering Velnarix—the Company's lead product.

The Disclosure Schedule notes that written notice was delivered to Orlund on August 27, 2026, but no document in the folder evidences Orlund's response. As of the November 17 email, Orlund's consent is not mentioned among the open items.

**Why it matters:** Loss of the Velnarix license would devastate the value of the acquisition. The $186M purchase price is predicated on the Company retaining its exclusive U.S. and Canadian rights to Velnarix.

**Recommended fix:** (a) Immediately confirm with Seller and Orlund's counsel the status of the consent request. (b) Add Orlund's written consent to Schedule 7.2(d) as a Required Consent, making it a condition to Closing under Section 7.2(d). (c) If Seller resists, at minimum add a specific closing condition in Section 7.2 that the Exclusive License Agreement remain in full force and effect and not terminated or subject to a termination right as of the Closing Date. (d) Do not close until Orlund's consent is in hand.

### 2. Seller Side Letter Would Eliminate Regulatory Compliance Cap Carve-Out

**Documents:** Seller Side Letter Draft, Section 2.2; SPA, Section 9.6(c); Letter of Intent, Section 5(d)

The Seller Side Letter (draft, undated) provides in Section 2.2 that Section 9.6(c) of the SPA is amended by **deleting** the words "or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)." As currently drafted in the SPA, Section 9.6(c) provides that the $18.6M General Cap does **not** apply to Losses arising from a breach of Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance), and that Seller's liability for such Losses is capped only at the full Purchase Price ($186M).

The Letter of Intent (Section 5(d)) expressly contemplated that "breaches of the regulatory compliance representation" would be carved out from the General Cap. The side letter reverses this.

**Why it matters:** Section 4.12 covers compliance with the FDCA, cGMP, FDA permits/registrations/approvals, and the absence of FDA Form 483s, warning letters, recalls, seizures or suspensions. The Disclosure Schedule already reveals a VAI-classified FDA inspection (item 8.1) and a voluntary market withdrawal of Lorvexa (item 8.2). For a specialty pharmaceutical manufacturer, regulatory risk is the principal post-closing hazard. Capping Peitort's recovery for regulatory breaches at $18.6M—less than the Seller Note alone—leaves Peitort bearing the vast majority of any significant regulatory failure (e.g., a warning letter, consent decree, or product recall) out of pocket.

**Recommended fix:** Do **not** sign the side letter as drafted. Restore "or Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance)" to Section 9.6(c). If Seller insists on a compromise, consider: (a) a separate, higher cap for Section 4.12 breaches (e.g., 25–50% of the Purchase Price); or (b) narrowing the carve-out to cover only FDA warning letters, consent decrees, and recalls (rather than all regulatory compliance breaches). In no event should the cap for regulatory breaches be reduced to the $18.6M General Cap.

### 3. Seller Note Set-Off Right Narrower Than SPA and LOI

**Documents:** Form of Seller Note, Section 4.1; SPA, Section 9.9; SPA, Section 11.6; Letter of Intent, Section 3(f)

SPA Section 9.9 provides: "Buyer may set off **any amount** to which any Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled under this Article IX against any amount otherwise payable by Buyer under the Seller Note, subject to the terms of the Seller Note."

The Letter of Intent (Section 3(f)) stated: "Buyer would have a right of set-off against amounts payable under the Seller Note for amounts owed to Buyer under **the indemnification provisions** of the Definitive Agreement."

But Seller Note Section 4.1 limits the set-off to: "Losses arising from **Product Liability Claims** relating to Company Products for which any Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled to indemnification by the Holder under Article IX."

SPA Section 11.6 (Order of Precedence) provides that "the Seller Note shall govern with respect to its payment, set-off and dispute resolution terms." The Seller Note's narrow set-off therefore controls.

**Why it matters:** Peitort cannot set off general indemnification claims (e.g., breaches of financial statement reps, tax reps, undisclosed liabilities, or—critically—regulatory compliance breaches) against the $24M note. The only losses Peitort can net against the note are product liability losses. This means that if, for example, a regulatory breach generates $10M in Losses, Peitort must still make full payments on the Seller Note and pursue a separate indemnification claim against Seller. Given that Seller is a limited liability company whose principal asset will be the $162M cash proceeds it receives at Closing, collecting on a post-closing indemnification judgment may be difficult. The set-off right is Peitort's primary security for indemnification recovery.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Seller Note Section 4.1 to track SPA Section 9.9, permitting set-off for **any** Losses for which a Buyer Indemnified Party is entitled to indemnification under Article IX of the SPA. At minimum, expand the set-off to include breaches of Fundamental Representations, Section 4.12 (Regulatory Compliance), and Section 4.11 (Taxes). If Seller resists, negotiate a tiered approach: full set-off for Fundamental Reps, Fraud, and Regulatory Compliance; product liability set-off for all other claims.

## SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 4. Governing Law and Jurisdiction Split Between SPA and Seller Note

**Documents:** SPA, Sections 11.10–11.11; Form of Seller Note, Sections 10.1–10.2

The SPA is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts. The Seller Note is governed by **Delaware** law with exclusive jurisdiction in the **Delaware Superior Court**. SPA Section 11.6 acknowledges this by providing that the Seller Note "shall govern with respect to its payment, set-off and dispute resolution terms."

**Why it matters:** If Peitort asserts a set-off under the Seller Note and Seller disputes it, the dispute is in Delaware. If Peitort simultaneously asserts an indemnification claim under the SPA, that claim is in New York. This creates the risk of inconsistent rulings, parallel proceedings, and increased litigation cost. It also means Peitort's set-off right (its principal security) is enforced in a forum and under a body of law different from the underlying indemnification obligation.

**Recommended fix:** Conforming the Seller Note to New York law and New York County jurisdiction is the cleanest solution. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps for its own convenience as a Delaware LLC), at minimum add a provision in the Seller Note permitting the Maker to raise set-off as an affirmative defense in any New York action brought under the SPA, and vice versa. Alternatively, provide for arbitration of set-off disputes under a single set of rules.

### 6. Key Employee Agreement – Section 7.2(e) Circularity

**Documents:** SPA, Section 7.2(e); Key Employee Agreement, Section 1.1

SPA Section 7.2(e) makes it a condition to Buyer's Closing obligation that "the Key Employee Agreement shall be in full force and effect and Dr. Imogen Sallow shall not have repudiated it." However, Key Employee Agreement Section 1.1 provides that the Agreement "shall become effective automatically on, and subject to, the occurrence of the Closing."

The Agreement cannot be "in full force and effect" before the Closing because it does not become effective until the Closing occurs. This is a circularity: the condition to Closing requires the Agreement to be in effect, but the Agreement only takes effect at Closing.

**Why it matters:** In a technical sense, the condition in Section 7.2(e) can never be satisfied before the Closing Date, which could give Seller an argument that Peitort failed to satisfy a condition to its own Closing obligation. More practically, it creates ambiguity about whether Peitort can close if Dr. Sallow signs the Agreement but a court later finds the condition was not technically met.

**Recommended fix:** Amend SPA Section 7.2(e) to provide that the condition is satisfied if (a) the Key Employee Agreement has been duly executed by both the Company and Dr. Sallow before the Closing Date, and (b) Dr. Sallow has not repudiated or given notice of termination of the Agreement before the Closing Date. The Agreement then becomes effective at Closing by its own terms.

### 7. Key Employee Agreement – Section 3.3 Drafting Defect

**Documents:** Key Employee Agreement, Section 3.3

Section 3.3 provides: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort's compensation committee **will be recommended to grant** the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000."

This is grammatically incoherent. It does not clearly state whether (a) Peitort is obligated to grant the RSUs, (b) the compensation committee is obligated to recommend the grant (to the board), or (c) some unidentified party will recommend the committee to grant. The obligation, if any, is unclear.

**Why it matters:** The $1.2M equity grant is a material component of Dr. Sallow's compensation package and was presumably a factor in her willingness to sign the Key Employee Agreement (which is itself a condition to Closing). If the grant obligation is ambiguous, Dr. Sallow may have a claim for breach of the Agreement (or a Good Reason resignation under Section 5.4(d)) if the RSUs are not granted. This could jeopardize the retention of the Company's Chief Scientific Officer.

**Recommended fix:** Rewrite Section 3.3 to state: "Within 60 days after the Effective Date, Peitort shall grant the Executive restricted stock units with a grant-date value of $1,200,000, subject to approval by Peitort's compensation committee and the terms of Peitort's equity incentive plan." If the grant must be subject to committee discretion, say so expressly, but at minimum make the obligation to *submit* the grant for committee approval mandatory.

### 8. MCA Term vs. SPA Section 6.5 – Post-Closing Confidentiality Gap

**Documents:** Mutual Confidentiality Agreement, Section 9.1; SPA, Section 6.5

MCA Section 9.1 provides that the Agreement "remains in effect until the second anniversary of the Effective Date" (i.e., May 4, 2028). SPA Section 6.5 provides that "The Mutual Confidentiality Agreement between Buyer and Seller shall remain in effect until the Closing in accordance with its terms."

Read literally, SPA Section 6.5 could be interpreted as terminating the MCA at Closing (i.e., in December 2026), well before the MCA's own two-year term expires. The phrase "in accordance with its terms" is meant to preserve the MCA's internal mechanics, but the "until the Closing" language creates ambiguity.

**Why it matters:** Post-closing, Peitort will possess extensive Confidential Information about the Company (formulations, regulatory data, supplier terms, Orlund license details). If the MCA is deemed terminated at Closing, Peitort's confidentiality obligations to Seller (and vice versa) would lapse, potentially exposing both parties. More importantly for Peitort, the MCA's non-solicitation provision (Section 5.1, 18 months from May 4, 2026) and the legally required disclosure protections would no longer apply.

**Recommended fix:** Clarify SPA Section 6.5 to state that the MCA remains in effect for its full term as set forth in its own Section 9.1, and that the reference to "until the Closing" applies only to the pre-closing use restrictions in Section 2.1 (i.e., the purpose limitation narrows at Closing but the confidentiality obligations continue). Alternatively, delete SPA Section 6.5 and rely on the MCA's own term provision.

### 9. Tax Indemnity Survival Period Is Only 12 Months

**Documents:** SPA, Section 9.2(c); SPA, Section 9.1

SPA Section 9.2(c) provides that Seller indemnifies Buyer for pre-closing Taxes, but "claims under this Section 9.2(c) may be made only during the survival period applicable to the representations and warranties in Section 4.11." Section 4.11 is a general (not Fundamental) representation, so its survival period is 12 months after Closing (Section 9.1).

**Why it matters:** Tax audits can be initiated years after the relevant tax year. The IRS can generally assess tax for three years after filing (six years for substantial understatement). A 12-month survival period means that if the IRS opens an audit of the Company's 2025 tax return in January 2028 (16 months post-closing), Peitort has no indemnification recourse against Seller for any additional tax, interest or penalties attributable to the pre-closing period. For a pharmaceutical company with R&D credits, transfer pricing considerations, and potential state apportionment issues, this is a meaningful gap.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a longer survival period for the tax indemnity—ideally until the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (plus a 60-day buffer) for pre-closing tax periods. If Seller resists, a compromise of 18–24 months is better than 12.

Seven employees have received retention bonus letters (dated August 1, 2026) with an aggregate value of $1,150,000. Fifty percent ($575,000) is payable at Closing; fifty percent is payable six months after Closing, in each case subject to continued employment.

The $575,000 payable six months post-Closing is arguably not a current liability as of the Closing Date (it is contingent on continued employment and is not yet due). The treatment of these amounts will affect the working capital true-up. Peitort should confirm that Seller's estimate (due three Business Days before Closing per Section 2.3(a)) properly includes the $575,000 at-closing portion as a current liability.

If Seller excludes it, raise the issue in the 30-day dispute period under Section 2.3(b).

## MODERATE / DRAFTING ISSUES

### 11. Seller Note – Payment Block Duration

**Documents:** Form of Seller Note, Section 6.2

The subordination provision permits Senior Lenders to block payments on the Seller Note for up to 179 days in any 360-day period. This is a standard "springing" subordination mechanic, but the 179-day maximum is at the longer end of market.

**Why it matters:** If Peitort's senior lenders assert a payment block, Peitort could be unable to make quarterly interest payments ($360,000 per quarter) on the Seller Note for nearly six months. While this protects Peitort's senior lenders, it creates cash flow uncertainty and could strain the relationship with Seller. It also means that if an Event of Default occurs on the senior facility, the Seller Note payments could be suspended at the worst possible time.

**Recommended fix:** This is a term negotiated for Peitort's benefit (as maker of the note), so no change is needed. However, Peitort's treasury team should model the cash flow impact of a 179-day payment block to ensure the Company can absorb the deferred interest without triggering a cross-default.

### 12. Seller Note – Default Interest Rate

**Documents:** Form of Seller Note, Section 3.4

Default interest is the stated rate (6.0%) plus 2.0% = 8.0% per annum.

**Why it matters:** This is within market range but on the higher end for a corporate obligor. If Peitort experiences a temporary cash flow disruption (e.g., due to the payment block in Section 6.2 or a working capital adjustment payment under Section 2.3(c)), a 10-business-day cure period (Section 7.1(a)) could be tight, and the 8% default rate would increase the cost of the note during the default period.

**Recommended fix:** Consider negotiating the default rate down to 1.5% above stated (7.5%) or extending the cure period to 15 Business Days. Low priority given the overall deal terms.

### 14. SPA Section 9.6(d) – Insurance Offset

**Documents:** SPA, Section 9.6(d)

Losses are calculated "net of any insurance proceeds actually received by the Indemnified Party with respect to them, less the costs of recovery and any resulting premium increase."

**Why it matters:** The Disclosure Schedule (item 10.4) confirms that the Company's product liability insurer has accepted coverage for the pending Lorvexa claims "subject to a reservation of rights." A reservation of rights means the insurer may later deny coverage or seek contribution. If the insurer ultimately pays, those proceeds reduce Peitort's indemnification recovery from Seller. However, the "costs of recovery and any resulting premium increase" offset is favorable to Peitort.

**Recommended fix:** No change needed, but Peitort should: (a) monitor the reservation of rights letters closely; (b) ensure the Company (as Peitort's subsidiary post-closing) does not settle any product liability claim in a way that impairs the insurance recovery; and (c) track premium increases attributable to the Lorvexa claims so they can be added back to the Losses calculation.

### 16. SPA Section 4.6 – Undisclosed Liabilities and the $410K Defense Costs

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 4.6, item 4.1; SPA, Section 4.6

The Disclosure Schedule discloses $410,000 in accrued but unpaid defense costs (as of August 31, 2026) relating to the product liability claims. SPA Section 4.6 represents that the Company has no liabilities except those on the June 30, 2026 balance sheet, ordinary course liabilities since then, and those on Schedule 4.6.

**Recommended fix:** Confirm in the working capital estimate that defense costs accrued through the Closing Date are included as current liabilities. The $410,000 figure is a floor, not a ceiling.

**Documents:** Peitort Board Written Consent, Section 2; SPA, Section 11.8

The Board consent authorizes the Authorized Officers to execute the SPA "with such changes as that Authorized Officer, acting with the advice of counsel, approves as necessary or appropriate and not materially adverse to the Corporation."

**Why it matters:** This is a standard "not materially adverse" delegation. However, given the issues identified above (particularly the side letter removing the regulatory cap carve-out and the Seller Note set-off limitation), Peitort should ensure that any further amendments to the SPA or ancillary documents that are "materially adverse" are brought back to the Board for approval. The side letter, if signed as drafted, would arguably be materially adverse (it reduces Peitort's maximum recovery for regulatory breaches from $186M to $18.6M).

**Recommended fix:** Before executing the side letter or any further amendment, confirm with the General Counsel whether the change is "materially adverse" within the meaning of the Board consent. If yes, obtain a supplemental Board approval.
