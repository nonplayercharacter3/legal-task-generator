## Issue 1 – Non-Renewal Notice Was Likely Untimely; Agreement May Have Auto-Renewed for Three Years

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 9.2; Amendment No. 1, Recitals B–C; Distribution Agreement § 14.2

**Explanation:**

The Initial Term is two years from March 1, 2024, expiring February 28, 2026 (Agreement § 9.1). Section 9.2 provides that the Agreement auto-renews for a three-year Renewal Term "unless either Party gives the other written notice of non-renewal at least 90 days before expiry of the then-current term," and adds: "To be effective, such notice must be **received** no later than ninety (90) days prior to the last day of the then-current term."

Ninety days before February 28, 2026 is **November 30, 2025**. Rirux gave its Non-Renewal Notice on **December 10, 2025** (Amendment No. 1, Recital B) — ten days after the deadline. Section 14.2 confirms that "a notice is effective on receipt."

Brisosk has expressly disputed the timeliness (Amendment No. 1, Recital C), and on the face of the documents, Brisosk appears to be correct. If the notice was untimely, the Agreement renewed automatically for three years (through approximately February 28, 2029), and Rirux is bound by all its terms, including the full exclusivity in § 2.1.

**Why it matters:** This is the single most consequential issue. If the Agreement has renewed, Rirux cannot sell, lease, market, or distribute Products to any Customer in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland, or New York — including its own national accounts — for three more years. Rirux also cannot appoint any other distributor in those states.

**Recommended fix:**

- Immediately confirm the exact date and method of delivery of the Non-Renewal Notice and the date Brisosk received it. If there is any argument that the notice was received by November 30 (e.g., sent by overnight courier on November 28 but logged as received December 10 due to a holiday), preserve that evidence.
- Assess whether Rirux has any equitable arguments (e.g., waiver, estoppel, course of performance) that could support the position that the notice was effective despite the 10-day delay.
- If the renewal is likely to hold, begin planning for a negotiated exit or amendment: Rirux's leverage is limited while the Agreement is in force, but the ongoing dispute and the fact that Amendment No. 1 was executed with a mutual reservation of rights (Amendment No. 1, § 4) means both parties are operating under cloud. A commercial resolution (e.g., narrowed territory, reduced exclusivity, or a buyout of the remaining term) may be preferable to litigation.
- If Rirux decides to litigate the renewal question, note that the exclusive forum is Delaware state or federal court (Agreement § 14.1) and there is no arbitration or mediation clause.

**Facts needed from client:**
- Exact date the Non-Renewal Notice was dispatched, the method of delivery, and the date of confirmed receipt by Brisosk.
- Whether Rirux had any internal discussion or calendar reminder about the November 30 deadline, and who was responsible for sending the notice.
- Whether Rirux has continued to operate as though the Agreement were expiring (e.g., marketing to national accounts in the Territory, soliciting other distributors) after December 10, 2025. If so, that conduct could undermine a reformation or estoppel argument.
- The identity and approximate revenue of Rirux's national accounts in the five-state Territory.

## Issue 2 – Exclusivity Scope Dramatically Expanded from Prior Draft Without Apparent Carve-Outs

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 2.1; Prior Draft §§ 2.1–2.3, § 1.14; Side Letter, Recital A

**Explanation:**

The November 2023 prior draft provided:
- Territory: Pennsylvania and New Jersey only (§ 1.14).
- Exclusivity limited to **Healthcare Laundry Accounts** (§ 2.1).
- A separate **non-exclusive** appointment for all other Customers (§ 2.2).
- An express **reserved rights** provision: "All rights not expressly granted to Brisosk in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 are reserved to Rirux" (§ 2.3).

The final signed Agreement provides:
- Territory: Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland, **and New York** (§ 1.14) — three additional states.
- Exclusivity for **all Products to all Customers**, with no distinction between Healthcare Laundry Accounts and other customers (§ 2.1).
- Express prohibition on Rirux selling to "including Rirux's national accounts" (§ 2.1).
- **No reserved rights provision.** The prior draft's § 2.3 is absent.

The Side Letter's Recital A states: "the Parties agreed that Brisosk's exclusivity would cover Healthcare Laundry Accounts in Pennsylvania and New Jersey only." This directly contradicts the final Agreement.

**Why it matters:** Rirux has granted far broader exclusivity than appears to have been the commercial understanding. If the Agreement has renewed (see Issue 1), Rirux is locked out of three additional states and all non-healthcare customer segments for three years. Even if the renewal fails, Rirux should understand the scope of what it agreed to during the Initial Term and what it would be agreeing to in any renegotiation.

The Recital A in the Side Letter creates a potential reformation argument (the written Agreement does not reflect the parties' actual agreement), but under the order of precedence in Agreement § 14.3, the Distribution Agreement controls over side letters. A reformation claim would require clear and convincing evidence of mutual mistake or that the final terms were not the product of informed negotiation.

**Recommended fix:**

- If renegotiating (whether to exit the renewal or to amend going forward), insist on: (a) narrowing the Territory back to PA and NJ, or at minimum carving out DE, MD, and NY; (b) limiting exclusivity to Healthcare Laundry Accounts, with Rirux retaining the right to sell to other customer types directly or through other channels; (c) a national account carve-out listing specific accounts by name; and (d) restoration of a reserved rights provision.
- Preserve the prior draft and the Side Letter Recital A as evidence of the parties' original commercial understanding in case a reformation or parol evidence argument becomes necessary.
- Confirm with the client who negotiated the final Agreement and whether the expansion from the prior draft was a conscious business decision or a drafting error.

**Facts needed from client:**
- What was the commercial rationale for expanding the Territory from 2 states to 5 and from Healthcare-only to all customers? Was this a deliberate concession to secure Brisosk's signature?
- Which of Rirux's national accounts are located in the five-state Territory, and what is their annual revenue?
- Who at Rirux approved the final Agreement, and was the prior draft available to them during negotiation?
- Has Rirux sold to any customer in DE, MD, or NY (or to any non-healthcare customer in PA or NJ) since March 1, 2024? If so, that would constitute a breach of § 2.1 and could complicate any reformation argument.

## Issue 3 – No Stated Consequence for Failure to Meet Minimum Purchase Commitment

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 2.5; Exhibit B, ¶ 2; Agreement § 9.3

**Explanation:**

Section 2.5 requires Brisosk to purchase at least $3,500,000 in aggregate net invoice value per contract year. However, the Agreement does not specify any consequence for missing that target. There is no:
- Automatic termination right;
- Reduction or loss of exclusivity;
- Liquidated damages or make-up purchase obligation;
- Price adjustment; or
- Conversion from exclusive to non-exclusive status.

The only available remedy is a general material breach claim under § 9.3(a), which requires written notice and a 30-day cure period. Whether a single year's shortfall constitutes a "material breach" is a fact-intensive question that would likely be disputed.

**Why it matters:** Rirux has granted full exclusivity (see Issue 2) in exchange for a $3.5M annual purchase commitment, but has no contractual lever if Brisosk under-purchases. If Brisosk buys only $1M in a year, Rirux has locked itself out of the entire Territory but received a fraction of the expected revenue, and its only remedy is to litigate whether the shortfall was "material."

**Recommended fix:**

- In any renegotiation or amendment, add a specific consequence for missing the minimum purchase, such as: (a) automatic conversion to non-exclusive status for the following contract year; (b) a termination right exercisable by Rirux if the shortfall exceeds 20% of the minimum for two consecutive years; or (c) a make-up obligation requiring Brisosk to purchase the deficit in the following year at a premium.
- If the Agreement has renewed and Rirux is in a weak negotiating position, at minimum document Brisosk's actual purchases each year to build a record that could support a material breach argument if the shortfall persists.

**Facts needed from client:**
- Has Brisosk met the $3.5M minimum in Contract Year 1 (March 2024 – February 2025) and Contract Year 2 (March 2025 – February 2026)? If not, by how much?
- Is Rirux willing to accept a lower minimum in exchange for narrowing the exclusivity, or is the $3.5M figure a firm business requirement?

## Issue 4 – Side Letter Recital A Contradicts the Agreement; Order of Precedence Favors the Broader Grant

**Where:** Side Letter, Recital A; Distribution Agreement § 2.1; Agreement § 14.3

**Explanation:**

As noted in Issue 2, the Side Letter's Recital A records that the parties "agreed that Brisosk's exclusivity would cover Healthcare Laundry Accounts in Pennsylvania and New Jersey only." The final Agreement grants exclusivity for all customers in five states. Under § 14.3, the Distribution Agreement prevails over side letters in the event of a conflict.

**Why it matters:** While the Agreement controls as a matter of contract interpretation, the Recital is admissible evidence of the parties' understanding. If Rirux wishes to argue reformation (that the final Agreement contains a scrivener's error or does not reflect the bargained-for deal), Recital A is the best piece of evidence it has. Conversely, Brisosk could point to Recital A and argue that Rirux was on notice of the limited scope and nevertheless signed the broader Agreement, undermining any reformation claim.

The Recital also sits awkwardly with the Side Letter's own operative terms. The rebate tiers in Side Letter § 2.1 are keyed to the $3.5M minimum in Exhibit B, which was negotiated on the assumption of full-territory, all-customer exclusivity. If exclusivity were truly limited to Healthcare Laundry Accounts in two states, the $3.5M minimum might have been set at a different level.

**Recommended fix:**

- Do not attempt to rely on Recital A as a standalone basis to narrow the Agreement. It is a recital, not an operative term, and § 14.3 gives the Agreement priority.
- Use Recital A as part of a broader negotiation strategy: if Rirux seeks to amend the Agreement, it can point to the Recital as evidence that the current scope was not the original commercial deal and that both parties would benefit from a correction.
- If litigation over the renewal (Issue 1) proceeds, be prepared for Brisosk to argue that Rirux cannot now claim the Agreement is broader than intended because the Side Letter shows Rirux knew what it was signing.

**Facts needed from client:**
- Who drafted Recital A, and was it included in the Side Letter at the request of Rirux or Brisosk?
- Was the Side Letter negotiated simultaneously with the final Agreement, or was it a later addition?

## Issue 6 – "Critical Failure" Defined Differently in the Agreement and the SOW

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 1.4; SOW § 3.1(a); Agreement § 7.2; SOW § 3.2

**Explanation:**

- Agreement § 1.4: "Critical Failure" means "a failure rendering a Product **wholly inoperable**."
- SOW § 3.1(a): "Critical Failure" means "any malfunction of Covered Equipment that **stops the equipment from operating**, or that **reduces the rated throughput of the equipment by ten percent (10%) or more**."

The SOW definition is materially broader. A 10% throughput reduction is not "wholly inoperable."

The term is used in two distinct contexts:
1. **Warranty priority** (Agreement § 7.2): "Rirux shall give priority to warranty claims arising from a Critical Failure."
2. **Service levels and credits** (SOW §§ 3.2, 3.4): The broader SOW definition triggers 24/7 response, a 4-hour on-site requirement, a 24-hour restoration target, and a 5% service credit for missed on-site response.

Under the order of precedence (Agreement § 14.3), SOWs prevail "as to Services and Service Levels." So for service credit purposes, the SOW's broader definition likely controls. But for warranty priority under § 7.2, it is unclear which definition applies — the Agreement's definition (§ 1.4) or the SOW's (§ 3.1(a)).

**Why it matters:**

- For Rirux, the broader SOW definition means more events trigger the 24/7/4-hour response obligation and the service credit. A machine running at 89% throughput is a "Critical Failure" under the SOW but not under the Agreement.
- The ambiguity in warranty priority could lead to disputes about whether Rirux handled a warranty claim with the required urgency.
- The service credit exposure is compounded by Issue 7 below (no cap on stacking credits).

**Recommended fix:**

- Amend the SOW to either (a) conform the "Critical Failure" definition to the Agreement's narrower standard, or (b) create a separate defined term (e.g., "Service Critical Event") for the broader 10% throughput standard used in service levels, so that the warranty priority provision in § 7.2 is not implicated.
- If the parties cannot agree on a conforming definition, add a clarifying sentence to the SOW stating that the SOW definition applies solely for purposes of service levels and does not modify the Agreement's definition for warranty or other purposes.

## Issue 7 – Service Credits Can Stack Without a Cap

**Where:** SOW § 3.2 (last paragraph)

**Explanation:**

The SOW provides: "For **each** Critical Failure where Rirux does not meet the on-site response time in this Section 3.2, Rirux shall credit Brisosk five percent (5%) of the monthly support fee for that month."

The word "each" indicates the credit applies per event. There is no cap on the number of service credits that can accrue in a single month. If Rirux misses the on-site response for five Critical Failures in one month, Brisosk could claim 25% of the monthly support fee.

**Why it matters:**

The monthly support fee is calculated per unit (SOW § 6.1). For a large installed base (e.g., 200 washer-extractors at $85 = $17,000/month; 5 tunnel washers at $1,250 = $6,250/month; 100 dosing systems at $60 = $6,000/month; total ≈ $29,250/month), a single missed Critical Failure response costs Rirux ~$1,462. Five missed responses in a month would cost ~$7,312. In a bad month with multiple Critical Failures (e.g., a software update that causes widespread issues), the exposure could be significant.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a cap on aggregate service credits per month (e.g., "not to exceed 25% of the monthly support fee in any month") or per contract year.
- Alternatively, provide that service credits are the sole and exclusive remedy for failure to meet response times, which the current language arguably already does but which could be made explicit.

## Issue 8 – Warranty Period Drafting Creates Ambiguity for Delayed Installations

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 7.1

**Explanation:**

Section 7.1 provides a 12-month warranty "from the **earlier** of installation at the Customer site and ninety (90) days after delivery to Brisosk."

If Brisosk receives a Product and does not install it for, say, 180 days, the warranty starts at day 90 (the earlier trigger) and expires at day 90 + 365 = day 455. The Product is installed at day 180, so the Customer gets only 275 days (~9 months) of warranty from actual installation.

Conversely, if the Product is installed at day 30, the warranty starts at day 30 and expires at day 395, giving the Customer a full 12 months from installation.

**Why it matters:**

- This drafting is actually **favorable to Rirux** (it limits warranty exposure for slow-moving inventory). However, it is likely to generate customer complaints and disputes with Brisosk, who may be pressured by its Customers to honor a full 12-month warranty from installation.
- If Brisosk passes through the warranty to Customers (as it is entitled to do under § 7.4), Brisosk's Customers in the delayed-installation scenario will have less than 12 months of coverage, which could damage Rirux's brand and generate claims against Brisosk that Brisosk then passes back to Rirux.

**Recommended fix:**

- If Rirux wishes to maintain the current structure (which limits its exposure), consider adding clarifying language: "For purposes of this Section 7.1, if installation occurs more than ninety (90) days after delivery to Brisosk, the warranty period shall be measured from the ninety-day mark, and Brisosk shall inform the Customer of the applicable warranty expiration date at the time of installation."
- If Rirux is willing to concede the point to reduce friction, change "earlier" to "later" so the warranty always runs 12 months from installation (with a backstop of, e.g., 18 months from delivery to prevent indefinite warranty exposure on uninstalled inventory).

## Issue 9 – "Net Invoice Value" Is Undefined in the Agreement

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 2.5; Exhibit B, ¶ 2; Side Letter § 1.3

**Explanation:**

The minimum purchase commitment in § 2.5 and Exhibit B ¶ 2 uses the phrase "aggregate net invoice value." This term is not defined in the Agreement. The Side Letter defines "Net Purchases" (a different, capitalized term) for rebate calculation purposes, and that definition excludes taxes, freight, insurance, returns, credits, and SOW fees (Side Letter § 1.3).

It is unclear whether "net invoice value" for the minimum purchase commitment has the same exclusions as "Net Purchases" for rebate purposes. For example:
- Do SOW fees count toward the minimum purchase?
- Are returns and credits netted out?
- Is freight included or excluded?

**Why it matters:** If SOW fees (which can be substantial given the per-unit monthly rates in SOW § 6.1) count toward the $3.5M minimum, Brisosk's actual product purchase obligation is lower than it appears. Conversely, if they don't count, Brisosk must purchase $3.5M in actual Products. The ambiguity cuts both ways and could be a source of dispute.

**Recommended fix:**

- Add a definition of "net invoice value" to the Agreement (or to Exhibit B) that specifies inclusions and exclusions. At minimum, clarify whether SOW fees, freight, taxes, returns, and credits are included or excluded.
- Align the definition with the Side Letter's "Net Purchases" definition to avoid a situation where the two terms diverge.

## Issue 10 – SOW Coverage Limited to Equipment "Brisosk Sold or Installed"

**Where:** SOW § 1.2(a)

**Explanation:**

Covered Equipment under the SOW must satisfy two conditions: (a) "Brisosk sold or installed the Product at a Customer site in the Territory," and (b) Brisosk has registered the Product. If a Product was installed by a third party, a sub-distributor, or Rirux itself (e.g., during a pre-Agreement period or a demonstration), it is not Covered Equipment and Rirux has no SOW obligation to service it.

**Why it matters:**

- If the Agreement has renewed and Rirux is bound by exclusivity, all new installations in the Territory should go through Brisosk. But there may be legacy equipment at Customer sites that was installed before March 1, 2024 (or by Rirux directly during the negotiation period). Rirux may face Customer service requests for that equipment but have no contractual obligation under the SOW to respond.
- If Rirux does service legacy equipment as a goodwill matter, it is doing so outside the SOW's fee structure and service level protections.

**Recommended fix:**

- Identify any Rirux-installed or third-party-installed equipment currently in the Territory. If material, negotiate a transition provision requiring Brisosk to register and assume service responsibility for that equipment within a set period, or alternatively, have Rirux service it under a separate arrangement with appropriate fees.
- In any future SOW or amendment, consider broadening the coverage trigger from "Brisosk sold or installed" to "installed at a Customer site in the Territory" (regardless of who performed the installation), with registration as the sole gating requirement.

## Issue 11 – No Dispute Resolution Mechanism for the Renewal Dispute

**Where:** Distribution Agreement § 14.1 (governing law and jurisdiction); absence of arbitration/mediation clause

**Explanation:**

The Agreement provides for exclusive jurisdiction in Delaware courts but contains no arbitration clause, no mediation requirement, and no tiered dispute resolution process (e.g., executive escalation before litigation). The renewal dispute (Issue 1) will therefore require either a negotiated resolution or a Delaware court action.

**Why it matters:**

- Litigation in Delaware will be costly and time-consuming, and the outcome is uncertain given the 10-day lateness of the notice.
- The absence of a mediation step means the parties go directly from impasse to litigation, which is suboptimal given the ongoing commercial relationship (Amendment No. 1 shows the parties are still transacting).
- A public court filing could damage Rirux's relationship with other distributors or potential distributors in the Territory.

**Recommended fix:**

- Before filing any action, propose a structured mediation (e.g., under the Delaware Dispute Resolution Program or a private mediator) with a defined timeline. Frame it as a commercial issue, not a legal one.
- If the parties cannot resolve the renewal question, consider whether a limited-scope declaratory judgment action in Delaware is appropriate, or whether Rirux should simply operate on the assumption that the Agreement renewed and negotiate an amendment from that position.
- In any future agreement or amendment, include a tiered dispute resolution clause: good-faith negotiation → mediation → litigation (or arbitration).

## Issue 12 – No Non-Solicitation Provision

**Where:** Absent from all documents

**Explanation:**

None of the documents include a non-solicitation clause protecting either party's employees, technicians, or Customer relationships from being poached by the other party.

**Why it matters:**

Given the close operational integration (Rirux trains Brisosk's personnel under Agreement § 6.3; Rirux field engineers work at Customer sites under the SOW; Brisosk's first-line support personnel develop detailed knowledge of Rirux's products and Customer relationships), there is a risk that key personnel could move between the parties or that Brisosk could use its relationship with Rirux's field engineers to build a competing service capability.

**Recommended fix:**

- In any amendment or new agreement, add a mutual non-solicitation provision (e.g., 12–18 months) covering employees and, if appropriate, Customer relationships.

## Issues 13–15 (Lower Priority)

**13. SOW Monthly Fee Snapshot Timing.** SOW § 6.1 calculates the fee based on units "registered on the first day of each calendar month." If a unit is registered on March 15, it does not generate a fee until April 1. If a unit is decommissioned on March 20, it still generates a fee for March. This is a minor administrative point but could create small disputes. *Fix:* Clarify in the next SOW that the snapshot is as of 11:59 p.m. on the last day of the prior month.

**14. Customer Site Rules "In Advance."** SOW § 5.2 requires Customer safety and infection-control rules to be provided to Rirux "in advance." In healthcare settings, protocols can change on short notice. If a Customer updates its infection-control requirements mid-visit and Rirux personnel are not aware, Rirux could be in compliance with the SOW but in violation of the Customer's rules. *Fix:* Add language allowing Rirux personnel to comply with rules communicated at the site, and clarifying that failure to comply with uncommunicated rules is not a breach.

**15. No Additional Insured Requirement.** Agreement § 13.1 requires each party to maintain CGL and workers' comp but does not require either party to name the other as an additional insured. Given the cross-indemnification in § 11, this is a gap. *Fix:* In any amendment, require Rirux to add Brisosk as an additional insured on Rirux's CGL policy (at least for product liability) and require Brisosk to do the same.
