## 1. Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) Is Not a Fundamental Representation in the Operative Documents, Contrary to the Board's Approval

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Fundamental Representations"): *"the representations and warranties of Seller set forth in the Sections of Article IV listed in Section 1.1(f) of the Disclosure Schedule."*
- Disclosure Schedule, Section 1.1(f): Lists only §§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, and 4.5. Section 4.14 is **absent**.
- Board Consent, § 2(c): States that Fundamental Representations include *"Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) of the SPA, together with Sections 4.1 (Organization), 4.2 (Authority), 4.3 (Capitalization) and 4.5 (Brokers)."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

The SPA's definition of "Fundamental Representations" is controlled by the Disclosure Schedule (per SPA § 1.1 and § 11.9, which provides that the body of the Agreement prevails over the Disclosure Schedule in case of conflict—but here the body *incorporates by reference* the Disclosure Schedule's list, so the Disclosure Schedule is the operative source). Under the Disclosure Schedule as delivered, Section 4.14 is **not** a Fundamental Representation. The consequences are severe:

- **Cap applies.** Under SPA § 9.4(a), the 10% cap ($18.4 million) applies to all non-Fundamental Representation breaches. If Section 4.14 were a Fundamental Representation, the cap would not apply.
- **Short survival period.** Under SPA § 9.1, non-Fundamental Representations survive only six (6) months after the Closing Date. Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations.
- **Board approval is based on a misstatement.** The Board Consent expressly conditions its approval on Section 4.14 being a Fundamental Representation. The officers are authorized to make changes only if *"consistent with the principal terms in Section 2"* (Board Consent § 3). As the deal papers currently stand, the terms are **not** consistent with what the Board approved.

**Recommended fix:**

Add Section 4.14 to the list in Disclosure Schedule Section 1.1(f). This requires Seller's agreement. If Seller resists, Drapesk should consider whether the deal is acceptable on the Board's original understanding and, if not, whether a supplemental Board consent is required. Alternatively, negotiate a standalone environmental indemnity (see Issue 2 below) that provides equivalent protection regardless of the Fundamental Representation classification.

## 2. Environmental Claims Under Section 4.14 Are Effectively Time-Barred by the Interaction of §§ 9.1, 6.8, and 9.6

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.1: Non-Fundamental Representations survive until **six (6) months** after the Closing Date. A Claim Notice must be delivered *"prior to the expiration of the applicable survival period."*
- SPA § 6.8: The Phase II Report is due **no later than 180 days** after the Closing Date.
- SPA § 9.6: *"Buyer shall not deliver a Claim Notice with respect to any inaccuracy in or breach of Section 4.14 until thirty (30) days after Buyer's receipt of the Phase II Report."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

The math is inescapable if Section 4.14 is not a Fundamental Representation:

| Event | Timing |
|---|---|
| Closing Date | Day 0 |
| Survival period expires (§ 9.1) | Day 180 |
| Phase II Report due (§ 6.8) | Day 180 |
| Earliest Claim Notice permitted (§ 9.6) | Day 210 (180 + 30) |

The Claim Notice deadline (Day 210) falls **after** the survival period expires (Day 180). Drapesk can never timely deliver a Claim Notice for a Section 4.14 breach. The environmental representation is therefore illusory.

Even if the Phase II Report is delivered early (say, Day 150), Drapesk would have only 30 days to review it and deliver a Claim Notice (by Day 180), leaving no practical time to assess complex environmental findings.

This issue is compounded by the escrow release under § 9.7, which occurs within five Business Days after the six-month mark—meaning the $9.2 million escrow will be released to Seller before any environmental claim could be asserted.

**Recommended fix (in order of preference):**

1. **Add Section 4.14 to the Fundamental Representations** (per Issue 1). This extends survival to 60 days after the statute of limitations and removes the cap. The § 9.6 30-day delay would then be workable because the survival period is measured in years, not months.
2. **If Seller will not agree to (1), amend § 9.6** to provide that the environmental survival period is tolled during the Environmental Review Period and for an additional 180 days thereafter (i.e., survival for Section 4.14 claims runs until 30 days after the Phase II Report is delivered, plus 180 days to assess and make a claim).
3. **Negotiate a standalone environmental indemnity** outside Article IX, with its own survival period (e.g., the full applicable statute of limitations), its own cap (or no cap), and no dependency on the Phase II Report timing. This is the approach most likely to be acceptable to Seller if it resists reclassifying Section 4.14.
4. **In all cases, amend § 9.7** to provide that the escrow is not released until at least 30 days after delivery of the Phase II Report, or until the later of the six-month anniversary and the expiration of the environmental claim period.

## 3. Asymmetric Assignment Right Allows Seller to Delegate Indemnification Obligations and Be Released

**Document and Section:**
- SPA § 11.5: *"Buyer may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder without the prior written consent of Seller. Seller may assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder to any Person without the consent of Buyer, and upon delivery to Buyer of a written instrument by which the assignee assumes the obligations so assigned, Seller shall thereupon be released from such obligations."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

Seller can assign its indemnification obligations under Article IX to any third party—potentially an empty shell or an affiliate with no meaningful assets—and be completely released. Drapesk's primary post-closing recourse (the indemnity, backed by the $9.2 million escrow) could be rendered worthless if Seller assigns to an entity that cannot pay. The escrow itself is held by Calloway Trust Bank and would presumably follow the indemnity obligation, but any amounts above the escrow (up to the $18.4 million cap, or unlimited if Fundamental) would be at risk.

This is particularly concerning given that the principal expected claims are environmental (see Issues 1 and 2), which could exceed the escrow.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend § 11.5 to provide that Seller may not assign its obligations under Article IX (indemnification) without Buyer's prior written consent, or that any assignee must demonstrate net worth or provide a guaranty sufficient to cover the maximum indemnification exposure. At minimum, Seller should not be released from its indemnification obligations upon assignment.

## 4. Section 9.3(b) Requires Buyer to Indemnify Seller for Pre-Closing Operations

**Document and Section:**
- SPA § 9.3(b): Buyer indemnifies Seller for Losses arising from *"the ownership or operation of the Company or any of the Facilities, whether before or after the Closing."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

The phrase *"whether before or after the Closing"* is unusually broad. Read literally, if a third party (e.g., a former employee, a neighbor, a regulator) asserts a claim against Seller arising from the Company's pre-closing operations—such as a pre-closing environmental release, a pre-closing employment dispute, or a pre-closing contract claim—Drapesk would be obligated to indemnify Seller. This directly undercuts the purpose of Seller's environmental and operational representations in Article IV and could create circular indemnity claims.

For example: If a pre-closing electrolyte spill injures a third party who sues both the Company (now Drapesk's subsidiary) and Seller (as former parent), the Company's defense costs and any judgment could trigger Drapesk's indemnity to Seller under § 9.3(b), even though the underlying event occurred pre-closing and is the subject of Seller's representation in § 4.14(c).

**Recommended fix:**

Delete *"whether before or after the Closing"* from § 9.3(b), or replace with *"after the Closing."* If Seller insists on pre-closing coverage, limit it to claims that do not also give rise to an indemnity obligation of Seller under § 9.2 (i.e., no double-dipping).

## 5. Pre-Closing Invasive Sampling Requires Seller's Consent, Limiting Due Diligence

**Document and Section:**
- SPA § 6.2: *"Any invasive sampling prior to the Closing shall require Seller's prior written consent."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

Drapesk is acquiring lithium-ion battery recycling facilities with significant environmental risk (black mass storage, electrolyte handling, wastewater). The only pre-closing environmental data are the Phase I ESAs (November–December 2025) and the internal EHS audit (September 2025) listed in Disclosure Schedule § 4.14(f). If Drapesk's own environmental consultant recommends pre-closing soil or groundwater sampling to confirm conditions before closing, it cannot proceed without Seller's consent. Seller has every incentive to deny consent if sampling might reveal contamination that would breach § 4.14.

This is especially acute given that the Phase II is post-closing (SPA § 6.8), meaning Drapesk closes with only Phase I-level information and no ability to independently verify subsurface conditions.

**Recommended fix:**

Either (a) delete the consent requirement for sampling at the three Facilities listed in Disclosure Schedule § 4.10, or (b) provide that consent shall not be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed, and that Seller shall bear the cost of any sampling it consents to. Alternatively, negotiate a pre-closing Phase II at one or more facilities as a condition to closing.

## 6. Section 9.6 Caps Remediation at the "Least Stringent Standard" for Industrial Use

**Document and Section:**
- SPA § 9.6 (last sentence): *"Any remediation undertaken in respect of an environmental claim shall be conducted to the least stringent standard permitted under applicable Environmental Law for the continued industrial use of the relevant Facility."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

This provision limits the scope of any remediation that Seller is obligated to fund or perform. If the Phase II reveals, for example, groundwater contamination with PFAS or heavy metals, Seller's obligation extends only to the minimum standard for continued *industrial* use—not to residential standards, not to natural background levels, and not to any standard that might be required if Drapesk later repurposes the property. Given that two of the three Facilities are in urban areas (Henderson and Chandler) where future land-use changes are plausible, this cap could leave Drapesk with significant residual liability and stigmatization risk.

**Recommended fix:**

Delete the last sentence of § 9.6, or replace with language that requires remediation to the standard necessary to satisfy all applicable Environmental Laws for the *current and reasonably foreseeable future* use of the Facility. At minimum, carve out any contamination that exceeds applicable residential or ecological standards.

## 7. Employee Benefit Plans Include Seller-Sponsored Plans with No Transition Provision

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.15: Lists *"Trepal Holdings Corp. Group Medical, Dental and Vision Plan"* and *"Trepal Holdings Corp. Group Life and Disability Insurance Plan"* as plans in which the Company participates.
- SPA § 6.9: Provides only that base salary/wage rates shall be no less favorable for 12 months post-closing. No provision addresses benefit plan continuity.

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

At closing, the Company will no longer be part of Trepal's corporate family. The Trepal-sponsored health, dental, vision, life, and disability plans will presumptively terminate for Company employees. There is no SPA provision requiring Seller to (a) continue the plans for a transition period, (b) reimburse Drapesk for the cost of providing comparable benefits, or (c) provide COBRA or other bridge coverage. This creates an immediate benefits gap that could trigger employee attrition, ERISA issues, or claims of breach of the § 6.9 covenant if "no less favorable" is read to encompass benefits (though it is limited to "base salary or wage rates").

**Recommended fix:**

Add a closing deliverable or post-closing covenant requiring Seller to (a) continue the Trepal-sponsored plans for Company employees for a reasonable transition period (e.g., 90 days) or until the Company is enrolled in Drapesk's plans, whichever is earlier, at Seller's cost; or (b) pay Drapesk an amount equal to the incremental cost of providing comparable benefits during the first 12 months. Alternatively, confirm that Drapesk's onboarding plan addresses this and that the § 6.9 salary covenant is sufficient for the Board's purposes.

## 9. Spent Cell Supply Agreement (Pre-Existing) May Conflict with or Be Superseded by § 6.10(b)

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.8(10): *"Spent Cell Supply Agreement, dated July 1, 2022, between Seller and the Company, covering manufacturing scrap and spent cells generated by Seller's Affiliates."*
- SPA § 6.10(b): During the Restricted Period, Seller must deliver all spent lithium-ion cells, battery packs, and manufacturing scrap to the Company *"at prices and terms no less favorable to the Company than those in effect during the twelve (12) months prior to the Closing Date."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

The Spent Cell Supply Agreement is a Material Contract that continues post-closing (it is not listed as terminated, unlike the Intercompany Services Agreement). Post-closing, it will be a contract between Seller and Drapesk's subsidiary. There is potential for:

- **Conflicting terms.** The existing agreement may have pricing, volume, quality, or logistics terms that differ from what § 6.10(b) requires. § 6.10(b) sets a floor ("no less favorable"), but the existing agreement's specific terms control unless amended.
- **Ambiguity about which instrument governs.** If the existing agreement has a term that expires before the five-year Restricted Period, is Seller still obligated under § 6.10(b)? Presumably yes, but the interaction should be clarified.
- **Change-of-control or assignment issues.** The existing agreement may have its own change-of-control provisions not disclosed in § 4.4 of the Disclosure Schedule (which lists only four contracts requiring notice or consent).

**Recommended fix:**

Review the Spent Cell Supply Agreement in the data room. Confirm whether it has a change-of-control clause (if so, it should have been listed in Disclosure Schedule § 4.4). Add a sentence to § 6.10(b) or a side letter clarifying that § 6.10(b) supplements and, in the event of conflict, supersedes the Spent Cell Supply Agreement. Confirm the agreement's term extends through the Restricted Period or that § 6.10(b) fills any gap.

## 11. No Specific Representation That Phase I ESAs Are Accurate or That No RECs Were Identified

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.14(f): Seller represents only that it has *"delivered to Buyer copies of all environmental assessments, audits, reports and sampling data relating to any Facility that are in the possession or control of Seller or the Company."*
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.14(f): Lists three Phase I ESAs (November–December 2025) and other reports.
- SPA § 4.14(c): Represents no release of Hazardous Materials *"that requires investigation, remediation or reporting under Environmental Law."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

Section 4.14(f) is a delivery representation only—it does not represent that the Phase I ESAs are accurate, complete, or that no Recognized Environmental Conditions (RECs) were identified. If a Phase I ESA identified a REC (e.g., "further investigation recommended" for soil gas or groundwater), that would not necessarily breach § 4.14(c) unless the REC rises to the level of a release "requiring investigation, remediation or reporting." The line between a REC that "warrants further assessment" and a release "requiring investigation under Environmental Law" is not always clear.

Drapesk has not had the opportunity (based on these files) to confirm whether the Phase I ESAs identified any RECs. I do not have the Phase I reports themselves.

**Recommended fix:**

- **Immediate:** Request and review the three Phase I ESAs from the data room to confirm whether any RECs or "further investigation" recommendations were identified. If so, assess whether they implicate § 4.14(c) and whether additional disclosure should have been made.
- **Contractual:** Add a sentence to § 4.14(f) or a new § 4.14(g) representing that the Phase I ESAs delivered do not identify any RECs requiring a Phase II assessment prior to closing, or that all such RECs are disclosed in the Disclosure Schedule.

## 12. Environmental Consultant Is Engaged by Seller, Creating a Structural Conflict

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Environmental Consultant"): *"Halvorsen Ridge Environmental, LLC, or another nationally recognized environmental consulting firm engaged by Seller and reasonably acceptable to Buyer."*
- SPA § 6.8: *"Seller shall cause the Environmental Consultant to conduct a Phase II..."* and *"Seller shall bear all fees and expenses of the Environmental Consultant."*

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

The Phase II Report is the trigger for Drapesk's environmental claims (per § 9.6). The consultant is engaged, paid, and directed by Seller. While Buyer has "reasonable acceptability" rights over the firm's identity, it has no say over scope, methodology, or reporting once the engagement is made. The scope of work is set in Disclosure Schedule § 6.8, but the consultant's day-to-day direction comes from Seller. If the Phase II findings are unfavorable to Seller, there is a structural incentive for the consultant (as Seller's vendor) to understate findings or delay reporting.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend § 6.8 to provide that (a) the Environmental Consultant reports directly to Buyer as well as Seller, (b) Buyer has the right to attend all fieldwork and review all raw data, (c) the consultant is obligated to report all findings without regard to their impact on either party, and (d) Buyer may engage its own consultant to observe or replicate any portion of the Phase II at Seller's expense if Buyer reasonably believes the scope is being narrowed.

## 13. Escrow Release Timing Does Not Account for Environmental Claim Period

**Document and Section:**
- SPA § 9.7: Escrow released within five Business Days after the six-month post-closing date, less amounts "claimed in any Claim Notice delivered prior to such date and not yet resolved."

**Why it matters to Drapesk:**

As analyzed in Issue 2, no environmental Claim Notice can be delivered before Day 210 (at the earliest). The escrow releases at Day 180 + 5 Business Days ≈ Day 185. The escrow will be fully released to Seller before any environmental claim can be made. Even if a non-environmental claim is pending, the "retained amount" mechanism in § 9.7 only preserves escrow for claims already noticed—environmental claims cannot yet be noticed.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend § 9.7 to provide that the escrow (or a portion thereof, e.g., $5 million) shall be retained until 30 days after delivery of the Phase II Report, or until the expiration of the environmental survival period (whichever is later), before release. This should be negotiated in conjunction with the fix for Issue 2.
