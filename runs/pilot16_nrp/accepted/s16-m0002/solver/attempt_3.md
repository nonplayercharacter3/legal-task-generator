## 1. Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) Is Not a Fundamental Representation in the Operative Deal Documents, Contrary to the Board's Understanding

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Fundamental Representations"): *"the representations and warranties of Seller set forth in the Sections of Article IV listed in Section 1.1(f) of the Disclosure Schedule."*
- Disclosure Schedule, § 1.1(f): Lists only §§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, and 4.5 as Fundamental Representations. **Section 4.14 is absent.**
- Board Consent, § 2(c): States that Fundamental Representations *"include Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) of the SPA, together with Sections 4.1 (Organization), 4.2 (Authority), 4.3 (Capitalization) and 4.5 (Brokers)."*

**Why it matters:**
The SPA's definitional provision incorporates the Disclosure Schedule by reference. The Disclosure Schedule is the operative list. Because § 4.14 is not on that list, environmental representations are **not** Fundamental Representations. The consequences are severe:

- **Cap applies.** Under SPA § 9.4(a), Seller's liability for non-Fundamental Rep breaches is capped at 10% of the Purchase Price ($18.4 million). The Board Consent (§ 2(b)) confirms the cap does not apply to Fundamental Reps—but the Board believed § 4.14 was Fundamental.
- **Basket applies.** Under SPA § 9.4(b), Seller is not liable for non-Fundamental Rep breaches until aggregate losses exceed $920,000.
- **Short survival period.** Under SPA § 9.1, non-Fundamental Reps survive only six (6) months post-Closing, rather than until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations expires.
- **Bring-down standard is lower.** Under SPA § 7.2(a), non-Fundamental Reps need only be true and correct "in all material respects" at Closing, versus "in all respects" for Fundamental Reps.

The Board's approval was premised on environmental reps being Fundamental (Board Consent §§ 1.2, 2(c)). As drafted, the deal documents do not deliver the protection the Board authorized.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend Disclosure Schedule § 1.1(f) to add Section 4.14 to the list of Fundamental Representations. This is a one-line change to the Disclosure Schedule and should be raised with Seller's counsel immediately. If Seller resists, the alternative is to negotiate a separate, longer survival period and a higher or no cap specifically for § 4.14 breaches, but the cleanest fix is to conform the Disclosure Schedule to what the Board approved.

## 2. The Survival Period for § 4.14 Expires Before Drapesk Is Permitted to File an Environmental Claim

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.1: Non-Fundamental Reps survive until six (6) months after the Closing Date. A Claim Notice must be delivered *"prior to the expiration of the applicable survival period."*
- SPA § 6.8: The Phase II Report is due *"no later than 180 days after the Closing Date."*
- SPA § 9.6: *"Buyer shall not deliver a Claim Notice with respect to any inaccuracy in or breach of Section 4.14 until thirty (30) days after Buyer's receipt of the Phase II Report."*
- SPA § 9.7: The Escrow Fund is released within five (5) Business Days after six (6) months post-Closing, less amounts claimed before that date.

**Why it matters:**
Six months is approximately 180 days. The Phase II Report is due at 180 days. The Environmental Review Period adds 30 days after receipt. Therefore:

- If the Phase II Report is delivered on the last permissible day (day 180), Drapesk cannot file a Claim Notice until day 210—but the survival period expired at day 180. **Drapesk has zero days to assert an environmental claim.**
- Even if the Phase II Report arrives early (e.g., day 120), Drapesk would have only 30 days (until day 150) to analyze the report, quantify losses, and prepare a Claim Notice before the survival period expires at day 180. That is an extremely compressed window for a complex environmental matter involving three facilities.
- The escrow (SPA § 9.7) is released on the same 6-month timeline, so the $9.2 million security will be returned to Seller before Drapesk can even file a claim.

This drafting conflict renders the environmental indemnification effectively illusory. It is the most technically damaging issue in the deal documents.

**Recommended fix:**
Two changes are needed (either one would help, but both are preferable):

1. **Extend the survival period for § 4.14.** Add language to SPA § 9.1 providing that Section 4.14 survives until the later of (a) six months after the Closing Date, or (b) thirty (30) days after delivery of the Phase II Report. This aligns the survival period with the Environmental Review Period.
2. **Defer the escrow release for environmental claims.** Amend SPA § 9.7 to provide that the portion of the Escrow Fund allocable to § 4.14 claims is not released until thirty (30) days after delivery of the Phase II Report, or such later date as a Claim Notice is resolved.

If Seller will not agree to either, Drapesk should consider whether the environmental risk is adequately priced into the $184 million Purchase Price given that the indemnity is effectively unavailable.

## 3. Section 9.3(b) Imposes an Uncapped Buyer Indemnity for Pre-Closing Operations

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.3(b): Buyer indemnifies Seller for Losses arising from *"the ownership or operation of the Company or any of the Facilities, whether before or after the Closing."*
- SPA § 9.4(c): The 10% cap on Buyer's indemnification obligation applies to Section 9.3 *"other than with respect to Section 9.3(b)."* Section 9.3(b) is therefore **uncapped**.

**Why it matters:**
This provision is extraordinarily one-sided. It means that if a third party (e.g., a regulator, a neighboring property owner, or a worker) asserts a claim against Seller arising from the Company's pre-closing operations—such as a historical groundwater contamination claim at the Henderson facility—Drapesk must indemnify Seller for the full amount of that claim, with no cap and no basket. Meanwhile, Seller's own liability to Drapesk for the same underlying environmental condition is capped at $18.4 million, subject to a $920,000 basket, and (per Issues 1 and 2 above) may be time-barred before Drapesk can even assert it.

In practical terms, Drapesk could be on the hook for unlimited pre-closing environmental, operational, or employment liabilities of the Company, while its recourse against Seller for the same matters is limited and potentially unavailable.

**Recommended fix:**
Negotiate one or more of the following:
- Delete the words *"whether before or after"* so that § 9.3(b) covers only post-Closing ownership and operation (the standard approach).
- If Seller insists on pre-closing coverage, cap § 9.3(b) at the Indemnity Escrow Amount ($9.2 million) or at the same 10% cap that applies to the rest of § 9.3.
- Carve out from § 9.3(b) any Losses that are also recoverable by Drapesk under § 9.2 (i.e., prevent double-recovery in Seller's favor).

## 4. Asymmetric Assignment Rights Allow Seller to Shed Indemnification Obligations

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 11.5: *"Buyer may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder without the prior written consent of Seller. Seller may assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder to any Person without the consent of Buyer, and upon delivery to Buyer of a written instrument by which the assignee assumes the obligations so assigned, Seller shall thereupon be released from such obligations."*

**Why it matters:**
Seller can assign its indemnification obligations under Article IX to any entity—potentially an empty shell with no assets—and be fully released. Drapesk's recourse above the $9.2 million escrow would then be against an assignee with no ability to pay. There is no requirement that the assignee have a minimum net worth, provide a guaranty, or be subject to any credit standard. Conversely, Drapesk cannot assign its rights (e.g., to an affiliate or in connection with a future restructuring) without Seller's consent.

**Recommended fix:**
Amend § 11.5 to provide that Seller may not assign its obligations under Article IX (indemnification) without Buyer's prior written consent, or that any assignee of such obligations must (a) be an entity with net worth of not less than the Indemnity Escrow Amount and (b) provide a guaranty of its obligations to Buyer. Alternatively, require that Seller remain jointly and severally liable with any assignee for a period of at least two years post-Closing.

## 5. Stock Certificate to Be Delivered Post-Closing by Overnight Courier, Contrary to SPA § 3.2(a)

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 3.2(a): At the Closing, Seller shall deliver *"stock certificates evidencing the Shares, duly endorsed in blank or accompanied by stock powers duly executed in blank."*
- Email Thread, Message 2 (Jonah Reidel, April 14): *"Colette's office has the original certificate. We'll hold the original in escrow with our office pending release and send a PDF beforehand."*
- Email Thread, Message 6 (Elena Marchetti, April 21): *"Following closing: your office sends the original stock certificate and stock power to us by overnight courier."*

**Why it matters:**
The SPA requires delivery of the stock certificate **at** the Closing. The parties have informally agreed via email to deliver a PDF at Closing and the original by overnight courier afterward. This creates a window (potentially 1–2 business days) in which Drapesk has paid $184 million but does not yet possess the physical certificate evidencing its ownership of the Shares. If a dispute arises, if the certificate is lost in transit, or if Seller (or a creditor of Seller) asserts a competing claim, Drapesk's title is temporarily vulnerable. The email agreement does not amend the SPA (which requires a written instrument signed by both parties under § 11.4), so technically Seller would be in breach of § 3.2(a) if it did not deliver the original at Closing.

**Recommended fix:**
Either (a) deliver the original stock certificate and stock power at Closing as the SPA requires, or (b) execute a written amendment to SPA § 3.2(a) reflecting the agreed logistics, with a provision that if the original is not received within two (2) Business Days after Closing, Seller shall deliver a replacement certificate and Drapesk may escrow an equivalent amount from any subsequent payments. Given the remote closing format, option (a) is preferable: arrange for the original to be at Seller's counsel's office and released simultaneously with the wire confirmations.

## 6. No Specific Environmental Closing Condition; Pre-Closing Sampling Restricted

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA Article VII (Conditions to Closing): The conditions are (a) HSR expiration, (b) no restraining order, (c) bring-down of reps, (d) no MAE, (e) Seller deliverables. There is **no condition** specific to environmental due diligence or the Phase I ESAs.
- SPA § 6.2: *"Any invasive sampling prior to the Closing shall require Seller's prior written consent."*
- Board Consent § 1.2: The Board identified environmental matters as the Company's *"principal liability exposure."*

**Why it matters:**
Drapesk is closing a $184 million acquisition of an environmental remediation business without the right to conduct its own invasive sampling before Closing and without a closing condition tied to environmental findings. If a new spill, regulatory action, or contamination discovery occurs between signing (March 9) and closing (April 23), Drapesk's only protection is the general MAE condition (§ 7.2(d)) and the bring-down of § 4.14 (§ 7.2(a)). An MAE is a high bar, and the bring-down is only "in all material respects" (since § 4.14 is not Fundamental—see Issue 1). Drapesk could be forced to close even if a significant new environmental problem emerges in the two weeks between signing and closing.

**Recommended fix:**
Add a condition to SPA § 7.2 that no new release of Hazardous Materials, no new Governmental Order relating to Environmental Law, and no new claim or notice under Environmental Law has occurred since the date of the Agreement that would reasonably be expected to result in Losses exceeding a specified threshold (e.g., $500,000). Additionally, negotiate a right for Drapesk to conduct limited pre-closing sampling at the Henderson facility (the owned, operated plant with the most complex processes) without requiring Seller's consent, subject to a protocol agreed in advance.

## 7. Phase II Environmental Assessment Is Post-Closing with No Pre-Closing Guarantee of Access or Performance

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 6.8: *"Following the Closing, Seller shall cause the Environmental Consultant to conduct a Phase II environmental site assessment of each Facility..."* Seller bears all fees.
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Environmental Consultant"): *"Halvorsen Ridge Environmental, LLC, or another nationally recognized environmental consulting firm engaged by Seller and reasonably acceptable to Buyer."*
- Email Thread, Message 7 (Jonah Reidel, April 21): Halvorsen Ridge has requested site contacts to schedule field work.
- Email Thread, Message 8 (Priyanka Desai, April 21): Site contacts identified (Tolvanen at Henderson; Szymczak at Reno and Chandler).

**Why it matters:**
The Phase II is the mechanism by which Drapesk will learn the actual environmental condition of the facilities. But it occurs entirely post-closing, at Seller's direction ("Seller shall cause"), using a consultant Seller engages. There is no provision for what happens if:
- Seller fails to engage the consultant or delays field work;
- The consultant is not "reasonably acceptable" to Buyer and the parties cannot agree on a replacement;
- Seller contests the scope or findings of the Phase II Report.

The only remedy is a claim for breach of covenant under § 9.2(b), which is subject to the same cap, basket, and survival period as other non-Fundamental breaches. Given that the Phase II Report is the trigger for the Environmental Review Period (§ 9.6), any delay in delivering the report also delays Drapesk's ability to make claims (compounding Issue 2).

**Recommended fix:**
Add a provision to § 6.8 stating that if the Phase II Report is not delivered within 180 days after Closing, (a) the survival period for § 4.14 is extended by the delay, (b) the Escrow Fund release under § 9.7 is correspondingly delayed, and (c) Seller shall be in material breach of a covenant, giving Drapesk the right to terminate or seek specific performance. Also add a mechanism for resolving disputes over the consultant's identity or scope (e.g., if Buyer objects to the consultant, the parties shall select a mutually agreed alternative within 15 days, failing which Buyer may select and Seller shall reimburse the cost differential only if the Buyer-selected consultant is objectively more expensive).

## 8. Ambiguity Between Existing Spent Cell Supply Agreement and Section 6.10(b) Feedstock Covenant

**Documents and Sections:**
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.8, item 10: *"Spent Cell Supply Agreement, dated July 1, 2022, between Seller and the Company, covering manufacturing scrap and spent cells generated by Seller's Affiliates."* (No termination notation, unlike item 9.)
- SPA § 6.10(b): During the Restricted Period, Seller shall deliver to the Company all spent lithium-ion cells, battery packs, and manufacturing scrap generated by Seller or its Affiliates, *"at prices and on terms no less favorable to the Company than those in effect during the twelve (12) months prior to the Closing Date."*

**Why it matters:**
The Spent Cell Supply Agreement (2022) presumably continues post-closing (it is not listed as terminating at Closing, unlike the Intercompany Services Agreement in item 9). Section 6.10(b) imposes a free-standing covenant with its own pricing standard. It is unclear whether:
- The 2022 Agreement is the vehicle through which § 6.10(b) is performed (i.e., the covenant modifies the existing agreement), or
- § 6.10(b) creates a new, parallel obligation that coexists with the 2022 Agreement, potentially on different terms.

If the 2022 Agreement has a term that expires before the five-year Restricted Period, or contains pricing terms that differ from the "no less favorable" standard in § 6.10(b), a dispute could arise. Additionally, the 2022 Agreement is listed as a Material Contract, meaning it is covered by the § 4.8 representation that it is in full force and effect and not in material breach—but Drapesk should confirm its actual terms.

**Recommended fix:**
Request a copy of the Spent Cell Supply Agreement from the data room and confirm its term, pricing mechanism, and volume commitments. If its term expires before the end of the Restricted Period, negotiate an amendment (as a closing deliverable or post-closing covenant) to extend it through the Restricted Period on terms consistent with § 6.10(b). Alternatively, add language to § 6.10(b) clarifying that the covenant is performed through the Spent Cell Supply Agreement as amended to reflect the § 6.10(b) pricing standard.

## 9. Escrow Amount Is Modest Relative to Identified Environmental Risk

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 2.3: Indemnity Escrow Amount = $9,200,000 (5% of Purchase Price).
- SPA § 9.4(a): Cap on Seller's rep/warranty indemnity = 10% of Purchase Price = $18,400,000.
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.14(d): Three facilities with aboveground storage tanks, a lined stormwater basin, and active hydrometallurgical processing.
- Disclosure Schedule, § 4.17: Pollution Legal Liability insurance of $25 million per incident / $25 million aggregate (Meridale Specialty, policy PLL-30-019874, in force through April 1, 2029).

**Why it matters:**
The escrow provides only $9.2 million of secured recovery. The cap allows up to $18.4 million, meaning up to $9.2 million of potential recovery is unsecured and dependent on Seller's post-closing solvency (see Issue 4 regarding assignment). For an environmental business with three operating facilities handling hazardous materials, $9.2 million may be insufficient to cover a single significant contamination event (e.g., a groundwater plume at Henderson requiring multi-year remediation). The $25 million PLL policy provides some backstop, but insurance is subject to coverage disputes, exclusions, and the insurer's creditworthiness. SPA § 9.4(e) provides that Losses are calculated net of insurance proceeds actually received, which could reduce the indemnity recovery if the PLL policy responds.

**Recommended fix:**
While increasing the escrow may be difficult at this stage, consider:
- Confirming with the PLL insurer (Meridale) that the policy will follow the asset post-closing and that no change-in-control provision voids coverage. Request a written confirmation or endorsement as a closing deliverable.
- Negotiating a higher escrow (e.g., $18.4 million, matching the cap) or a staggered release (e.g., 50% at 6 months, 50% at 12 months or after Phase II).
- Requiring Seller to maintain a minimum net worth or provide a parent guaranty for the unsecured portion of the indemnity.

## 10. Narrow Knowledge Qualifier for Threatened Litigation

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.13: No action, suit, claim or proceeding pending or, *"to the Knowledge of Seller,"* threatened.
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Knowledge of Seller"): *"the actual knowledge of the individuals listed in Section 1.1(k) of the Disclosure Schedule, after reasonable inquiry of their direct reports."*
- Disclosure Schedule, § 1.1(k): Five named individuals (Venhagen, Okafor-Pryce, Tolvanen, Szymczak, Abernathy-Ruiz).

**Why it matters:**
The knowledge qualifier is limited to actual knowledge of five specific people, after reasonable inquiry of their *direct reports* only. If a plant-level supervisor or environmental technician is aware of a threatened regulatory action or third-party claim but does not report it up through the direct-report chain to one of the five named individuals, the representation remains technically accurate. For an environmental business with three facilities, operational knowledge can be siloed at the facility level. The "direct reports" limitation means the inquiry does not extend to, e.g., contractors, lab technicians, or subordinates of subordinates.

**Recommended fix:**
Expand the knowledge qualifier to include "after reasonable inquiry of employees at all levels involved in environmental, health, safety, legal, and operational matters at the Facilities," or add the facility-level EHS managers (beyond Szymczak, who is the Director of EHS) to the list in Disclosure Schedule § 1.1(k). At minimum, confirm that Szymczak's direct reports include the on-site EHS personnel at all three facilities.

## 12. SPA § 9.6 Limits Remediation to "Least Stringent Standard" for Industrial Use

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.6: *"Any remediation undertaken in respect of an environmental claim shall be conducted to the least stringent standard permitted under applicable Environmental Law for the continued industrial use of the relevant Facility."*

**Why it matters:**
This provision caps the remediation standard at what is needed for "continued industrial use." If a regulator (e.g., NDEP or ADEQ) requires a higher standard—such as a residential standard, a no-additional-remediation determination, or a specific cleanup level below the industrial threshold—Seller's indemnification obligation would not cover the excess cost. Drapesk would bear the difference. This is particularly relevant for the Henderson facility, which is an owned property in an industrial zone adjacent to developed areas, where a regulator could impose stricter standards.

Conversely, this language could also be read to limit Drapesk's own remediation obligations (i.e., Drapesk need only remediate to the industrial standard), which would be favorable. The provision is ambiguous as to whether it limits only Seller's indemnity or also the scope of remediation that Drapesk must undertake.

**Recommended fix:**
Clarify that § 9.6 limits only the scope of Seller's indemnification obligation and does not limit Drapesk's right to undertake (and recover the cost of) remediation to a higher standard if required by a Governmental Authority or if Drapesk elects to do so. Alternatively, delete the sentence and address the remediation standard in the Phase II scope of work or a separate environmental covenant.
