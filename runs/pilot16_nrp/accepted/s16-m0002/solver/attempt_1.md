## 1. Fundamental Representations Discrepancy: Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) Missing from Disclosure Schedule

**Documents:** SPA § 1.1 ("Fundamental Representations" definition); Disclosure Schedule § 1.1(f); Drapesk Board Written Consent § 2(c).

**Issue.** SPA § 1.1 defines "Fundamental Representations" as those "set forth in the Sections of Article IV listed in Section 1.1(f) of the Disclosure Schedule." The Disclosure Schedule at § 1.1(f) lists only Sections 4.1 (Organization), 4.2 (Authority), 4.3 (Capitalization), and 4.5 (Brokers). Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) is **not** included.

However, the Drapesk Board Written Consent at § 2(c) expressly states that the Fundamental Representations "include Section 4.14 (Environmental Matters) of the SPA, together with Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.5." The board approved the acquisition on that understanding.

**Why it matters.** Being a Fundamental Representation carries three critical consequences under the SPA:

- **No cap.** § 9.4(a) excludes Fundamental Representations from the 10% cap ($18.4 million). As drafted, environmental claims are capped at $18.4 million.
- **No basket.** § 9.4(b) excludes Fundamental Representations from the $920,000 tipping basket. As drafted, Drapesk must absorb the first $920,000 of environmental losses before recovering anything.
- **Longer survival.** § 9.1 provides that Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after expiration of the applicable statute of limitations (potentially 10+ years for environmental claims under CERCLA or state analogues). As drafted, Section 4.14 survives only 6 months post-closing.

Additionally, § 7.2(a) requires Fundamental Representations to be true "in all respects" at closing (vs. "in all material respects" for others), giving Drapesk a stronger walk-away right.

The board consent reflects a different deal than the one documented. This is both a substantive risk-allocation problem and a corporate governance problem: the board did not authorize the transaction as it currently stands.

**Recommendation.**

- Raise immediately with Seller's counsel. The Disclosure Schedule at § 1.1(f) must be amended to add Section 4.14 to the list of Fundamental Representations, consistent with the board's approval.
- If Seller resists, Drapesk should not close without a new board consent reflecting the actual risk allocation, or a specific written waiver from the board acknowledging the change.
- Confirm that the SPA's definition in § 1.1 (which cross-references the Disclosure Schedule) will automatically pick up the corrected list.

## 2. Survival Period / Phase II Timing Gap: Environmental Claims May Be Time-Barred Before They Can Be Filed

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1 (survival), § 6.8 (Phase II due 180 days post-closing), § 9.6 (no environmental Claim Notice until 30 days after Phase II Report receipt).

**Issue.** As currently drafted (with Section 4.14 *not* a Fundamental Representation):

- Survival for Section 4.14: **6 months** after Closing (§ 9.1). If Closing is April 23, 2026, survival expires **October 23, 2026**.
- Phase II Report deadline: **180 days** after Closing (§ 6.8). That is **October 20, 2026**.
- Environmental Review Period: Buyer may not deliver a Claim Notice regarding Section 4.14 until **30 days after receipt** of the Phase II Report (§ 9.6). If the report is delivered on the last permissible day (October 20), the earliest a claim can be filed is **November 19, 2026** — approximately **27 days after the survival period has expired**.

Even if the Phase II is delivered early, any delay in field work (weather, access, lab turnaround) pushes the report closer to the 180-day deadline and compresses or eliminates the window to file.

**Why it matters.** Drapesk could discover significant contamination in the Phase II Report and be contractually barred from indemnification because the survival period lapsed before the claim was permitted to be made. This defeats the entire purpose of the Phase II mechanism.

**Recommendation.**

- If Issue 1 is resolved (Section 4.14 becomes a Fundamental Representation), this gap is cured because Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations expires.
- If Issue 1 is *not* resolved, § 9.1 must be amended to provide a longer survival period for Section 4.14 specifically (e.g., 18 months post-closing, or the later of 6 months or 30 days after delivery of the Phase II Report).
- Alternatively, § 9.6 should be amended to state that the Environmental Review Period does not extend beyond the survival period, or that the survival period for Section 4.14 is tolled during the Environmental Review Period.
- Coordinate with the fix to Issue 1; the two are interdependent.

## 3. Uncapped Buyer Indemnity for Pre-Closing Ownership and Operation

**Documents:** SPA § 9.3(b) (Buyer indemnifies Seller for "the ownership or operation of the Company or any of the Facilities, whether before or after the Closing"); § 9.4(c) (cap exclusion for § 9.3(b)).

**Issue.** Section 9.3(b) requires Drapesk to indemnify Trepal for all Losses arising from "the ownership or operation of the Company or any of the Facilities, **whether before or after the Closing**." Section 9.4(c) provides that the 10% cap on Buyer's indemnity does **not** apply to § 9.3(b). This is an **uncapped** obligation.

In a stock purchase, the Company's pre-closing liabilities (including environmental) generally inure to the Company itself, which Drapesk will own. The unusual feature here is that Drapesk must also reimburse Trepal if Trepal is sued in its capacity as former parent for pre-closing operations. Given that the Company handles hazardous materials (electrolyte, heavy metals, PFOA/PFAS per the SPA's definition of "Hazardous Materials" in § 1.1), a third-party or governmental claim naming Trepal as a parent-level responsible party would trigger this indemnity.

**Why it matters.** This creates an open-ended, uncapped exposure for Drapesk that is atypical and arguably one-sided. It effectively requires Drapesk to protect Trepal from the very environmental liabilities that Drapesk is purchasing the Company to assume.

**Recommendation.**

- Negotiate deletion of "whether before or after the Closing" from § 9.3(b), limiting the indemnity to post-closing operations only (which is what Drapesk would naturally bear as owner).
- Alternatively, if Seller insists on pre-closing coverage, cap § 9.3(b) at the Indemnity Escrow Amount ($9.2 million) or at a stated dollar figure, and require that it be satisfied from the Escrow Fund.
- At minimum, clarify that § 9.3(b) does not apply to Losses that are also recoverable by Drapesk under § 9.2(a) (i.e., no double-dipping by Seller).
- Flag to the board: this term was not specifically addressed in the Board Written Consent's summary of principal terms.

## 4. Existing Spent Cell Supply Agreement Not Reconciled with Section 6.10(b) Feedstock Covenant

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule § 4.8, item 10 (Spent Cell Supply Agreement, dated July 1, 2022, between Seller and the Company); SPA § 6.10(b) (post-closing feedstock covenant).

**Issue.** The Disclosure Schedule identifies an existing "Spent Cell Supply Agreement" between Seller and the Company covering "manufacturing scrap and spent cells generated by Seller's Affiliates." The SPA does not state whether this agreement is terminated, amended, or superseded at Closing. Section 6.10(b) imposes a new, broader covenant requiring Seller to deliver "all spent lithium-ion cells, battery packs and manufacturing scrap generated by Seller or its Affiliates" at "prices and terms no less favorable" than the trailing 12-month average.

**Why it matters.** If the old agreement survives closing with different (potentially less favorable) pricing or volume terms, it could conflict with § 6.10(b). If it is intended to be superseded, the SPA should say so expressly to avoid ambiguity. The old agreement is also a "Material Contract" under § 4.8, meaning Seller has represented it is valid and in full force — but its post-closing status is unclear.

**Recommendation.**

- Confirm with Seller whether the Spent Cell Supply Agreement is to be terminated at Closing (like the Intercompany Services Agreement, which § 4.8 item 9 states "will be terminated at Closing") or whether it continues and is supplemented by § 6.10(b).
- If it terminates, add a closing deliverable: a termination agreement or amendment.
- If it continues, add a proviso to § 6.10(b) stating that it supersedes the Spent Cell Supply Agreement to the extent of any conflict, and that the terms of the Spent Cell Supply Agreement are incorporated by reference for any matters not addressed in § 6.10(b).
- Review the actual Spent Cell Supply Agreement (available in the data room per Disclosure Schedule Preliminary Statement 5) to identify any terms (pricing formula, minimum volumes, exclusivity) that could be inconsistent with § 6.10(b).

## 5. Employee Benefit Plans: Trepal Group Plans Will Lapse at Closing

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule § 4.15, items 2–3 (Trepal Holdings Corp. Group Medical, Dental and Vision Plan; Trepal Holdings Corp. Group Life and Disability Insurance Plan); SPA § 6.9 (12-month salary protection only).

**Issue.** The Company participates in two Trepal-sponsored group benefit plans. Upon Closing, the Company will no longer be a subsidiary of Trepal, and these plans will presumably terminate as to Company employees. Section 6.9 protects base salary and wages for 12 months but is silent on benefits (health, dental, vision, life, disability).

**Why it matters.** Loss of benefits at closing could trigger retention risk for key personnel, including the Knowledge Persons identified in Disclosure Schedule § 1.1(k) (e.g., Ingrid Tolvanen, VP Operations; Rafael Szymczak, Director EHS) who are critical to the Phase II process and post-closing operations. It could also raise COBRA or ERISA compliance questions if the plans are treated as terminated.

**Recommendation.**

- Request that Seller provide a transition benefits arrangement (e.g., 6–12 months of continued coverage at Seller's expense, or a stipend) as a condition to closing or a post-closing covenant.
- Alternatively, confirm that Drapesk will replace these plans effective at Closing and that the cost has been factored into the deal model.
- Confirm with the benefits plans' administrator whether a change of control triggers any acceleration, vesting, or termination provisions.

## 7. Seller's Unilateral Right to Assign Indemnification Obligations

**Documents:** SPA § 11.5 (Successors and Assigns).

**Issue.** Section 11.5 permits Seller to "assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder to any Person without the consent of Buyer," with Seller released upon the assignee's assumption. This means Trepal could assign its indemnification obligations under Article IX to a shell entity or an affiliate with limited assets, leaving Drapesk with a contractually valid but practically uncollectible claim.

**Why it matters.** The entire indemnification structure (escrow, cap, basket, survival) is only as good as Seller's (or its assignee's) ability to pay. A $18.4 million cap is meaningless if the indemnifying party has been stripped of assets.

**Recommendation.**

- Amend § 11.5 to require Buyer's prior written consent for any assignment of Seller's indemnification obligations under Article IX, or to provide that any such assignment does not release Seller unless the assignee is an entity with net worth no less than the Indemnity Escrow Amount.
- Alternatively, add a representation that Seller will not take any action to impair its ability to satisfy indemnification obligations (e.g., asset sales, distributions) for the duration of the survival period.

## 8. Short General Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations

**Documents:** SPA § 9.1.

**Issue.** All non-Fundamental Representations survive only 6 months post-closing. For an environmental business, this is short. Representations such as § 4.12 (Compliance with Laws, looking back to January 1, 2021), § 4.11 (Permits), and § 4.16 (Taxes) may not reveal all issues within 6 months. Regulatory audits, permit condition violations, or tax assessments can take well over 6 months to surface.

**Why it matters.** Drapesk may discover a compliance gap or tax exposure at month 7 or 8 and have no indemnification recourse.

**Recommendation.**

- If the Fundamental Reps fix (Issue 1) is achieved, the most critical environmental representation is covered. However, consider extending the general survival period to 12–18 months, or at minimum carving out § 4.12 (Compliance with Laws) and § 4.16 (Taxes) for a longer period.
- The Tax Matters in Article X (§§ 10.1–10.3) do not have their own survival provision; they are governed by the general 6-month period unless the tax representation in § 4.16 is given a longer tail. Consider a specific tax survival period tied to the statute of limitations.

## 9. Pre-Closing Invasive Sampling Requires Seller Consent

**Documents:** SPA § 6.2 (Access to Information).

**Issue.** Section 6.2 provides that "Any invasive sampling prior to the Closing shall require Seller's prior written consent." The structure of the deal defers definitive environmental verification to the post-closing Phase II (§ 6.8). Drapesk has no unilateral right to conduct soil or groundwater sampling before closing.

**Why it matters.** If Drapesk's due diligence team identifies a specific area of concern (e.g., the stormwater retention basin at Henderson, or the electrolyte drainage areas) and wants to sample before closing to confirm or rule out contamination, it must ask Seller's permission. Seller has no obligation to consent. This limits Drapesk's ability to make a fully informed closing decision.

**Recommendation.**

- Negotiate a right to conduct a limited number of pre-closing sampling events (e.g., up to 3 bore locations per facility) at Drapesk's expense, with Seller's consent not to be unreasonably withheld.
- Alternatively, add a specific closing condition that Drapesk's environmental consultant has completed a limited pre-closing verification sampling at the Henderson Facility (the largest and most complex site).
- If Seller resists, document the risk in the closing memo and ensure the board is aware that pre-closing environmental verification is limited to the Phase I ESAs and the EHS audit already in the data room.

## 10. Section 9.6 Remediation Standard Limitation

**Documents:** SPA § 9.6 (last sentence).

**Issue.** Section 9.6 provides that "Any remediation undertaken in respect of an environmental claim shall be conducted to the least stringent standard permitted under applicable Environmental Law for the continued industrial use of the relevant Facility."

**Why it matters.** While this limits Drapesk's remediation cost exposure (a positive), it could also mean that the site is not remediated to a standard that would support a future change in use, a resale, or a financing transaction. If Drapesk's long-term strategy involves expanding operations or potentially divesting a facility, the "least stringent standard for continued industrial use" may be insufficient. Additionally, if a regulator later determines that a higher standard is required (e.g., due to a change in receptors or new data), Drapesk bears that cost with no recourse against Seller.

**Recommendation.**

- This is a lower-priority item, but flag it to the client. If Drapesk has specific long-term plans for any of the three facilities that might require a higher cleanup standard, negotiate a carve-out or a specific standard for that facility.
- Confirm that the PLL insurance policy (Disclosure Schedule § 4.17, $25 million per incident) would respond to remediation costs above the "least stringent" standard, or that the policy's terms are consistent with this limitation.
