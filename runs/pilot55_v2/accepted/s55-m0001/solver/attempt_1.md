## Summary

I have reviewed all six documents in the matter folder. Below are the issues I identified, ordered by severity. The two most significant are structural: the Parent Guaranty does not cover the entity actually performing the services, and the SOW exclusivity clause locks Zilain's entire group into Geilaind far beyond the scope of the six-vessel arrangement. Both should be resolved before Zilain relies on the deal.

## Issue 1 – Parent Guaranty Does Not Cover the Actual Service Provider

**Where:** Parent Guaranty §1.1 (definition of "Provider"); MSA §1.1 (definition of "Provider"); SOW No. 1 preamble and §2.1.

**The problem:** The MSA defines "Provider" broadly as "Geilaind and any Affiliate of Geilaind that executes an SOW." SOW No. 1 is executed by Geilaind Ship Management (Cyprus) Ltd, an affiliate. But the Parent Guaranty carves out its own definition in §1.1: "Provider means Geilaind Marine Services LLC, a limited liability company, and its successors and permitted assigns." The Cyprus entity is not Geilaind Marine Services LLC and is not its successor or assign. Because the Guaranty defines the term, the MSA's broader definition does not apply (Guaranty §1.2 incorporates only terms "not defined in this Guaranty").

The result: if the Cyprus entity defaults, Zilain has no claim under the Guaranty. The Guaranty is effectively useless for the only SOW currently in force.

**Why it matters:** The entire purpose of the Parent Guaranty (MSA §2.5) is to give Zilain recourse to the creditworthy parent if the operating entity fails. As drafted, that protection does not exist for SOW No. 1.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Guaranty §1.1 to define "Provider" as "Geilaind Marine Services LLC and any Affiliate of Geilaind Marine Services LLC that executes a SOW under the MSA, in each case as the Provider under that SOW." Alternatively, add the Cyprus entity as a named co-obligor or require it to execute a deed of adherence to the Guaranty.

## Issue 2 – Exclusivity Clause Is Extraordinarily Broad

**Where:** SOW No. 1 §§5.1–5.3.

**The problem:** Section 5.2 provides that "During the SOW Term and for 24 months thereafter, Customer shall, and shall cause its Affiliates to, engage Provider exclusively for all ship management, crewing and drydock supervision services for any vessel owned, operated or chartered by Customer or any of its Affiliates, in any trading area."

This means:
- It covers **all** vessels in Zilain's group, not just the six Covered Vessels.
- It covers **all** trading areas, not just the Gulf.
- It extends **24 months beyond** the SOW Term.
- It binds **Affiliates**, which Zilain may not control.
- It covers **all** ship management, crewing and drydock supervision, not just the services in the SOW.

Section 5.3 compounds this by requiring Zilain to notify Provider within 30 days of acquiring or chartering in any vessel and to "negotiate in good faith" an amendment or new SOW. Combined with §5.2, Zilain cannot onboard a new vessel with a different manager without first negotiating with Geilaind.

The initial SOW Term is three years with automatic one-year renewals (SOW §2.2). If Zilain misses the 120-day non-renewal notice, it is locked in for another year, after which the 24-month exclusivity tail begins. In the worst case, Zilain's entire fleet is captive to Geilaind for up to seven years (3 + 1 + 24 months) from the initial term.

**Why it matters:** This is a group-wide, all-vessel, all-trading-area lock-in that goes far beyond the commercial deal for six Gulf feeders. It restricts Zilain's ability to use other managers, to acquire vessels, and to respond to market changes. It also creates leverage for Geilaind in any future fee negotiations.

**Recommended fix:**
- Limit exclusivity to the Covered Vessels under this SOW (and any vessels added by amendment to this SOW).
- Delete the Affiliate obligation or limit it to Affiliates that Zilain directly controls.
- Reduce or eliminate the 24-month post-term tail.
- In §5.3, replace "negotiate in good faith" with a clear mechanism: either Zilain may add the new vessel to this SOW on the stated per-vessel fee, or Zilain is free to engage another provider if the parties cannot agree within 60 days.

## Issue 3 – "Environmental Losses" Defined Too Narrowly

**Where:** Quality Agreement §1.2 (definition); MSA §12.3(c) (carve-out from liability cap).

**The problem:** "Environmental Losses" is defined as "fines, penalties and assessments imposed by a Governmental Authority under MARPOL or OPA 90 arising from a Pollution Incident." This is the only environmental category carved out from the liability cap in MSA §12.3(c).

What is **not** included:
- Third-party claims for pollution damage (e.g., a coastal community sues for beach cleanup, a fisherman sues for lost catch).
- Voluntary or directed cleanup costs that are not imposed as a "fine, penalty or assessment."
- Natural resource damage claims.
- Costs imposed under the Clean Water Act or VGP (the definition references only MARPOL and OPA 90).

These excluded amounts fall within the general liability cap in MSA §12.2 (12 months' fees ≈ $1.86 million for this fleet). A single significant pollution incident generating third-party claims could easily exceed that cap, and Zilain would be stuck.

**Why it matters:** Zilain is the vessel owner. Under OPA 90 and general maritime law, the owner is strictly liable for pollution. Zilain's ability to recover from Geilaind for third-party pollution claims is limited to the cap unless those claims qualify as "Environmental Losses" – which they do not, as defined.

**Recommended fix:** Expand the definition of "Environmental Losses" in Quality Agreement §1.2 to include: (a) all costs of prevention, mitigation, cleanup and remediation; (b) all third-party claims for property damage, economic loss, personal injury or death arising from a Pollution Incident; (c) natural resource damage assessments; and (d) fines, penalties and assessments under any Applicable Law (not just MARPOL and OPA 90). Correspondingly, MSA §12.3(c) already carves out "Environmental Losses" from the cap, so the expanded definition flows through automatically.

## Issue 4 – Package Limitation Clause Undermines the Cap

**Where:** MSA §12.4.

**The problem:** "Nothing in this Agreement deprives Provider of any right to limit liability that is available to a ship manager under any applicable convention or statute."

This allows Provider to invoke statutory limitation of liability (e.g., the Limitation of Liability Act, 46 U.S.C. §30505, or the Limitation of Liability for Certain Claims of the 1957 Brussels Convention if applicable) in addition to, or in place of, the contractual cap in §12.2. In practice, statutory limitation for a ship manager could be significantly lower than 12 months' fees, particularly for a small fleet. The clause also makes it unclear which limit applies: the contractual cap or the statutory limit, whichever is lower.

**Why it matters:** The contractual cap was presumably negotiated as the floor of Provider's liability. Section 12.4 allows Provider to go below that floor by invoking statute. This is one-sided: there is no reciprocal provision allowing Customer to invoke any statutory right to increase Provider's liability.

**Recommended fix:** Delete §12.4 in its entirety. If Provider insists on retaining it, add: "provided that Provider's liability shall in no event be less than the amount set forth in Section 12.2." Alternatively, specify that the contractual cap in §12.2 is the exclusive limitation and supersedes any statutory right to limit.

## Issue 5 – Customer Indemnity Is Overly Broad

**Where:** MSA §11.2.

**The problem:** "Customer shall defend, indemnify and hold harmless Provider…from and against all Losses arising from third-party claims relating to the Covered Vessels or the Services."

The phrase "relating to the Covered Vessels or the Services" is extremely broad. A third-party claim "relating to" a vessel could include claims arising from the vessel's design, its cargo, its commercial employment decisions, or even claims by the vessel's own crew (arguably "relating to" the vessel). The carve-back for Provider negligence, breach or wilful misconduct helps, but it places the burden on Zilain to prove Provider's fault to avoid the indemnity.

Compare with §11.1 (Provider's indemnity), which is limited to claims "caused by the negligence or breach of this Agreement of Provider." The asymmetry – Provider's indemnity requires causation by Provider's fault; Customer's indemnity requires only that the claim "relate to" the vessel or services – favors Provider.

**Why it matters:** In a casualty where fault is shared or unclear, Zilain could be required to indemnify Provider for a portion of a third-party claim that is at least partly attributable to Provider's management decisions.

**Recommended fix:** Narrow §11.2 to: "Customer shall defend, indemnify and hold harmless Provider…from and against all Losses arising from third-party claims to the extent caused by the negligence or breach of this Agreement of Customer or its personnel." This mirrors the causation standard in §11.1 and makes the indemnities reciprocal.

## Issue 6 – Quality Agreement Privity Gap

**Where:** Quality Agreement preamble (parties); MSA §2.2 (SOW structure); SOW No. 1 §11.1 (incorporation).

**The problem:** The Quality Agreement is signed only by Zilain and Geilaind Marine Services LLC. The actual service provider is Geilaind Ship Management (Cyprus) Ltd. The Cyprus entity is bound to comply with the Quality Agreement only indirectly, through the chain: SOW incorporates MSA → MSA §3.1(a) requires Provider to act in accordance with the Quality Agreement.

This indirect incorporation creates a privity problem. If Zilain wants to enforce a specific Quality Agreement obligation (e.g., the 1-hour pollution notification in QA §6.1, or the KPI remediation in QA §7.3) against the Cyprus entity, it must do so through the MSA/SOW chain rather than directly under the Quality Agreement. A court could find that the Cyprus entity is not a party to the Quality Agreement and that Zilain's remedy is only for breach of the MSA (subject to the MSA's liability cap and limitations).

**Why it matters:** The Quality Agreement contains many of the most operationally important protections (notification deadlines, KPIs, audit rights, environmental standards). If enforcement against the Cyprus entity is uncertain, those protections are weakened.

**Recommended fix:** Have the Cyprus entity execute the Quality Agreement as a party (or as a "Provider" with the same obligations as Geilaind Marine Services LLC). Alternatively, add an express provision in SOW No. 1 stating that the Quality Agreement is incorporated into the SOW by reference and that the Cyprus entity assumes all obligations under it as if it were a signatory.

## Issue 7 – Vessel Takeover Protocol Not in Order of Precedence

**Where:** MSA §2.3 (order of precedence); SOW No. 1 §§2.3, 9.2 (references to the Protocol).

**The problem:** MSA §2.3 lists the order of precedence as: (a) MSA; (b) SOW; (c) Quality Agreement. The Vessel Takeover Protocol is not listed. It is referenced in the SOW but does not appear in the hierarchy. If a provision of the Protocol conflicts with the MSA or Quality Agreement, it is unclear which prevails.

For example, Protocol §4.4 says the parties "shall agree in good faith" on pre-takeover rectification items. If they cannot agree, there is no stated mechanism. The MSA's dispute escalation (MSA §18.3) would apply, but that is a 15-Business-Day executive meeting – not ideal for time-sensitive pre-takeover decisions.

**Why it matters:** The Protocol governs the physical transfer of six vessels. Ambiguity about its legal status could create disputes at the most operationally sensitive moment.

**Recommended fix:** Add the Protocol to MSA §2.3 (or SOW §11.1) in the order of precedence, ranked below the SOW but above (or alongside) the Quality Agreement. Add a sentence to Protocol §4.4: "If the Parties cannot agree on the classification of an item within five (5) Business Days, the item shall be treated as a post-takeover rectification item and included in the vessel's operating budget."

## Issue 8 – Liability Cap Applies to the Guaranty

**Where:** Parent Guaranty §2.5; MSA §12.2.

**The problem:** Guaranty §2.5 states: "The Guarantor's liability under this Guaranty shall not exceed the liability Provider would have had under the MSA and the applicable SOW, including any limitation or exclusion of liability under Section 12 of the MSA."

This means the parent guarantor's maximum exposure is the same as the operating entity's capped liability (12 months' fees, excluding the §12.3 carve-outs). For a parent guaranty, this significantly reduces its value. The whole point of a parent guaranty is to provide a credit backstop that exceeds the operating entity's limited assets. If the cap applies, the Guarantor's maximum payment is roughly $1.86 million – the same amount Zilain could potentially recover from the Cyprus entity directly.

**Why it matters:** In a scenario where the Cyprus entity is undercapitalized or insolvent, Zilain would look to the parent. But the parent's maximum liability is the same capped amount. The Guaranty provides no additional financial security beyond what the operating entity itself owes.

**Recommended fix:** Delete the reference to "any limitation or exclusion of liability under Section 12 of the MSA" from Guaranty §2.5. The Guarantor should be liable for the full amount of the Guaranteed Obligations as they would exist without the MSA's limitation provisions. If Geilaind resists, at minimum exclude the §12.1 indirect loss waiver from the Guaranty's limit, so that the parent is on the hook for lost hire and off-hire (Zilain's most likely commercial losses from a management failure).

## Issue 9 – Split Governing Law and Jurisdiction

**Where:** MSA §§18.1–18.2 (Delaware law, Delaware courts); Parent Guaranty §§9.1–9.2 (English law, English courts).

**The problem:** The MSA and SOW are governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in New Castle County, Delaware. The Parent Guaranty is governed by English law with exclusive jurisdiction in the English courts. If Zilain needs to enforce the Guaranty (the scenario where it matters most – Provider insolvency or refusal to pay), it must do so in England under English law, separately from any Delaware proceedings against the operating entity.

This creates:
- Two sets of legal costs (Delaware and English counsel).
- Potential for inconsistent outcomes.
- Delay, as English proceedings may not align with Delaware timing.
- Complexity in coordinating stays or security.

**Why it matters:** In a distress scenario, speed and simplicity matter. Split jurisdiction complicates enforcement and increases cost at the worst time.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate to align the Guaranty's governing law and jurisdiction with the MSA (Delaware). If Geilaind insists on English law for the Guaranty (likely because the parent is an English plc), at minimum add a provision allowing Zilain to bring proceedings in Delaware for the purpose of obtaining interim relief or security, with the English forum remaining exclusive for the merits.

## Issue 10 – KPI Termination Rights Are Too Narrow

**Where:** SOW No. 1 §9.1; Quality Agreement §7.3; MSA §14.3.

**The problem:** SOW §9.1 gives Zilain a specific termination right only for:
(a) failure of KPI No. 3 (Pollution Incidents) or KPI No. 4 (Detentions) on two or more occasions in 12 months; or
(b) failure to implement a remediation plan within the agreed timetable.

There is **no** specific termination right for sustained failure of other KPIs – such as KPI No. 8 (unplanned off-hire > 3 days/vessel/year), KPI No. 9 (crew retention < 80%), or KPI No. 1 (LTIF > 1.0). For those, Zilain's only route is the general material breach provision in MSA §14.3(a), which requires:
- A finding of "material breach" (arguably a KPI miss is not a "breach" of the MSA itself, since KPIs are in the Quality Agreement);
- 30 days to cure; and
- The burden of establishing materiality.

**Why it matters:** If Geilaind's management leads to repeated unplanned off-hire (e.g., 5–6 days per vessel per year, above the 3-day KPI target but not a "Pollution Incident" or "Detention"), Zilain has no clean termination right. It would need to argue that sustained KPI failure constitutes a "material breach" of the MSA – a factual and legal argument that is uncertain.

**Recommended fix:** Expand SOW §9.1 to include: "Provider fails to meet any KPI in Schedule 1 of the Quality Agreement for three (3) consecutive quarters, despite delivery of a remediation plan under Section 7.3 of the Quality Agreement." This gives Zilain a clear exit if performance is chronically below standard, without requiring a "material breach" finding.

## Issue 11 – No Insurance Maintenance Covenants

**Where:** MSA §8.2.

**The problem:** MSA §8.2 requires Provider to maintain E&O insurance of at least $10 million per claim, CGL, and workers' compensation. But it lacks:
- A requirement to maintain the insurance continuously for the full Term (the word "maintain" implies continuity, but there is no express covenant).
- A notice requirement if coverage is cancelled, reduced, or if a claim is made that could exhaust the policy.
- A requirement to name Customer as additional insured or loss payee.
- A minimum aggregate limit (only "per claim" is specified).
- A requirement to maintain coverage at the stated level if claims deplete the policy.

**Why it matters:** If Provider's E&O policy is cancelled or a large claim exhausts it, Zilain may discover this only when it needs to make a claim. The $10 million per-claim limit is helpful, but without a maintenance covenant and notice requirement, Zilain has no early warning.

**Recommended fix:** Add to MSA §8.2: (a) "Provider shall maintain the insurance described in this Section 8.2 continuously throughout the Term and for such period thereafter as any claim may remain unresOLVED"; (b) "Provider shall give Customer at least thirty (30) days' prior written notice of any cancellation, material reduction in coverage, or material change to the policy"; (c) "Provider shall name Customer as an additional insured under the commercial general liability policy"; and (d) "The limits stated in this Section 8.2 are minimums and shall not be reduced by the payment of any claim."

## Issue 12 – Auto-Renewal Combined with Exclusivity Creates a Sticky Trap

**Where:** SOW No. 1 §2.2 (auto-renewal); SOW No. 1 §5.2 (exclusivity tail).

**The problem:** The SOW auto-renews for successive one-year periods unless either party gives 120 days' notice of non-renewal. The exclusivity in §5.2 runs for the "SOW Term" (which includes all renewal periods) plus 24 months. If Zilain is not diligent about calendar management, it could be locked in for multiple renewal years, each extending the exclusivity tail.

The 120-day notice period is long. For a three-year initial term starting March 9, 2026, the first non-renewal notice is due by approximately November 9, 2028. Missing that date means another year, and the exclusivity tail extends accordingly.

**Why it matters:** This is a governance and calendar risk. If Zilain's relationship with Geilaind deteriorates but the non-renewal notice is missed, Zilain is committed for another year plus 24 months of group-wide exclusivity.

**Recommended fix:**
- Reduce the non-renewal notice period from 120 days to 60 or 90 days.
- Add a requirement that Provider give Zilain written reminder notice of the upcoming renewal date at least 150 days in advance.
- Consider making renewal opt-in rather than opt-out (i.e., the SOW expires at the end of the Initial SOW Term unless Zilain affirmatively renews).

## Issue 13 – Force Majeure Does Not Address Crew Obligations

**Where:** MSA §16.1.

**The problem:** The force majeure clause excuses "failure or delay in performing its obligations, other than payment obligations." If a force majeure event (e.g., a pandemic, war, or government action) prevents Provider from performing, it is excused. But the clause does not address:
- Whether Provider must still maintain crew on board (crew are entitled to wages under MLC 2006 regardless of force majeure).
- What happens to the Management Accounts during force majeure (Customer still funds them monthly in advance under MSA §7.3).
- Whether the 60-day termination trigger applies to crewing obligations or only to "performance in respect of a Covered Vessel."

**Why it matters:** In a prolonged force majeure event, Zilain could be funding Management Accounts (including crew wages) while Provider is excused from performing. The 60-day termination right helps, but it requires the event to "prevent performance in respect of a Covered Vessel" – if the vessel is still trading but crew cannot be changed, for example, the trigger may not be met.

**Recommended fix:** Add to MSA §16.1: "Force majeure shall not excuse Provider from its obligations to maintain crew on board a Covered Vessel in compliance with the Maritime Labour Convention 2006, or from its obligation to fund or manage the Management Accounts. If a force majeure event continues for more than thirty (30) consecutive days, Customer may suspend funding of the Management Accounts for the affected vessel until performance resumes, without penalty."

## Issue 14 – Kickoff Meeting Notes Are Non-Contractual

**Where:** Kickoff Meeting Notes, header and §2.2.

**The problem:** The notes explicitly state: "Status: Internal working notes. Not a contractual document." and "Contractual terms remain as set out in the MSA, SOW No. 1 and the Quality Agreement."

Several operational commitments were made in the meeting that are not clearly reflected in the contractual documents:
- Provider's Fleet Manager has "more than fourteen years" of experience (Notes §4.1). SOW §4.1 requires "not less than ten (10) years." The specific individual is not named in the SOW.
- Three superintendents: one in Houston, two from Limassol (Notes §4.2). SOW §4.2 requires "one technical superintendent for every two (2) Covered Vessels" and "at least one…based in Houston." The specific individuals are not identified.
- DPA notification by March 31, 2026 (Notes §4.3, Action Item 4). This is a deadline in the notes only; the contractual requirement is "before the first takeover date" (SOW §4.3).
- Joint oil spill drill within 60 days (Notes §7.3). This is also in Protocol §7.3, so it is covered.

**Why it matters:** If Provider later assigns a less experienced fleet manager or superintendent, Zilain's only contractual protection is the generic "equivalent qualifications" standard in MSA §4.1. The specific commitments made at kickoff are not enforceable.

**Recommended fix:** This is less a drafting issue and more a practical one. I recommend that Zilain's team track the action items in the notes and, for any commitment that is critical (e.g., the named Fleet Manager, the Houston-based superintendent), seek to have the individual's name and qualifications recited in a SOW amendment or side letter. At minimum, the SOW should require that the named Fleet Manager not be replaced without Customer's consent.

## Issue 15 – MSA Section 2.4 Exclusivity Is Overridden, but the Override Is Broader

**Where:** MSA §2.4; SOW No. 1 §§5.1–5.2.

**The problem:** MSA §2.4 provides a narrow exclusivity: "During the term of each SOW, Customer shall engage Provider exclusively for the technical management of the Covered Vessels under that SOW." This is sensible – it means Zilain cannot use another technical manager for the six Gulf feeders while the SOW is in effect, but it can use others for other vessels.

SOW §5.1 expressly modifies MSA §2.4. SOW §5.2 replaces the narrow exclusivity with the group-wide, all-vessel, all-trading-area, 24-month-tail provision discussed in Issue 2. The modification is valid under MSA §2.3 (the SOW "expressly identifies the section of this Agreement that it modifies"). But the result is that the SOW's exclusivity is dramatically broader than the MSA's default.

**Why it matters:** This confirms that the broad exclusivity was a deliberate negotiation point, not a drafting error. It also means that any future SOW that does not expressly modify MSA §2.4 will revert to the narrower MSA standard. Zilain should be aware of this asymmetry when negotiating additional SOWs.

**Recommended fix:** Addressed in Issue 2 above. If the broad exclusivity is retained (which I would advise against), at minimum add a carve-out: "provided that Customer may engage another provider for any vessel that is not a Covered Vessel under this SOW and that is operated outside the U.S. Gulf trading area."

## Issue 16 – No Right to Terminate for Convenience

**Where:** MSA §14 (Term and Termination); SOW No. 1 §9 (Additional Termination Rights).

**The problem:** Zilain's termination rights are:
- MSA §14.3: for cause (material breach with 30-day cure, or insolvency).
- MSA §14.4(a): sale, total loss, CTL or requisition of a vessel.
- MSA §14.4(b): detention > 14 days due to Provider's failure.
- SOW §9.1: KPI failure (Pollution/Detentions only) or remediation plan failure.
- MSA §16.1: force majeure > 60 days.

There is **no** termination for convenience. Once the SOW is in effect, Zilain is committed for the full Initial SOW Term (3 years) plus any renewal periods, unless one of the above triggers is met.

**Why it matters:** If Zilain's strategy changes (e.g., it decides to in-house technical management, or it sells the fleet to a buyer who wants their own manager), it cannot exit the SOW without paying a break cost or waiting for a cause-based trigger. The MSA itself can be terminated under §14.2 (90 days' notice when no SOW is in effect), but that does not help while a SOW is running.

**Recommended fix:** Add a termination-for-convenience right in the SOW: "Customer may terminate this SOW in whole or in respect of one or more Covered Vessels on one hundred eighty (180) days' written notice, subject to payment of Fees accrued through the effective date of termination and a transition fee equal to [X] months' management fee for the affected vessels." This gives Zilain an exit at a known cost.

## Issue 17 – Management Account Threshold Is Low for Single Items

**Where:** SOW No. 1 §7.2; MSA §7.2.

**The problem:** The expenditure threshold is "five percent (5%) of the approved annual budget for the relevant Covered Vessel, or US$50,000 for any single unbudgeted item, whichever is lower."

For a vessel with an annual operating budget of, say, $1.5 million, 5% is $75,000. The "whichever is lower" means the single-item threshold is $50,000. This is quite low for marine operations. A single engine component, a propeller shaft repair, or a set of new life rafts could easily exceed $50,000. Each such item requires Customer's advance approval.

Combined with the emergency expenditure carve-out (MSA §7.2), this creates a practical tension: if an item is "emergency" (necessary to protect life, vessel or environment), no approval is needed. If it is not emergency but exceeds $50,000, approval is needed. The line between "emergency" and "non-emergency" for a $60,000 repair that could become an emergency if delayed is unclear.

**Why it matters:** In practice, this will generate a high volume of approval requests, slowing response times and creating friction. It also gives Zilain's approval function a bottleneck role that may not be resourced adequately.

**Recommended fix:** Increase the single-item threshold to $100,000 or 10% of the annual budget, whichever is lower. Add a sentence: "If Customer does not respond to a request for approval within five (5) Business Days, the expenditure shall be deemed approved." This prevents delays from stalling necessary work.

## Issue 18 – No Service Level for Superintendent Response Times

**Where:** SOW No. 1 §4.2; Quality Agreement (no specific provision); MSA §3.4 (reporting only).

**The problem:** The SOW requires one superintendent per two vessels, with at least one based in Houston. But there is no specified response time for a superintendent to attend to a technical issue, a casualty, or a port state control deficiency. The Quality Agreement requires notification deadlines (QA §6.1) and deficiency close-out times (QA §3.4: 30 days where no time is set by the authority), but does not require a superintendent to be physically present within a specified time.

For a Gulf Coast feeder fleet, response time matters. If a vessel has a main engine failure at sea, the difference between a superintendent arriving in 6 hours versus 24 hours can mean the difference between a minor delay and a major off-hire event.

**Why it matters:** KPI No. 8 (unplanned off-hire ≤ 3 days/vessel/year) is the metric that captures the financial impact of slow response. But there is no operational standard that drives the KPI. Zilain has no contractual lever to require faster response short of arguing that slow response constitutes a KPI failure (which requires two consecutive quarters) or a material breach.

**Recommended fix:** Add to SOW §4.2 or the Quality Agreement: "The assigned superintendent shall respond to any technical issue or casualty affecting a Covered Vessel by remote instruction within four (4) hours and shall be physically present at the vessel or at the nearest port within twenty-four (24) hours, except where the nature of the issue does not require physical presence."

## Issue 19 – MSA Section 14.2 Allows Termination Only When No SOW Is in Effect

**Where:** MSA §14.2.

**The problem:** "Either Party may terminate this Agreement on ninety (90) days' written notice at any time when no SOW is in effect." This means neither party can terminate the MSA while any SOW is running. If SOW No. 1 is in effect (which it will be for at least 3 years), the MSA is locked in.

This is not necessarily a problem for Zilain (it wants the MSA in effect while the SOW is running), but it means that if Zilain wants to renegotiate the MSA terms (e.g., to fix the issues identified in this memo) while SOW No. 1 is in effect, it cannot terminate and restart. It must amend under MSA §19.3.

**Why it matters:** This is a minor point, but it means the MSA issues (liability cap, indemnity, package limitation) cannot be fixed by termination and re-papering. They must be addressed by amendment while the SOW is running, which gives Geilaind leverage.

**Recommended fix:** No change needed to the clause itself. The practical takeaway is that the issues in this memo should be resolved by amendment before the SOW term begins (i.e., before the first takeover date of April 1, 2026), when Zilain has the most leverage.

## Issue 20 – No Requirement for Provider to Maintain Financial Viability

**Where:** MSA (no provision); Parent Guaranty §5.1 (financial statements only).

**The problem:** There is no covenant requiring Provider (the Cyprus entity) to maintain a minimum net worth, liquidity ratio, or capitalization level. The only financial visibility Zilain has is:
- The Parent Guaranty §5.1: annual audited financial statements of the **Guarantor** (Geilaind Holdings plc), not the Provider.
- MSA §6.6: audit right over Fee and Management Account records (once per year).

If the Cyprus entity becomes financially distressed, Zilain would not know until it tried to enforce the Guaranty (which, per Issue 1, may not cover the Cyprus entity in any event).

**Why it matters:** A ship management company that is undercapitalized may cut corners on maintenance, crew quality, or safety compliance – exactly the risks Zilain is trying to manage through the Quality Agreement.

**Recommended fix:** Add to the SOW: "Provider shall maintain at all times during the SOW Term a minimum working capital of US$[X] and shall provide Customer with quarterly unaudited financial statements showing its working capital and liquidity position." Alternatively, require the Guarantor to provide a solvency certificate annually.
