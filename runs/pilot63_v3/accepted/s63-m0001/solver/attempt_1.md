## Issues Requiring Immediate Attention

### 1. Kestrune Avionics Autopilot License – No Closing Condition

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 ("Required Consents"); SPA § 7.2(d); Disclosure Schedule § 4.6, Item 2; SPA § 4.4

**Issue:** The Kestrune Avionics GmbH Autopilot Firmware License (dated February 8, 2022) provides the flight-control autopilot firmware installed on **all** Company drone models. Section 12.3 of that agreement permits Kestrune to terminate the license upon any change of control of the Company effected without Kestrune's prior written consent. The Disclosure Schedule flags this contract with an asterisk, confirming that consent is required. However, the defined term "Required Consents" in SPA § 1.1 covers **only** the Hangar Lease landlord consent. The Kestrune consent is not a closing condition under § 7.2(d), and § 4.4 (which addresses governmental consents) is inapplicable because Kestrune is a private party.

**Why it matters:** If Kestrune terminates the license after closing, the Company's entire product line loses its flight-control software. Re-engineering an alternative autopilot or negotiating a new license could take years and would fundamentally undermine the business Bisart is acquiring for $42 million plus up to $6 million in earn-out. Because this is not a closing condition, Bisart is obligated to close even if Kestrune has signaled it will not consent.

**Recommendation:** Before going back to the other side, we should (a) add the Kestrune consent to the definition of "Required Consents" so that § 7.2(d) makes it a condition to Buyer's closing obligation; (b) alternatively, if Seller resists, require delivery of a written consent or waiver from Kestrune as a Seller closing deliverable under § 3.2; and (c) in any event, confirm the status of discussions with Kestrune before closing. If Kestrune consent cannot be obtained, this should be treated as a deal-breaking issue.

### 2. Uncapped Buyer Indemnity for Seller Guarantees

**Where:** SPA § 9.3(c); SPA § 9.5(b); SPA § 6.8; Disclosure Schedule § 4.16

**Issue:** Section 9.3(c) requires Buyer to indemnify Seller for "any Seller Guarantee, including any payment, performance or other obligation of Seller or its Affiliates under any Seller Guarantee, whether arising before or after the Closing." The Buyer cap in § 9.5(b) applies only to "claims under Section 9.3(a)" (breach of Buyer's representations). It does not extend to § 9.3(b) (covenant breaches) or § 9.3(c) (Seller Guarantee indemnity). As a result, Buyer's indemnity obligation for the Seller Guarantee is **uncapped**.

The Seller Guarantee covers the Company's obligations under the Fleet Financing Agreement, which had an outstanding principal balance of $7,315,400 as of January 31, 2026 (Disclosure Schedule § 4.16, Item 1). Although § 2.2(b) provides that Buyer will pay off the Indebtedness at closing, the guarantee may cover pre-closing defaults, late fees, or other obligations that surface after closing. Additionally, § 6.1(c) permits the Company to draw up to an additional $500,000 under the Fleet Financing Agreement between signing and closing, increasing the exposure.

**Why it matters:** If Fallowgate asserts a pre-closing default (e.g., a technical covenant breach, a late payment, or a cross-default) and calls the guarantee after closing, Buyer must indemnify Seller for the full amount with no cap. Given the $7.3+ million debt balance, this is a material uncapped exposure.

**Recommendation:** Negotiate to (a) include § 9.3(c) within the scope of the Buyer cap in § 9.5(b), or at minimum cap it at the outstanding principal balance of the Fleet Financing Agreement as of the Closing Date; (b) require Seller to deliver a payoff letter and full release of the guarantee as a condition to closing (currently only "customary Lien releases" are required under § 3.2(f)); and (c) confirm with Fallowgate that the guarantee will be released upon payoff.

### 3. Short Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations

**Where:** SPA § 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 1.1 ("Escrow Release Date"); Escrow Agreement § 5.1

**Issue:** Non-fundamental representations and warranties survive only "until 11:59 p.m. New York time on the Escrow Release Date" (SPA § 9.1). The Escrow Release Date is defined as twelve months after the date of the Escrow Agreement (Escrow Agreement § 1.1). The Escrow Agreement is dated March 2, 2026, making the Escrow Release Date **March 2, 2027**. The Closing is scheduled for **September 15, 2026**. This gives Buyer only approximately **five and a half months** after closing to discover and assert claims for breaches of non-fundamental representations (including IP, Material Contracts, Environmental, Insurance, Product Liability, and Taxes).

**Why it matters:** Five and a half months is an unusually short period for a company with complex software IP, FAA regulatory compliance, environmental obligations (pesticide storage, battery handling), and product liability exposure. Many issues (e.g., latent software defects, environmental contamination, unresolved product claims, tax audits) will not surface within that window. After March 2, 2027, Buyer's only recourse for non-fundamental rep breaches is gone (subject to the narrow exception for claims asserted in good faith before expiration).

**Recommendation:** Negotiate to extend the survival period for non-fundamental representations to at least 18–24 months post-closing. If Seller insists on tying survival to the Escrow Release Date, negotiate to extend the Escrow Release Date to 18 or 24 months after the **Closing Date** (rather than 12 months after the Escrow Agreement date). At minimum, carve out the IP representations (§ 4.7), Environmental (§ 4.13), and Tax (§ 4.12/renumbered § 4.13) representations for a longer survival period aligned with applicable statutes of limitation.

### 4. Asymmetric Assignment Rights

**Where:** SPA § 11.4

**Issue:** Section 11.4 provides that "Buyer may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder, by operation of law or otherwise, without the prior written consent of Seller." Conversely, "Seller may assign this Agreement or any of its rights hereunder, in whole or in part, without the consent of Buyer."

**Why it matters:** This asymmetry is unusual and adverse to Buyer. If Bisart undergoes a merger, reorganization, or sale of assets, it could lose its rights under the SPA (including indemnification rights and earn-out set-off rights) without its consent. Meanwhile, Seller can assign its indemnification obligations (or its earn-out rights) to a third party that may be less creditworthy or harder to reach, without Buyer's approval.

**Recommendation:** Make the assignment provision mutual: neither party may assign without the other's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld), or at minimum require Buyer's consent for any assignment by Seller of its indemnification obligations or earn-out rights. If Seller resists, limit Seller's free assignment to assignments to Affiliates only.

## Significant Issues

### 5. Broad Earn-Out Information and Access Rights

**Where:** SPA § 2.5(d); compare spa_prior_draft.md § 2.5(d) (page 3)

**Issue:** The final SPA grants Seller the right, during the entire Earn-Out Period (fiscal year 2027) and until the earn-out is finally determined, to receive quarterly reports of Net Revenue "broken down by customer, by acreage serviced and by product and service line, with the applicable pricing," and to have "reasonable access... to the books, records, customer lists, acreage and application logs, pricing schedules and personnel of the Company relevant to the calculation of Net Revenue" (§ 2.5(d)).

The prior draft (page 3) contained a drafting note: *"[Note to Seller: Buyer is considering Seller's request for customer-level reporting and audit rights and will respond in the next turn.]"* The final agreement granted the full request without apparent modification.

**Why it matters:** This gives Seller (and its accountants and representatives) detailed visibility into the Company's customer relationships, pricing, acreage data, and operations for at least 15 months post-closing (through the end of fiscal 2027 plus 75 days for the earn-out statement). Even though § 6.6 imposes post-closing confidentiality on Seller, and § 6.7 imposes a four-year non-compete, the practical risk is that Seller's personnel will absorb competitive intelligence about Bisart's agricultural drone business. After the non-compete expires in 2030, this information could be deployed. The access to "personnel" also creates integration friction and potential employee unease.

**Recommendation:** Narrow § 2.5(d)(i) to require reporting by product and service line only (not by individual customer or acreage), and limit § 2.5(d)(ii) access to financial books and records necessary to verify Net Revenue calculations—excluding customer lists, pricing schedules, and personnel access. If Seller insists on broader rights, limit access to Seller's outside accountants only (not "representatives") and require all accessed personnel to be bound by written confidentiality acknowledgments.

### 6. Buyer's Covenant Breach Indemnity Potentially Uncapped

**Where:** SPA § 9.3(b); SPA § 9.5(b)

**Issue:** Section 9.5(b) caps Buyer's liability for "claims under Section 9.3(a)" at $4,200,000. Section 9.3(b) (Buyer's indemnity for breach of its own covenants) is not included in this cap. Buyer's post-closing covenants include the broad earn-out operational covenant (§ 2.5(g)), the Seller Guarantee release efforts (§ 6.8), the D&O indemnification preservation (§ 6.13), the employee compensation protections (Article X), and the further assurances obligation (§ 6.12). A breach of any of these is potentially uncapped.

**Why it matters:** The earn-out covenant in § 2.5(g) prohibits actions taken with the "primary purpose" of avoiding or reducing the earn-out. Given the broad information rights in § 2.5(d), Seller will have visibility into business decisions and could argue that ordinary-course integration decisions (e.g., reallocating sales resources, changing pricing, consolidating product lines) were made with a "primary purpose" of reducing Net Revenue. An uncapped indemnity for a § 9.3(b) covenant breach creates significant litigation risk.

**Recommendation:** Extend the § 9.5(b) cap to cover all of Section 9.3, or at minimum include § 9.3(b). If Seller resists, add a clarification to § 2.5(g) that ordinary-course business decisions made in good faith and in the best interests of the Company shall not be deemed to have the "primary purpose" of reducing the earn-out.

### 7. Side Letter FAA Representation – Timing Gap

**Where:** side_letter.md § 2.3; SPA Article IV lead-in

**Issue:** The original Article IV provides that Seller's representations are made "as of the date of this Agreement and as of the Closing Date." The Side Letter (§ 2.3) modifies this for the new FAA representation (Section 4.12): Seller makes it "as of the date of this Letter Agreement [June 22, 2026] and as of the Closing Date." This creates a **three-month gap** (March 2 – June 22, 2026) during which the FAA authorizations are not covered by any representation. If an FAA authorization was suspended, revoked, or allowed to lapse during that period, Seller would not be in breach of the representation.

The covenant in Side Letter § 6.14(a) requires maintenance of FAA authorizations from the date of the Letter Agreement forward, but does not look back to the SPA signing date.

**Why it matters:** The Section 44807 Exemption expires July 31, 2027 (Annex 1, Part A, Item 2). A petition for extension was filed May 28, 2026 (Annex 1, Part B, Item 1). If the FAA denied or adverse-modified the exemption between March 2 and June 22, Seller would not have breached any representation, and Buyer would have no indemnification claim.

**Recommendation:** Amend Side Letter § 2.3 to provide that the new Section 4.12 representation is made as of the date of the **original Agreement** (March 2, 2026) and as of the Closing Date, consistent with the rest of Article IV. Alternatively, add a specific representation that no FAA authorization has been suspended, revoked, or adversely modified between March 2, 2026 and the date of the Letter Agreement.

### 8. Tax Ownership of Escrow Earnings

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 6.1, § 6.2

**Issue:** Buyer is treated as the tax owner of the Escrow Fund and must report all income earned on it (§ 6.1). The tax distribution mechanism in § 6.2 provides that, upon Buyer's written request, the Escrow Agent will disburse 40% of quarterly earnings to Buyer to fund its tax liability. However, this distribution "may not reduce the Escrow Fund below the Escrow Amount," and it requires Buyer to affirmatively request it each quarter.

**Why it matters:** The Escrow Fund is invested in a money market fund (Schedule 2). While current yields are modest, Buyer bears the tax on earnings that economically belong to Seller (to the extent the escrow is ultimately released to Seller). If Buyer fails to make the quarterly request, it will owe tax on income it never receives. Additionally, the 40% distribution may not fully cover Buyer's tax liability depending on its marginal rate.

**Recommendation:** Add language requiring the Escrow Agent to automatically make the § 6.2 tax distribution to Buyer each quarter without the need for a written request, or require Seller to reimburse Buyer for any tax paid on escrow earnings ultimately attributable to Seller. At minimum, establish an internal process to ensure the quarterly request is made.

### 9. Employee Compensation Lock for 12 Months Post-Closing

**Where:** SPA § 10.1

**Issue:** For 12 months after closing, Buyer must provide each Continuing Employee with (a) base salary "no less favorable than that in effect immediately before the Closing" and (b) benefits "substantially comparable in the aggregate to those provided to similarly situated employees of Buyer."

**Why it matters:** This constrains Bisart's ability to harmonize compensation during integration. If Furrowline employees are overpaid relative to Bisart's comparable roles, Bisart cannot reduce their compensation for a year. Conversely, the "substantially comparable" benefits standard could require Bisart to upgrade Furrowline benefits to match its own (potentially more generous) programs, increasing cost.

**Recommendation:** This is a common provision and likely negotiable only in limited respects. Consider adding a carve-out for employees whose positions are eliminated as part of a good-faith integration plan (with severance in lieu), and clarifying that "substantially comparable" means comparable in value, not identical in structure, to avoid being locked into specific benefit designs.

## Moderate Issues

### 10. Open-Source Software – Tension Between SPA Representation and Bill of Sale

**Where:** SPA § 4.7(e); Bill of Sale, Annex A § 4

**Issue:** SPA § 4.7(e) represents that "The Company Software does not contain any open-source software licensed under terms that require the disclosure or licensing of any source code of the Company Software." However, Bill of Sale Annex A § 4 states: "The Company Software includes third-party and open-source components incorporated under their applicable licenses."

**Why it matters:** The Bill of Sale acknowledgment that open-source components exist does not itself contradict § 4.7(e) (which only excludes copyleft/open-source that triggers source-code disclosure). But the tension warrants diligence confirmation. If any open-source component in the Company Software is licensed under GPL, AGPL, or similar copyleft terms, the § 4.7(e) representation is breached. Given the short survival period (Issue 3 above), this claim may expire before it can be asserted.

**Recommendation:** Confirm through technical diligence (open-source scanning) that no copyleft-licensed components are present in the Company Software before closing. If any are found, require Seller to identify them in the Disclosure Schedule and assess the impact on the IP representation.

### 11. Knowledge Transfer Limitations in Bill of Sale

**Where:** Bill of Sale § 3.4

**Issue:** Seller's knowledge transfer obligation is limited to 90 days post-closing and 40 hours per individual. The Company Software comprises a telemetry dashboard (web and mobile), ground-station firmware (two form factors), payload calibration tools, dealer diagnostic tools, and build/release automation (Bill of Sale Annex A). This is a substantial software platform.

**Why it matters:** Forty hours per engineer over 90 days may be insufficient for a full transfer of institutional knowledge about build processes, deployment architecture, and known issues. If key Seller-affiliate engineers are unavailable or uncooperative, the Company could face operational gaps in maintaining and evolving the software.

**Recommendation:** Increase the knowledge transfer period to 120 days and the hourly cap to 60 hours per individual, or remove the hourly cap for the first 30 days. Add a requirement that Seller identify the specific individuals with material knowledge before closing so Buyer can plan for their availability.

### 12. Pre-Closing Draws Under Fleet Financing Agreement

**Where:** SPA § 6.1(c); Disclosure Schedule § 4.16

**Issue:** Section 6.1(c) permits the Company to draw up to an additional $500,000 under the Fleet Financing Agreement between signing and closing "in the ordinary course of business." The outstanding balance was $7,315,400 as of January 31, 2026. The payoff amount at closing (which Buyer funds under § 2.2(b)) will reflect any additional draws.

**Why it matters:** While the additional draws increase the payoff amount (reducing the net cash to Seller under § 2.2(d)), they also increase the Company's debt load at closing. More importantly, if the draws are for equipment that is not yet delivered or is defective, Buyer inherits the obligation without the corresponding asset benefit.

**Recommendation:** Require Seller to provide a schedule of all draws made under the Fleet Financing Agreement after the most recent financial statement date (January 31, 2026), with supporting documentation for each draw, as part of the pre-closing information rights under § 6.2. Confirm that the payoff letter reflects the actual balance at closing.

### 13. D&O Indemnification Tail

**Where:** SPA § 6.13

**Issue:** For six years post-closing, Buyer must cause the Company not to amend D&O exculpation or indemnification provisions in any manner that would adversely affect pre-closing directors and officers.

**Why it matters:** This is a long-tail cost obligation. The Company will need to maintain D&O insurance or reserve funds to cover potential claims by former directors and officers for up to six years. The Board Consent (§ 8.3) authorizes adding Furrowline to Bisart's D&O program, but the six-year tail for pre-closing individuals may require a separate run-off policy.

**Recommendation:** Confirm the cost of a six-year D&O tail policy for the Company's pre-closing directors and officers and ensure it is reflected in the transaction model. Consider whether the cost should be a Transaction Expense (payable by Seller) or borne by the Company (effectively by Buyer).

### 14. Remote ID Retrofit as De Facto Closing Condition

**Where:** Side Letter § 6.14(d); SPA § 7.2(b)

**Issue:** Side Letter § 6.14(d) requires Seller to complete Remote ID broadcast module installation on 14 FL-S20 aircraft before the Closing, with delivery of an officer's certificate confirming completion. Because § 7.2(b) makes Seller's performance of all pre-closing covenants (including those added by the Side Letter) a condition to Buyer's closing obligation, failure to complete the retrofit gives Buyer the right to walk.

**Why it matters:** While this protects Buyer (it's a condition in Buyer's favor), the practical risk is delay. If the retrofit modules are on backorder or the 14 aircraft are in customer hands (leased fleet), completion by September 15, 2026 could be at risk, potentially delaying closing.

**Recommendation:** Confirm the status of the retrofit modules and the availability of the 14 aircraft well before the Outside Date (December 15, 2026). If there is any risk of delay, discuss with Seller whether a partial completion (with a post-closing covenant to finish) would be acceptable, or whether the Outside Date should be extended.

### 15. Board Consent – Broad Delegation to Authorized Officers

**Where:** bisart_board_consent.md § 3.2(b)–(c); § 7.1–7.2

**Issue:** The Board Consent authorizes the CEO, CFO, and General Counsel (each an "Authorized Officer") to approve changes to the transaction documents that they determine, "after consultation with Counsel," are "not materially adverse to the Corporation." Execution of a document is "conclusive evidence" of that determination (§ 3.2(c)). The only changes requiring further Board approval are those that increase the Base Purchase Price, increase the Maximum Earn-Out Payment, or reduce the Escrow Amount (§ 3.2(d)).

**Why it matters:** The uncapped indemnity in § 9.3(c) (Issue 2), the short survival period (Issue 3), and the asymmetric assignment right (Issue 4) could each be characterized as "not materially adverse" by an Authorized Officer, even though they represent significant financial and legal risk. The "conclusive evidence" language in § 3.2(c) makes it very difficult to challenge the officer's determination after the fact.

**Recommendation:** This is an internal governance issue rather than a negotiation point with Seller. Ensure that the issues identified in this memo are escalated to the Committee before any further amendments are signed. Consider whether the Board should add specific items to the § 3.2(d) list (e.g., any increase in Buyer's indemnification exposure, any reduction in survival periods, any assignment right changes).
