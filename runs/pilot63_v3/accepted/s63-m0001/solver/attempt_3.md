### 1. Kestrune Avionics Autopilot License – No Consent Mechanism or Closing Condition

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 ("Required Consents"); SPA § 4.4; Disclosure Schedule § 4.6, item 2; Bill of Sale, Annex A ¶ 4; SPA § 7.2(d); SPA § 3.2(e); SPA § 6.4(b)

**Issue:** The Disclosure Schedule identifies the Kestrune Avionics GmbH Autopilot Firmware License Agreement (dated February 8, 2022) as a Material Contract. Section 12.3 of that license permits Kestrune to terminate upon any change of control of the Company effected without Kestrune's prior written consent. The Bill of Sale expressly excludes this firmware from the Company Software being conveyed (Annex A, ¶ 4), confirming the Company operates it under license only.

However, the defined term "Required Consents" in SPA § 1.1 captures *only* the Hangar Lease landlord consent. The Kestrune consent is not a Required Consent, is not a closing condition under § 7.2(d), is not a Seller deliverable under § 3.2(e), and is not subject to the efforts covenant in § 6.4(b). Seller has no contractual obligation to obtain it, and Buyer has no walk-right if it is not obtained.

**Why it matters:** The Kestrune license covers "the flight-control autopilot firmware installed on all Company drone models" (Disclosure Schedule § 4.6, item 2). Without it, the Company cannot operate its drones. A post-closing termination by Kestrune would effectively destroy the core operating capability of the acquired business. At a $42 million purchase price, this is an existential risk.

**Recommendation:**
- Add the Kestrune consent to the definition of "Required Consents" in § 1.1, making it a closing condition under § 7.2(d) and a Seller deliverable under § 3.2(e).
- Alternatively, if Seller resists making it a condition, add a specific covenant requiring Seller to obtain Kestrune's written consent (or a waiver of the termination right) before Closing, and make failure to do so a breach that triggers Buyer's termination right under § 8.1(b).
- Request a copy of the Kestrune license in its entirety to confirm the scope of the termination right and whether there are any cure periods or notice requirements.

### 2. Fundamental Representations Definition Not Conformed After Side Letter Renumbering

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 ("Fundamental Representations"); Side Letter § 2.1 (insertion of new § 4.12); Side Letter § 2.2 (renumbering)

**Issue:** SPA § 1.1 defines "Fundamental Representations" as "the representations and warranties of Seller set forth in Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.12." At signing (March 2, 2026), Section 4.12 was the Tax representation. The Side Letter (June 22, 2026) inserts a *new* Section 4.12 (Remote Pilot and Airspace Compliance) and renumbers the old Sections 4.12–4.18 to 4.13–4.19. The Side Letter does not amend the "Fundamental Representations" definition in § 1.1.

After the Side Letter takes effect:
- "Section 4.12" now refers to the new FAA compliance representation → it becomes a Fundamental Representation (enhanced survival, excluded from cap and basket).
- The Tax representation is now "Section 4.13" → it is **no longer** a Fundamental Representation.

**Why it matters:** Loss of Fundamental status for the Tax representation means:
- Tax claims are now subject to the $420,000 tipping basket (§ 9.5(a)(i));
- Tax claims are now subject to the $4,200,000 cap (§ 9.5(a)(ii)); and
- Tax claims survive only until the Escrow Release Date (March 2, 2027) rather than 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations (§ 9.1).

For a company with complex sales/use tax obligations across multiple states (see old § 4.12(f) on agricultural-use exemptions), this materially reduces Buyer's protection.

**Recommendation:**
- Amend SPA § 1.1 by side letter or clean-up to restore "Section 4.13" (Taxes) to the Fundamental Representations definition, or to add both "Section 4.12" (FAA) and "Section 4.13" (Taxes).
- Confirm with the deal team whether the FAA representation was intended to be Fundamental. If so, the current (accidental) result is correct for that rep but the Tax rep must be restored.

### 3. Asymmetric Assignment Clause

**Where:** SPA § 11.4

**Issue:** "Buyer may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations hereunder, by operation of law or otherwise, without the prior written consent of Seller… **Seller may assign this Agreement or any of its rights hereunder, in whole or in part, without the consent of Buyer.**"

**Why it matters:** Seller can unilaterally assign:
- Its right to receive the Earn-Out Payment (up to $6M) to any third party;
- Its indemnification obligations to an affiliate or special purpose vehicle with no assets;
- The entire agreement, including its covenants (non-compete, non-solicit, confidentiality).

If Seller assigns its indemnification obligations to an entity with no substance, Buyer's recovery above the escrow (i.e., for Fundamental Representation claims or fraud) could be impaired. The non-compete in § 6.7 binds "Seller" and its "controlled Affiliates" – if Seller assigns to an uncontrolled third party, the covenant may not follow.

**Recommendation:**
- Delete the second sentence or make assignment mutual: neither party may assign without the other's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld).
- If Seller insists on assignment freedom for its earn-out right, carve that out specifically but require that Seller's indemnification and covenant obligations remain non-assignable without Buyer's consent.
- Add a provision that any assignment by Seller does not relieve it of its obligations under the Agreement.

### 4. Extremely Short Survival Period for Non-Fundamental Representations

**Where:** SPA § 9.1; Escrow Agreement § 1.1 ("Escrow Release Date"); SPA § 3.1 (Closing Date)

**Issue:** SPA § 9.1 provides that all non-Fundamental representations survive "until 11:59 p.m. New York time on the Escrow Release Date." The Escrow Agreement § 1.1 defines the Escrow Release Date as "twelve (12) months after the date of this Escrow Agreement." The Escrow Agreement is dated March 2, 2026. Therefore, the Escrow Release Date is **March 2, 2027**.

The scheduled Closing is **September 15, 2026**. This gives Buyer approximately **5.5 months** post-Closing to discover and assert claims for breaches of:
- Intellectual Property (§ 4.7)
- Material Contracts (§ 4.6)
- Employees and Benefit Plans (§ 4.11)
- Environmental Matters (§ 4.13/4.14)
- Insurance (§ 4.14/4.15)
- Product Warranty and Liability (§ 4.15/4.16)
- Indebtedness (§ 4.16/4.17)
- Brokers (§ 4.17/4.18)
- Affiliate Transactions (§ 4.18/4.19)
- Financial Statements (§ 4.5)
- Compliance with Laws (§ 4.10)

**Why it matters:** Five and a half months is well below the market standard of 12–18 months for general representations in a transaction of this size. Many issues (e.g., latent IP defects, undisclosed contract breaches, environmental conditions, employee classification problems) may not surface within that window. Once the Escrow Release Date passes, the escrow is disbursed to Seller (§ 5.1 of the Escrow Agreement) and the survival period expires – Buyer's remedy is extinguished.

**Recommendation:**
- Amend the Escrow Agreement definition of "Escrow Release Date" to run from the **Closing Date** rather than the date of the Escrow Agreement. This would provide 12 months post-Closing (through approximately September 15, 2027).
- Alternatively, amend SPA § 9.1 to provide a fixed survival period of 18 months post-Closing for non-Fundamental representations, with the escrow release aligned to that date.
- If Seller resists, a minimum fallback is 12 months from Closing (not from signing).

### 5. Ambiguity in Fundamental Representation Cap – "Base Purchase Price Actually Received by Seller"

**Where:** SPA § 9.5(a), second sentence

**Issue:** The cap for Fundamental Representation claims is "the Base Purchase Price actually received by Seller." At Closing, the amount wired directly to Seller under § 2.2(d) is the Base Purchase Price *minus* the Escrow Amount ($4.2M), *minus* the debt payoff (~$7.3M), *minus* Transaction Expenses. This nets to approximately $30.5M (less expenses), not $42M.

**Why it matters:** If "actually received" is read as the net amount in § 2.2(d), the Fundamental Rep cap is roughly $11.5M lower than the full Base Purchase Price. Given that the escrow ($4.2M) is released to Seller after 12 months and the debt payoff benefits the Company (which Seller owns at Closing), one could argue Seller "receives" the full $42M in economic substance. But the literal language supports the lower number.

**Recommendation:**
- Replace "the Base Purchase Price actually received by Seller" with "the Base Purchase Price" (i.e., $42,000,000) to eliminate ambiguity.
- If Seller insists on the "actually received" language, clarify that it includes the Escrow Amount and the amounts paid on behalf of the Company under § 2.2(b) and (c).

### 6. FieldPath Software Potentially Omitted from Bill of Sale Annex A

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 ("Company Software" definition); Bill of Sale § 1 ("Company Software" definition); Bill of Sale Annex A; SPA § 11.10

**Issue:** The SPA defines "Company Software" to include "the FieldPath flight-planning and variable-rate application software." The Bill of Sale defines "Company Software" as "the software listed on Annex A." Annex A lists three categories: (1) Telemetry Dashboard (FleetView), (2) Ground-Station Firmware (GCS-series), and (3) Ancillary Tools. FieldPath is not explicitly identified in Annex A.

SPA § 11.10 provides that "the Bill of Sale shall control as to the identification of the assets conveyed thereunder."

**Why it matters:** If FieldPath is held by Seller or an Affiliate and is not listed in Annex A, it may not be effectively conveyed by the Bill of Sale. The Software Hosting Services Agreement (Disclosure Schedule § 4.6, item 7) references "FieldPath services" as a hosted component, suggesting it is a distinct software product. If it is a core revenue-generating product (the SPA definition pairs it with "variable-rate application"), its omission from the Bill of Sale would be a material gap.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with the deal team and technical diligence whether FieldPath is (a) already owned by the Company, (b) held by Seller or an Affiliate, or (c) a component of FleetView.
- If held by Seller or an Affiliate, add FieldPath to Bill of Sale Annex A before Closing.
- If it is a component of FleetView, add a clarifying note to Annex A to avoid ambiguity.

### 7. Buyer Indemnity for Seller Guarantees – Economic Risk of Fleet Financing

**Where:** SPA § 9.3(c); SPA § 6.8; Disclosure Schedule § 4.16; Board Consent § 5

**Issue:** SPA § 9.3(c) requires Buyer to indemnify Seller for "any Seller Guarantee, including any payment, performance or other obligation of Seller or its Affiliates under any Seller Guarantee, whether arising before or after the Closing." The Seller Guarantee covers the Company's obligations under the Fleet Financing Agreement, which has an outstanding principal balance of $7,315,400 (Disclosure Schedule § 4.16, item 1).

Separately, SPA § 6.8 requires Buyer to use commercially reasonable efforts to replace Seller's guarantee with one from Buyer or an Affiliate. The Board Consent (§ 5.1) authorizes this.

**Why it matters:** Regardless of whether the replacement guarantee is effected:
- If Buyer provides the replacement guarantee, Buyer (or an Affiliate) is directly liable for $7.3M+ in equipment financing.
- If the replacement is not effected and the Company defaults, Fallowgate calls the Seller Guarantee, Seller pays, and then Buyer indemnifies Seller under § 9.3(c).

In either scenario, Buyer bears the full economic risk of the Fleet Financing. This is a contingent liability of over $7.3M that is not reflected in the purchase price structure or the escrow.

**Recommendation:**
- Confirm with the deal team that the $7.3M debt payoff at Closing (SPA § 2.2(b)) is intended to eliminate this exposure. If the debt is paid off at Closing, the guarantee should be released simultaneously, and § 9.3(c) becomes moot.
- If the debt is NOT paid off at Closing (i.e., it remains outstanding and Buyer assumes it through the Company), then the § 9.3(c) indemnity and § 6.8 replacement guarantee create a layered obligation. In that case, seek to delete § 9.3(c) or limit it to pre-Closing defaults only.
- Review the payoff letter mechanics to confirm whether the Fleet Financing is being paid off or assumed.

### 8. Broad Earn-Out Information Rights Create Competitive Exposure

**Where:** SPA § 2.5(d); SPA § 6.6; SPA § 6.7

**Issue:** During the Earn-Out Period (through December 31, 2027) and until final determination, Buyer must:
- Deliver quarterly Net Revenue reports "broken down by customer, by acreage serviced and by product and service line, with the applicable pricing"; and
- Give Seller and its accountants "reasonable access… to the books, records, customer lists, acreage and application logs, pricing schedules and personnel of the Company relevant to the calculation of Net Revenue."

**Why it matters:** Seller is bound by a 4-year non-compete (§ 6.7(a)) covering agricultural UAVs in the US and Canada. However, the non-compete does not cover other agricultural technology sectors, and Seller's affiliates (other than "controlled Affiliates") are not restricted. The detailed customer-level, acreage-level, and pricing-level information delivered to Seller creates a roadmap that could facilitate:
- Competition in adjacent agricultural technology markets not covered by the non-compete;
- Solicitation of the Company's customers by Seller's uncontrolled affiliates or successors;
- Litigation leverage in any earn-out dispute.

While § 6.6 imposes confidentiality on Seller, the practical risk of information leakage over a 15+ month period (earn-out determination in ~March 2028) is significant.

**Recommendation:**
- Limit the quarterly reports to aggregate Net Revenue by product/service line only, without customer-level or acreage-level detail.
- Replace the broad access right with a right to receive a certified financial statement and a limited audit right (which is already provided in the second sentence of § 2.5(d)).
- If Seller insists on detailed reporting, add a specific prohibition on use of the information for any competitive purpose and an enhanced injunctive remedy for breach.

### 9. MAE Carve-Out for Seasonal Weather and Crop Prices

**Where:** SPA § 1.1 ("Material Adverse Effect" definition), proviso clause (v)

**Issue:** The MAE definition excludes "seasonal weather conditions or crop prices" from constituting an MAE, subject only to the "disproportionate effect" proviso.

**Why it matters:** The Company operates in Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa, and Oklahoma (per the Part 107 Waiver in Side Letter Annex 1, Part A, item 3). A severe drought, hail event, or crop price collapse in the Central Plains between signing (March 2026) and closing (September 2026) could materially reduce the Company's revenue and value without giving Buyer a walk-right under § 7.2(e). The "disproportionate effect" proviso provides some protection, but the burden would be on Buyer to show the Company was hit harder than its industry peers.

**Recommendation:**
- This is a negotiated business point and may be difficult to change. However, consider adding "hail, tornado or other catastrophic weather event directly affecting the Company's operating territory" as an excluded-from-the-carve-out (i.e., such events CAN constitute an MAE even though general seasonal weather cannot).
- At minimum, ensure the working capital target ($3.85M) is set at a level that protects against revenue shortfalls in the interim period.

## MODERATE ISSUES

### 10. Twelve-Month Employee Compensation Lock

**Where:** SPA § 10.1

**Issue:** For 12 months post-Closing, Buyer must maintain each Continuing Employee's base salary at no less than the pre-Closing level and provide benefits "substantially comparable in the aggregate to those provided to similarly situated employees of Buyer."

**Why it matters:** This constrains Buyer's ability to rationalize compensation as part of integration. If Furrowline's compensation structure is above Bisart's norms for comparable roles, Buyer is locked into the higher cost for a year. The "substantially comparable in the aggregate to those provided to similarly situated employees of Buyer" language could also be read to require Buyer to upgrade Furrowline employees to Bisart's benefit levels if they are higher.

**Recommendation:**
- Clarify that "substantially comparable" means Furrowline employees receive benefits no less favorable than they had at Furrowline, not that they must be upgraded to Bisart's levels.
- Add an exception for employees whose positions are eliminated in a bona fide integration restructuring (with severance in lieu).

### 11. Six-Year D&O Indemnification Lock

**Where:** SPA § 6.13

**Issue:** For 6 years post-Closing, Buyer must cause the Company not to amend its charter or bylaws in any manner that would adversely affect the exculpation or indemnification rights of pre-Closing directors and officers.

**Why it matters:** This is a long lock that limits the Company's governance flexibility. If Buyer wishes to restructure the Company (e.g., merge it into another subsidiary, adopt a new charter), it must preserve the D&O protections of former Furrowline directors and officers for 6 years. This is not unusual but is longer than the typical 3–5 year tail.

**Recommendation:**
- Acceptable as a matter of market practice, but confirm that the Company's D&O insurance policy (referenced in § 4.14) includes a 6-year tail or that Buyer's program (Board Consent § 8.3) will cover the exposure.

### 12. FAA Exemption No. 21874 Expiration – July 31, 2027

**Where:** Side Letter Annex 1, Part A, item 2; Side Letter Annex 1, Part B, item 1; Side Letter § 6.14(a)(iii)

**Issue:** The Section 44807 Exemption (which authorizes operation of spray drones up to 165 lb max takeoff weight and swarm operations of up to three aircraft per pilot) expires **July 31, 2027** – approximately 10 months after the expected Closing date. A petition for extension to July 31, 2029 was filed May 28, 2026.

The Side Letter covenant (§ 6.14(a)(iii)) requires pre-Closing renewal filings only for authorizations expiring within 6 months after Closing (i.e., before March 15, 2027). The July 31, 2027 expiration falls outside this window, so no pre-Closing filing is contractually required (though one has already been made).

**Why it matters:** If the FAA denies or delays the extension, the Company loses authority to operate its larger spray drones post-Closing. This would be a material impairment of the business. There is no closing condition or specific indemnity tied to this authorization's continued validity.

**Recommendation:**
- Add a specific closing condition or bring-down representation that Exemption No. 21874 remains in full force and effect at Closing (this is partially covered by new § 4.12(a), which represents all FAA Authorizations are "valid and in full force and effect").
- Consider adding a covenant that requires Seller to use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain the extension before Closing, given it is the Company's most operationally critical authorization.
- Monitor the status of the pending petition (filed May 28, 2026) and request updates as a condition to Closing.

### 13. Knowledge Transfer Limited to 40 Hours Per Individual

**Where:** Bill of Sale § 3.4

**Issue:** Post-Closing, Seller's software personnel are available for knowledge transfer for 90 days, but "limited to no more than forty (40) hours per individual."

**Why it matters:** For a complex software platform with multiple components (telemetry dashboard, ground-station firmware, flight-planning software, CI/CD pipelines, build environments), 40 hours per engineer may be insufficient to transfer institutional knowledge, particularly for the GCS firmware and build/signing processes. If key personnel from Bramand's engineering organization are the only ones who understand certain systems, the 40-hour cap could leave gaps.

**Recommendation:**
- Increase the cap to 80 hours per individual, or remove the cap for the first 30 days and apply a 40-hour limit thereafter.
- Add a requirement that Seller identify in writing (before Closing) the specific individuals who will be available and their areas of expertise, so Buyer can assess adequacy.

### 14. Escrow Fund Competition – Working Capital vs. Indemnification Claims

**Where:** SPA § 2.4(d); SPA § 9.6(a); Escrow Agreement § 4.6; Escrow Agreement § 5.1

**Issue:** The $4.2M Escrow Fund secures both (a) Seller's indemnification obligations and (b) any working capital shortfall owed to Buyer (SPA § 2.3). If the final working capital adjustment results in a shortfall to Buyer AND there are indemnification claims, both compete for the same $4.2M.

**Why it matters:** The working capital statement is due 90 days post-Closing (~December 14, 2026). The Escrow Release Date is March 2, 2027. If there is a material working capital shortfall (say $1–2M) and also indemnification claims, the escrow may be insufficient to cover both, leaving Buyer to pursue Seller directly for the balance – which is problematic given the short survival period and the asymmetric assignment clause.

**Recommendation:**
- This is a structural issue that is difficult to fix without increasing the escrow. At minimum, ensure the working capital target ($3.85M) is well-calibrated through the illustrative calculation in Schedule 2.4 to minimize the probability of a material shortfall.
- Consider whether the escrow should be increased to $5M+ to provide adequate headroom for both purposes.
