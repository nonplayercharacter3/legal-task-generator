### Issue 2: The Saltmarsh Pavilion change-of-control consent is not a closing condition, and the Seller's representations appear inconsistent with it

- **Where:**
  - Consent §1.4(a) and (b): the Managers were advised that the Kestrel Bay Parks Conservancy license requires the Conservancy's prior written consent to any change of control of Lumenhall, and that the Conservancy may terminate on 30 days' notice if consent is not obtained.
  - Consent §5 (Resolution 4): the Authorized Officers may seek that consent "after the Closing."
  - SPA §1.1 "Required Consents": the lender consent, the ticketing counterparty consent, and NYSLA approval. The Conservancy is **not** listed.
  - SPA §4.4(c) and §4.5: no third-party consent is required other than the Required Consents.
  - SPA §4.8(c): a venue-occupancy contract is a Material Contract, so §4.4(c) applies to the license.
  - SPA §4.9: the license is in full force and effect and no default or termination notice has been received.
  - SPA §6.3 (Seller uses only commercially reasonable efforts on Required Consents), §7.2(d) (closing condition limited to Required Consents).
- **Why it matters:** The Saltmarsh Pavilion is one of the Company's two core businesses (SPA Recital B). The Seller knew, as of March 13, that the Conservancy's consent was needed. Yet §4.5 says no third-party consent is needed other than Required Consents, and §4.4(c) carves out only the Required Consents. Those representations appear inaccurate on the face of the documents. However, if the Disclosure Schedule discloses the consent requirement, the representations are qualified and Rebond has no breach claim (SPA Article IV lead-in: "Except as set forth in the Disclosure Schedule"). In either case, Rebond bears the risk of losing the venue on 30 days' notice, and nothing in the SPA protects it. The Seller's plan to seek consent only after Closing means the risk is unresolved at signing.
- **Fix:**
  - Before any further closing step, obtain the Conservancy's written consent or a written waiver. If the parties are amending the SPA anyway (Issue 1), add the Conservancy consent to "Required Consents" and make it a §7.2(d) condition, in form reasonably satisfactory to Buyer.
  - If the Conservancy will not consent before Closing, negotiate a special, uncapped indemnity for losses from termination or non-renewal of the license, supported by a dedicated escrow or holdback, and/or a purchase price adjustment. Make clear that the indemnity applies regardless of Disclosure Schedule disclosure.
  - Ask the client for its own assessment of how much of the Company's value depends on the Saltmarsh venue. The folder does not contain financial figures for that.
- **Open point:** The Saltmarsh License Agreement itself (notice period, cure rights, change-of-control definition, any renewal term) and the Disclosure Schedule entries against §§4.4, 4.5, 4.8 and 4.9.

### Issue 3: The Side Letter overrides the SPA's uncapped tax protection and caps it at 10% of the Purchase Price

- **Where:**
  - SPA §8.4(b): the Indemnity Cap does not apply to Fraud, Fundamental Representations, or breaches of §4.12 (Taxes); those Losses are capped at the Purchase Price, except for Fraud.
  - SPA §8.4(a): the Indemnity Cap (10% of the Purchase Price, or $8,640,000) applies to liability under §8.2(a) only.
  - Side Letter §3(b): "the Indemnity Cap shall apply to all Losses under Section 8.2, including Losses arising from any breach of Section 4.12," and "the only Losses excluded from the Indemnity Cap are Losses arising from Fraud and from breaches of the Fundamental Representations."
  - SPA §10.3: in a conflict among the Transaction Documents, the Side Letter controls first, then the SPA, then the Escrow Agreement. The Side Letter is a Transaction Document (Side Letter §6(a)).
  - The Feb Draft's §8.4(b) has the same tax carve-out, so this was a negotiated point that the Side Letter later reversed.
- **Why it matters:** Under the SPA as drafted, tax-representation losses are recoverable up to the full $86.4 million Purchase Price. Under the Side Letter, they are capped at $8.64 million. The Side Letter also appears to cap the §8.2(c) Pre-Closing Taxes indemnity and §8.2(b) covenant claims, which the SPA's §8.4(a) arguably does not cap at all. Because the Side Letter controls under §10.3, Seller will argue that the cap applies. The Side Letter's "understandings" language and its "to clarify" framing give Rebond some arguments, but they are weak against an express precedence clause. This is the most significant economic change in the file. Its effect is also tied to the escrow amount, which equals the cap (Issue 5).
- **Fix:**
  - Amend the Side Letter (signed by both parties under SPA §10.2 and Side Letter §6(a)) to delete §3(b) or restore SPA §8.4(b) and the uncapped treatment of §8.2(c).
  - If Seller will not agree, consider a separate tax-specific cap and a dedicated tax escrow (Issue 5).
  - Before negotiating, determine with the client whether it understood the §3(b) change when it signed. Pull the drafting history of the Side Letter. That history bears on any reformation or mutual-mistake argument, which requires New York law research that I have not done.
- **Open point:** Negotiation history of Side Letter §3(b); whether the Side Letter's language was intended as an amendment.

### Issue 4: The Seller controls tax audits without Buyer consent, and Buyer's settlement protection is narrow

- **Where:**
  - Side Letter §3(c): the Seller controls, at its own expense, any audit or proceeding "relating solely to a pre-Closing tour settlement." Buyer has information and participation rights only (§3(c)(1)–(3)). Buyer's consent is required for a settlement only if it "would reasonably be expected to increase the Taxes of the Company for any period beginning after the Closing Date" (§3(c)(4)).
  - SPA §8.5: the Seller may not assume the defense of "any claim relating to Taxes" without Buyer's consent.
  - SPA §6.6: Buyer's approval right for pre-closing returns; cooperation on audits.
  - SPA §4.12(c)–(e): nonresident performer withholding, admissions/amusement/sales taxes, no pending or threatened audits "to Seller's knowledge."
  - Side Letter §3(a): Seller says the Company's tour settlements are examined by state and local authorities "periodically," and that the exams cover nonresident withholding and admissions/sales taxes.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Under Side Letter §3(c), the Seller would control and could settle a pre-Closing tax claim in a way that binds the Company's tax position, exposes the Company to penalties, or establishes a practice that continues after Closing, without Buyer's consent, so long as the settlement does not increase post-Closing taxes. Because the Company is Buyer's post-Closing business, Buyer bears the operational and reputational consequences.
  - "Solely" is ambiguous. Touring audits will usually touch both pre- and post-Closing periods, and the Side Letter does not say who controls those mixed proceedings.
  - The control right conflicts with SPA §8.5, which the Side Letter overrides under §10.3.
  - Side Letter §3(a) suggests tax examinations are ongoing or recurring. That bears on whether the §4.12(e) representation was accurate at signing (Issue 9).
- **Fix:**
  - Amend §3(c) so that Buyer controls, or has the right to consent to any settlement of, any audit that involves a post-Closing period, a continuing practice (such as nonresident withholding procedures), or any matter for which Buyer seeks indemnity. The Seller should have only the right to participate, unless Buyer waives control.
  - Require the Seller to give Buyer written notice of any audit within a short period (for example, 10 business days) and to provide all audit correspondence.
  - Include a Buyer step-in right if the Seller fails to diligently defend.
  - Define "pre-Closing tour settlement" and add a rule for mixed proceedings.
  - Under SPA §6.6, give Buyer a right to require changes to pre-Closing returns, with a dispute mechanism, and not merely a review-and-approve right.
- **Open point:** Whether any audit is pending now. The Disclosure Schedule (Issue 9) and tax audit files will show that.

### Issue 5: After 18 months, Buyer's recovery depends on an LLC that is free to distribute its proceeds

- **Where:**
  - SPA §2.4(a)–(b): the $8,640,000 escrow is released to Seller 18 months after Closing, less pending claims.
  - SPA §8.6: indemnifiable Losses are paid first from escrow and then "directly by Seller."
  - SPA §8.1: general representations survive 18 months; §4.12 and the Fundamental Representations survive until 60 days after the statute of limitations.
  - Escrow §5.2: release to Seller at the end of the Escrow Period.
  - Consent §6 (Resolution 5): after Closing, net proceeds are applied per the Operating Agreement, after expenses and reserves the Managing Manager "determines to be reasonably necessary."
  - Consent §1.5: the Managers determined the sale is in the best interests of "the Company and its members."
  - No parent guaranty, member guaranty, net-worth covenant, or distribution restriction appears in any document in the folder. SPA §6.1(d) restricts dividends only before Closing.
- **Why it matters:** Tax and fundamental-representation claims can survive for years after the escrow is released. Once the escrow is gone, Rebond's only recourse is against Drorand, a holding LLC whose sole asset is the sale proceeds. The Managers have expressly approved distributing those proceeds, with reserves left to the Managing Manager's discretion. This is probably the main practical credit risk in the deal.
- **Fix:**
  - Obtain a guaranty from Drorand's members or a creditworthy parent, or a net-worth or minimum-asset covenant running through the survival periods.
  - Restrict distributions by the Seller until the later of the Escrow Period's end and resolution of pending tax claims, or require a reserve for indemnity obligations.
  - Consider a separate tax holdback or escrow that runs to the statute of limitations plus 60 days, sized to the tax exposure once the tax diligence is complete.
  - Add an express set-off right against any amount payable to the Seller (including any net working capital true-up; see Issue 13).
- **Open point:** The Operating Agreement (Issue 10) will show what the Company may distribute and what member-level obligations exist.

### Issue 6: The Escrow Agreement covers only breaches of representations, but the SPA says escrow secures all indemnifiable Losses

- **Where:**
  - SPA §1.1 "Indemnifiable Losses": Losses under §8.2(a), (b) or (c).
  - SPA §2.4(a): escrow secures "all Indemnifiable Losses."
  - SPA §8.6: Indemnifiable Losses are satisfied first from escrow.
  - Escrow §1.1 "Indemnifiable Claim": limited to breach of a representation under §8.2(a).
  - Escrow §4.2: the Escrow Agent disburses only in respect of an Indemnifiable Claim.
- **Why it matters:** A claim under §8.2(b) (covenant breach) or §8.2(c) (Pre-Closing Taxes) cannot be paid from escrow under the Escrow Agreement as drafted, even though the SPA says it should be. Pre-Closing Taxes is one of Rebond's most important protections in light of Issue 4. The mismatch would also give the Seller an argument that a tax claim must be pursued directly against the LLC, which is the weakest recovery route (Issue 5).
- **Fix:** Amend the Escrow Agreement's definition of "Indemnifiable Claim" to include all of §8.2(a), (b) and (c). Because the Escrow Agreement is subordinate under SPA §10.3, the SPA should be amended as well if the parties prefer a different scope.
- **Open point:** The Escrow Agreement is an unsigned form. It can still be fixed before signing.

### Issue 7: Survival gaps for the Pre-Closing Taxes indemnity and the covenant indemnity

- **Where:**
  - SPA §8.1: survival is stated for "representations and warranties." It says "Covenants shall survive in accordance with their terms." It says nothing about the §8.2(c) indemnity.
  - SPA §8.2(c): Pre-Closing Taxes indemnity, "to the extent not taken into account" in the working capital determination.
- **Why it matters:** It is arguable that §8.2(c) has no express survival period, which would leave the Seller room to argue that the tax indemnity expired. That is a serious gap for a claim that could arise years after Closing, once tax audits are completed.
- **Fix:** Add an express survival clause for §8.2(c) (and for covenant indemnity under §8.2(b) where the covenant has no stated term), running to the statute of limitations plus 60 days, consistent with §4.12.

### Issue 8: Forum and governing law conflict between the SPA and the Escrow Agreement

- **Where:**
  - SPA §2.4(c): disputes concerning escrow claims are resolved under SPA §10.8 and §10.9.
  - SPA §10.8: New York law. SPA §10.9: exclusive jurisdiction in New York County courts, with a jury waiver.
  - Escrow §5.3: Disputed Amounts are released only on Joint Written Instructions or a Final Order of the Court of Chancery of Delaware.
  - Escrow §12.6: Delaware law, and exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery.
- **Why it matters:** A dispute over disputed escrow funds could proceed in two courts under two laws. Rebond could win in New York and still be unable to get the funds released if the Escrow Agent insists on a Delaware order. The Escrow Agent would also be pulled between the two. The Chancery Court's jurisdiction over a contract dispute about money may also be limited. I have not researched that point, and it should be checked.
- **Fix:** Conform the Escrow Agreement to SPA §10.8 and §10.9, or amend SPA §2.4(c) and §10.9 to match the Escrow Agreement. Either approach should be confirmed with the Escrow Agent.

### Issue 11: No pro-sandbagging clause, and Buyer's pre-Closing knowledge may be used against it

- **Where:** The SPA contains no clause stating that Buyer's knowledge of a breach, or disclosure of a matter in the diligence process, does not waive its indemnity rights. Buyer already has information about the Conservancy consent (Consent §1.4) and about tax examinations (Side Letter §3(a)).
- **Why it matters:** If Buyer closes knowing of a breach, the Seller may argue that Buyer waived the claim or that its reliance was unreasonable. Whether New York law permits a buyer to recover for breaches it knew of before Closing is a question for research that I have not completed. The answer may depend on whether the SPA contains an express reservation of rights, which it does not appear to.
- **Fix:** Add an express reservation of rights, stating that Buyer's knowledge and any diligence do not limit its indemnity rights. Confirm the New York rule before Closing, and document in writing any matters Buyer knows about and wants to preserve.

### Issue 12: The basket is ambiguous as applied to tax and Fundamental Representations, and it is a tipping basket of 0.5%

- **Where:** SPA §8.4(a) (Seller not liable under §8.2(a) until Losses exceed $432,000, then "from the first dollar"); SPA §8.4(b) (removes the cap, but says nothing about the basket).
- **Why it matters:** The $432,000 figure is 0.5% of the $86.4 million Purchase Price, not 5%. Because §8.4(b) removes only the cap, the Seller can argue that the basket still applies to Fundamental Representation and §4.12 claims. That would leave the first $432,000 of tax losses for Rebond to absorb. This is a drafting point, but it could be worth real money.
- **Fix:** State expressly that the basket does not apply to Fraud, Fundamental Representations, §4.12, or §8.2(b) and (c) claims.

### Issue 13: Net working capital mechanics and the Artist Deposit treatment

- **Where:**
  - SPA §2.3(a): in the estimate, Artist Deposits are current assets only to the extent the related engagement has not been cancelled.
  - SPA §2.3(b): the closing statement of "actual" net working capital is silent on the Artist Deposit cancellation rule. It does not say who pays a shortfall or how.
  - Side Letter §4: the Artist Deposit schedule is "for informational purposes," is "intended to assist Buyer" in preparing the §2.3(b) statement, and "does not modify" §2.3(a).
  - SPA §6.1(a): Seller may pay Artist Deposits in the ordinary course, up to $500,000 per engagement, without Buyer's consent.
  - Both drafts state the $3,200,000 target. Neither provides an escrow, set-off right, or payment direction for a post-Closing true-up.
- **Why it matters:** A cancelled-engagement deposit counted as a current asset at the actual-NWC stage would inflate the price Rebond pays. Side Letter §4 does not fix this, because it confirms that the §2.3(a) rule is not modified without saying whether the same rule applies in §2.3(b). Because §2.3(b) has no payment direction or set-off right, collection depends on the Seller's solvency (Issue 5).
- **Fix:** Amend §2.3(b) to apply the cancellation rule to actual net working capital, to state who pays a true-up and how, and to allow set-off against escrow or against amounts payable to the Seller. Consider a true-up reserve. Consider requiring the Seller to disclose any pre-Closing deposits above a set threshold.

### Issue 14: Materiality scrape and the Fraud definition

- **Where:**
  - SPA §8.4(c): materiality qualifiers are disregarded only "for purposes of calculating Losses."
  - SPA §1.1 "Fraud": "actual and intentional fraud ... in the making of the representations and warranties in this Agreement."
- **Why it matters:** A single scrape (for Losses only) does not remove materiality qualifiers from the question of whether a breach occurred. Rebond would have to prove a material breach before it could recover. The Fraud definition excludes fraud in closing certificates (§7.2(c)), in the Side Letter, and in the Consent.
- **Fix:** Apply a double materiality scrape (for both breach and Losses). Broaden "Fraud" to cover certificates and the Transaction Documents.

### Issue 15: Non-compete and non-solicit scope

- **Where:** SPA §6.4 (three years; restricts Seller and "its Affiliates" from promoting, producing, or booking live music events, or operating an outdoor amphitheater, "within the State of New York"); SPA §6.5 (confidentiality binds Seller only).

### Issue 17: Escrow Agreement execution and administration gaps

- **Where:**
  - Escrow signature blocks are blank; the Escrow Agent's signatory name is blank; the date is "____, 2026."
  - Schedule A: account details are "to be provided" by the Escrow Agent.
  - Schedule B: Buyer's second authorized representative is "to be designated by Buyer before the Closing." Escrow §7.6 requires call-back verification by someone other than the signer.

  - SPA §2.4(b) releases less "pending and unresolved claims." Escrow §5.2 retains amounts for "Claim Notices delivered before the end of the Escrow Period." The two phrasings differ.

The objection-timing inconsistency could affect when Buyer can actually draw on escrow. Buyer also bears tax on escrow income it may not economically own.
- **Fix:** Complete the blanks. Designate Buyer's second representative. Verify wire instructions by independent call-back before any transfer. Conform the objection-timing and pending-claim language. Confirm the tax-owner treatment with the client's tax advisers.
