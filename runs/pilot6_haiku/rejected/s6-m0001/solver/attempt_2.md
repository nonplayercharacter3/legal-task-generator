## 1. Bottom Line

1. **The largest exposure is in the Side Letter, not the SPA.** Side Letter ¶3(b) caps tax recovery at $8,640,000 (10% of the Purchase Price). The executed SPA does not cap Pre-Closing Taxes under §8.2(c) at all, and it caps §4.12 tax-rep breaches only at the Purchase Price. Under SPA §10.3, the Side Letter takes precedence.
2. **Tax claims cannot be paid from escrow on Buyer's instruction alone.** The Escrow Agreement limits escrow claims to §8.2(a) rep breaches. The escrow also releases after 18 months, while tax audits may run longer.
3. **Seller's post-closing credit is weak.** Seller is a holding LLC whose only asset is the Shares. Its managers have approved distribution of net proceeds.
4. **The Saltmarsh Pavilion license requires Conservancy consent to the change of control, but the Conservancy is not a Required Consent.** Seller's own managers plan to seek that consent after closing. This likely makes SPA §§4.4(c) and 4.5 inaccurate unless the Disclosure Schedule discloses the point.
5. **Seller's authority evidence covers managers only.** SPA §4.2 says the sale was authorized by managers *and members*. The folder contains no member approval.

## 3. Issues, in Order of Importance

### Issue 1: Side Letter ¶3(b) caps tax recovery and conflicts with SPA §8.4(b)

- **Where:** Side Letter ¶3(b). Related: SPA §§8.2(c), 8.4(a)–(b), 10.2, 10.3; Side Letter ¶6(a).
- **What it says:** SPA §8.4(b) excludes from the Indemnity Cap Losses from Fraud, Fundamental Representations, and breaches of §4.12 (Taxes). Those Losses are subject only to a Purchase Price ceiling. Side Letter ¶3(b) instead says the Indemnity Cap applies to "all Losses under Section 8.2, including Losses arising from any breach of Section 4.12," and that only Fraud and Fundamental Representations are excluded. It describes this as "clarif[ying]" Article VIII.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - Under the executed SPA, the §8.4(a) cap reaches only §8.2(a) claims. Pre-Closing Taxes under §8.2(c) carry no stated cap. Side Letter ¶3(b) would cap them at $8.64M, and it would also cap §8.2(b) covenant claims.
  - The Side Letter prevails over the SPA and Escrow Agreement (SPA §10.3). It is a Transaction Document and was signed by both Buyer and Seller, so it satisfies the written-amendment requirement of SPA §10.2 and Side Letter ¶6(a). Expect Seller to rely on it.
  - Tour-settlement taxes (nonresident performer withholding and admissions, amusement, and sales taxes) are the exposure the Side Letter itself identifies in ¶3(a). This is the most likely significant post-closing claim.
  - The February 2026 draft already contained the §8.4(b) carve-out (see Section 5). The Side Letter reversed a term Rebond had in the draft.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - *If not closed:* Do not close on this text. Require a corrected ¶3(b) restoring SPA §8.4(b), and expressly exclude §8.2(c) from any cap, documented by a written amendment under SPA §10.2.
  - *If closed:* Send Seller written notice that Rebond does not accept ¶3(b) as an accurate statement of the parties' agreement and reserves all rights. Evaluate reformation for mutual mistake, recognizing that New York requires clear and convincing evidence of mutual mistake. Confirm this standard before relying on it.
  - Determine who drafted ¶3(b) and whether Rebond's deal team reviewed the change.
- **Open points:** No Side Letter drafts are in the folder, so the negotiation history cannot be checked. Tax exposure has not been quantified. Without a tax diligence estimate, it is not possible to say how much the cap matters in dollars.

### Issue 2: Escrow Agreement limits escrow claims to rep breaches

- **Where:** Escrow Agreement §1.1 ("Indemnifiable Claim" is limited to breaches under SPA §8.2(a)); §§4.1, 4.2, 5.1, 7.1. Compare SPA §§1.1 ("Indemnifiable Losses"), 2.4(a), 8.6, and 10.3.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** SPA §2.4(a) says escrow secures "all Indemnifiable Losses." Under SPA §1.1, that term includes §8.2(b) (covenants) and §8.2(c) (Pre-Closing Taxes). The Escrow Agreement reaches only §8.2(a). The Escrow Agent disburses only on Claim Notices for "Indemnifiable Claims" (§4.2) and is not bound by the SPA (§7.1: it has "no duty to inquire into the Purchase Agreement"). A tax claim therefore cannot be paid from escrow unless Seller signs Joint Written Instructions (§5.1), which Seller can withhold. Because the Escrow Agreement ranks last under SPA §10.3, the SPA does not override it in practice.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - *If the Escrow Agreement is unsigned:* Revise the definition of "Indemnifiable Claim" to include §8.2(b) and §8.2(c) claims. Add a tax-claim procedure, including a deemed-release mechanism for claims Seller does not timely object to.
  - *If signed:* Obtain a corrective amendment under Escrow Agreement §12.2, which requires signatures from Buyer, Seller, and the Escrow Agent.

### Issue 3: Seller controls tour-settlement tax audits and settlements, and the SPA does not clearly limit that control

- **Where:** Side Letter ¶3(c)(1)–(4). Related: SPA §8.5 (Seller may not assume defense of Tax claims without Buyer consent); SPA §6.1(e) (pre-closing tax-settlement restriction); SPA §6.6 (Seller prepares post-closing Company returns).
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - ¶3(c) gives Seller control of an audit "relating **solely** to a pre-Closing tour settlement." Multi-jurisdiction audits, audits spanning pre- and post-closing periods, and audits of withholding practices that continue after Closing may fall outside that scope, and the scope will be disputed.
  - ¶3(c)(4) requires Buyer's consent only to a settlement that would increase Company taxes for periods after Closing. Seller may therefore settle a pre-closing matter without Buyer consent, even where Buyer pays under §8.2(c) or where the settlement sets filing positions the Company must follow.
  - SPA §8.5 bars Seller from controlling tax defense without Buyer's consent. Side Letter ¶3(c) overrides that under §10.3, but the overlap is not expressly resolved.
  - SPA §6.6 lets Seller prepare the Company's pre-closing returns due after Closing. Buyer gets "review and approval" 30 days before filing, but the SPA sets no mechanism if Buyer disagrees.
- **Recommended fix:** Amend ¶3(c) to define scope clearly, with allocation rules for mixed-period audits. Require Buyer consent to any settlement that Buyer or the Company will pay or that affects post-closing filings. Give Buyer control of audits touching ongoing practices. Under §6.6, give Buyer a written approval right with a dispute mechanism, and have Seller bear the cost of pre-closing returns.
- **Open points:** Whether any audit is currently pending. Side Letter ¶3(a) and ¶3(c) suggest examinations occur, but SPA §4.12(e) says no audit is pending or threatened. This must be checked against the Disclosure Schedule.

### Issue 4: Conservancy consent to the change of control is not a Required Consent

- **Where:** SPA §1.1 ("Required Consents," items (a)–(c)); §§4.4(c), 4.5, 4.8(c), 4.9, 6.3, 7.2(d). Written Consent §1.4(a)–(b) and §5 (Resolution 4, second paragraph).
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - Written Consent §1.4 states that the Saltmarsh Pavilion License Agreement requires the Conservancy's prior written consent to any change of control of Lumenhall. If that consent is not obtained, the Conservancy may terminate on 30 days' notice.
  - Written Consent §5 authorizes officers to seek that consent "after the Closing." The SPA does not require it at all.
  - §4.5 represents that no third-party consent is needed other than the Required Consents. §4.4(c) represents that no Material Contract conflict exists other than for Required Consents. The license is a Material Contract under §4.8(c) (venue occupancy). Both representations appear inaccurate unless the Disclosure Schedule discloses the consent requirement.
  - If the representations are inaccurate, Rebond's claim is subject to the 10% cap under §8.4(a) and to Buyer-knowledge arguments (Issue 8). The Saltmarsh venue is central to the business (SPA Recital B). Loss of the venue may not clear the Material Adverse Effect threshold.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - *If not closed:* Add the Conservancy's written consent to Required Consents, make it a §7.2(d) condition, and obtain a specific indemnity outside the cap, supported by escrow.
  - *If closed:* Obtain the Conservancy consent now, with Seller's cooperation under Written Consent §5. Pursue a special indemnity from Seller if the consent is refused or conditioned.
- **Open points:** Whether the Disclosure Schedule discloses the consent requirement. Whether a consent request has been made. The license terms (term, fees, termination rights, and any notice history). None of these documents is in the folder.

### Issue 5: Post-closing recourse against Seller is weak

- **Where:** SPA §8.6 (escrow first, then Seller directly); SPA §8.4(b) (Purchase Price ceiling); SPA §6.1(d) (distribution restriction applies only before Closing); Written Consent §6 (Resolution 5: distribution of net proceeds after reserves set by the Managing Manager). No guaranty or credit support appears in the folder.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** After Closing, Seller will hold roughly $77.76M in cash, which is the Purchase Price less the $8.64M escrow, subject to the working capital adjustment. Its managers have authorized distribution of proceeds to members under the Operating Agreement. Beyond the escrow, Rebond's recovery depends on Seller's solvency. That exposure is most serious for tax claims and for the Purchase-Price-level recovery on §4.12 and Fundamental Representations.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain a guaranty from the members or a parent. Failing that, include a minimum net worth covenant, a restriction on distributions until the later of 18 months or the expiry of the applicable statute of limitations, and a separate tax escrow or holdback sized to the tax exposure. Do this before Closing if the deal is still open.
- **Open points:** Identity and financial position of Seller's members (Operating Agreement needed). Whether Seller has any other assets.

### Issue 6: Seller's authority rests on manager approval only

- **Where:** SPA §4.2 ("duly authorized by all necessary action of Seller's managers and members"); SPA §4.3 (title to the Shares). Written Consent preamble (cites NY LLC Law §408(d) and Operating Agreement §6.7); §1.5 (determination "in the best interests of the Company and its members"); §2 (Resolution 1); §5 (Resolution 4, purporting to act for "the Company and Lumenhall"); §8.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - The Written Consent is signed only by the three managers. The sale is of Seller's only asset, the Shares. The Operating Agreement may require member approval for a sale of substantially all assets. It is not in the folder.
  - If member approval was required and not obtained, §4.2 is inaccurate. §4.2 is a Fundamental Representation, so the survival period is longer and recovery can reach the Purchase Price under §8.4(b). More seriously, the sale could be challenged, which would put Rebond's title to the Shares (§4.3, also Fundamental) at risk.
  - Resolution 4 purports to authorize Seller's managers to act on behalf of Lumenhall, a separate corporation whose board must approve its own actions. The folder contains no Lumenhall board or shareholder resolutions, and the SPA §3.2 closing deliverables include no Company secretary's certificate.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain the Operating Agreement and confirm whether member approval is required. If it is, obtain member consent or ratification before Closing, with an incumbency certificate. Obtain Lumenhall's board and shareholder resolutions and a secretary's certificate. Add these to the SPA §3.2 deliverables by amendment if necessary.
- **Open points:** Operating Agreement terms. The text of the cited NY LLC Law provision (confirm the cited section supports the consent's approach). Whether the managers are also members.

### Issue 7: Escrow release timing and claim reservation do not match the tax exposure

- **Where:** SPA §§2.4(b), 8.1 (general reps survive 18 months; §4.12 survives until 60 days after the statute of limitations; Fundamental Representations survive similarly; §8.2(c) survival is not addressed); Escrow Agreement §§1.1 ("Escrow Period," "Release Date"), 4.2, 5.2.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - Escrow releases at 18 months, less Claim Notices delivered before the end of the Escrow Period (§§4.2, 5.2). Tax claims often surface later, through audits. Unless a Claim Notice is delivered first, that exposure is not reserved, and recovery after release depends on Seller's credit (Issue 5).
  - §8.1 does not say how long the §8.2(c) Pre-Closing Taxes indemnity survives. Seller may argue the 18-month period applies, which would be inconsistent with the §4.12 survival period.
- **Recommended fix:**
  - Calendar the Release Date. Before it, deliver Claim Notices for every known or reasonably possible tax and rep claim, with good-faith estimates (§4.2(b) allows reasonable estimates).
  - Amend §8.1 so that §8.2(c) survives until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations, matching §4.12.
  - Consider extending the escrow for tax exposure or adding a separate tax holdback.

### Issue 8: Buyer's knowledge may bar claims, and the SPA has no pro-sandbagging clause

- **Where:** SPA Article IV and Article VIII (no express clause preserving claims despite Buyer's knowledge). Side Letter ¶3(a) (Seller disclosed the tour-settlement examinations). Written Consent §1.4 (disclosed the Conservancy consent issue).
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** Under New York law, a buyer that learns before closing that a representation is inaccurate may be treated as having waived the breach unless it preserves its rights. See, e.g., *Galli v. Metz*, 973 F.2d 145 (2d Cir. 1992). Rebond now has specific knowledge of the tour-settlement and Conservancy issues. Confirm the current state of New York law before relying on this analysis.
- **Recommended fix:** If closed, send Seller a reservation-of-rights letter now covering Issues 1–6. If not closed, add an express clause preserving claims despite Buyer's knowledge, and specific indemnities for the known issues.
- **Open points:** When Rebond first learned of each issue, and whether any closing document contains a waiver or reservation.

### Issue 9: The Disclosure Schedule is not in the folder

- **Where:** SPA §1.1 ("Disclosure Schedule"); Article IV lead-in ("Except as set forth in the Disclosure Schedule"); §§4.1, 4.8, 4.9, 4.12, 4.13.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** Every representation is qualified by the Disclosure Schedule. Disclosures may carve out tax audits, the Conservancy consent, and Material Contract matters. Without the Schedule, Rebond cannot know what it has a claim for.
- **Recommended fix:** Obtain and review the Disclosure Schedule immediately. Check it against Issues 1–8, particularly §§4.4, 4.5, 4.9, and 4.12(e).

### Issue 10: Conflicting forum and governing law for escrow disputes

- **Where:** SPA §§2.4(c), 10.8, 10.9. Side Letter ¶6(c). Escrow Agreement §§1.1 ("Final Order"), 5.3, 12.6.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** The SPA and Side Letter use New York law and exclusive New York County courts, with a jury waiver. The Escrow Agreement uses Delaware law and the exclusive jurisdiction of the Delaware Court of Chancery. SPA §2.4(c) routes escrow-claim disputes to the SPA forum. One claim could therefore be litigated in two forums. The "Final Order" definition also refers to "the court ... described in Section 5.3," which names only Chancery. Whether Chancery will hear a money claim should be confirmed.
- **Recommended fix:** Conform Escrow Agreement §12.6 to New York law and New York County courts. Alternatively, expressly carve escrow release disputes to Chancery in SPA §2.4(c) and the Side Letter, and confirm Chancery's jurisdiction.

### Issue 11: Working capital and Artist Deposits

- **Where:** SPA §1.1 ("Artist Deposits"); §2.3(a)–(b); §6.1(a); Side Letter ¶4.
- **Why it matters to Rebond:**
  - §2.3(a) treats Artist Deposits as current assets, raising the Purchase Price dollar-for-dollar, unless the engagement has been cancelled. Rebond has no express right to review or object to Seller's estimate before Closing.
  - §2.3(b) does not expressly repeat the Artist Deposits rule for the post-closing statement. The actual figure could therefore be calculated differently.
  - Side Letter ¶4 requires a schedule of deposits within 45 days, but it is "for informational purposes," does not modify §2.3(a), and sets no consequence for late delivery or for deposits tied to engagements that have failed.
  - The §2.3(b) true-up is not secured beyond the escrow.
- **Recommended fix:** If not closed, give Rebond a review and comment right on the estimate, expressly apply the cancelled-engagement rule in §2.3(b), and require deposits to be supported by signed engagement contracts. Post-closing, reconcile the Side Letter ¶4 schedule against the closing statement and pursue any overstatement.
- **Open points:** The estimate, the closing statement, and the deposit schedule are not in the folder.

### Issue 12: Required Consents: weak efforts standard and timing risk

- **Where:** SPA §6.3 (Seller uses "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain Required Consents); §1.1 (lender consent "together with a customary payoff letter"; NYSLA approval); §§3.2(d), 7.2(d).
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** The efforts standard is weak, Seller need not pay consent fees, and "customary payoff letter" is undefined. NYSLA approval may not fit the outside date. Buyer can waive the §7.2(d) condition, so it should not waive without the specific consents in hand.
- **Recommended fix:** Confirm the status of each Required Consent. Define the payoff letter content. Set a specific efforts obligation and a fee-bearing covenant if consents are still outstanding.

### Issue 13: Notice addresses are missing, and the escrow objection clock is inconsistent

- **Where:** SPA §10.1 (notices go to "addresses set forth on the signature page"). The executed signature page lists no addresses. Escrow Agreement §12.1 and Schedule B list no addresses. Escrow §4.3 (30 days from Seller's "receipt") vs. §4.4 (30 days from "delivered to Seller"). SPA §8.5 (30 days after "receipt").
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** Claim Notices start 30-day clocks. A defective notice, or one sent to the wrong place, can put Rebond at risk of losing the deemed-acceptance benefit under SPA §8.5 and Escrow §4.4. The conflicting triggers in §§4.3 and 4.4 could also lead the Escrow Agent to disburse early.
- **Recommended fix:** Add physical and email addresses for every party to the SPA and the Escrow Agreement, by amendment or written confirmation. Conform Escrow §4.4 to "receipt" as shown by the evidence under §4.5, and state that the Escrow Agent disburses only after that period ends.

### Issue 14: Escrow account, wire, and representative controls

- **Where:** Escrow Agreement §§2.2, 7.6; Schedule A (account details "to be provided ... by secure delivery"); Schedule B (Buyer's second representative "to be designated ... before the Closing").
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** Wire-fraud risk. Joint Written Instructions require an authorized representative from each party. With only one Buyer representative designated, call-back verification under §7.6 (which must go to a representative other than the signer) may not be possible.
- **Recommended fix:** Designate Buyer's second representative and provide addresses. Verify all wire details by call-back to independently sourced numbers.

### Issue 15: Escrow income is taxed to Buyer

- **Where:** Escrow Agreement §11.1 (Buyer treated as owner and reports all income), §6.3 (earnings added to the escrow).
- **Why it matters to Rebond:** Rebond will report and pay tax on income from funds it does not receive until disbursement, while Seller receives the earnings. That cost falls on Rebond.
- **Recommended fix:** Have Rebond's tax advisor confirm the treatment, including whether the buyer-as-owner approach is correct for this type of escrow. Negotiate a tax distribution from Seller or allocate the tax cost.
