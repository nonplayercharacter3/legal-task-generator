## Summary

I reviewed the three-document deal package (Supply Agreement, Statement of Work, Order Form) for the polaluminum chloride (PACl) supply to the Ostler Creek Water Treatment Plant. I identified twelve issues ranging from critical to minor. The three most consequential are: (1) a direct conflict between the Supply Agreement's blanket exclusivity and the Order Form's narrow exclusivity, which under the stated order of precedence locks Felound into buying *all* water treatment chemicals at *all* plants from Zapeiv; (2) an auto-renewal structure with a 30-day non-renewal window that, if missed, commits Felound to another five years; and (3) a definition of "Specifications" that lets Zapeiv unilaterally change product requirements. Details and proposed fixes follow.

---

## Issue 1 – Exclusivity Scope Conflict

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 4.1; Order Form § 4.1–4.3; Supply Agreement § 17.2 (order of precedence).

**Problem.** Supply Agreement § 4.1 requires Customer to purchase "one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of **all Water Treatment Chemicals** for use at **any Facility** exclusively from Supplier." The defined term "Water Treatment Chemicals" (§ 1.12) sweeps in coagulants, disinfectants, pH adjusters, corrosion inhibitors, and more. "Facility" (§ 1.3) covers any plant, pumping station, or related installation owned or operated by Customer or its Affiliates.

Order Form § 4.1–4.3, by contrast, limits exclusivity to the "Exclusive Product" (PACl coagulant only) at the "Covered Site" (Ostler Creek WTP only).

Under § 17.2, the Supply Agreement prevails over the Order Form in case of conflict. Read literally, § 4.1 controls, meaning Felound would be contractually barred from buying *any* water treatment chemical—chlorine, caustic soda, orthophosphate, anything—from anyone else at *any* of its plants for the duration of the term (and each renewal term).

**Why it matters.** Felound operates multiple municipal treatment plants. Locking 100% of all chemical purchasing to a single supplier at a single-product negotiated price is an extraordinary concentration of risk. It eliminates Felound's ability to source competitively, switch suppliers for a single product line, or respond to market changes. It also gives Zapeiv leverage over unrelated product categories.

**Recommended fix.** Conform § 4.1 to the scope actually negotiated. Replace with:

> "During the Term, Customer shall purchase one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of the Exclusive Product (as defined in the applicable Order Form) for use at the Covered Site (as defined in the applicable Order Form) exclusively from Supplier, and shall not buy the Exclusive Product for use at the Covered Site from any third party."

If Zapeiv wants a broader exclusivity as a commercial concession, it should be priced and scoped explicitly in the Order Form, not buried in the master agreement.

## Issue 2 – Auto-Renewal with 30-Day Non-Renewal Window

**Where:** Order Form § 2.2 (auto-renewal for successive five-year terms); § 2.3 (non-renewal notice window: "no earlier than 120 and no later than 90 days" before expiration).

**Problem.** The agreement renews automatically for five-year terms unless a party delivers notice in a 30-day window (days 120 through 90 before term end). Missing the window by a single day—whether due to internal approval delays, a holiday, or a notice addressed to the wrong person—locks Felound in for another five years. There is no cure mechanism for a late notice.

Compounded by Issue 1, a missed non-renewal notice would extend the (overly broad) exclusivity for another five years.

**Why it matters.** For a municipal water authority, a five-year lock-in on chemical supply is a significant strategic commitment. The narrow window creates a "use it or lose it" trap with no safety valve.

**Recommended fix.** Two changes:

1. Widen the window and add a cure: "Either Party may prevent the next renewal by giving written notice of non-renewal at least ninety (90) days before the then-current term expires. A notice is effective on the date of receipt. If a Party fails to give timely notice, the other Party may, within fifteen (15) days after the expiration of the notice period, deliver a reminder notice; if the first Party then delivers a non-renewal notice within ten (10) days after the reminder, the non-renewal is effective."

2. Alternatively (or additionally), cap the number of auto-renewals: "The Agreement will renew for no more than two (2) successive Renewal Terms."

## Issue 3 – "Specifications" Defined as Supplier's Data Sheets "From Time to Time"

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 1.8; SOW § 3.1 (specific PACl requirements); Supply Agreement § 17.2 (precedence).

**Problem.** § 1.8 defines "Specifications" as "Supplier's standard product data sheets for that Product, **as in effect from time to time**." This gives Zapeiv the unilateral right to change the product specs by updating its data sheets. The SOW § 3.1 pins down specific requirements (NSF/ANSI/CAN 60, minimum 10.0% Al₂O₃, 70% basicity), but under § 17.2 the Supply Agreement's definition controls over the SOW.

Consequently, Zapeiv could revise its data sheet to, say, 9.5% Al₂O₃ or a different basicity range, and that revision would become the contractual "Specification" governing acceptance and rejection under § 6.3 and the warranty under § 7.1.

**Why it matters.** Coagulant dosage is calibrated to the plant's treatment process. A change in basicity or aluminum content alters the effective dose, can affect finished-water quality (turbidity, pH, residual aluminum), and may require re-optimization of the treatment train. Felound needs the product spec to be fixed for the duration of the term, or at least subject to a mutual change process.

**Recommended fix.** Amend § 1.8 to:

> "**Specifications** means, for each Product, the product requirements set forth in the applicable Statement of Work or Order Form, as amended from time to time by mutual written agreement of the Parties."

Then add to SOW § 3.4 (Change Notification): "No change in manufacturing location, raw material source, or process will be effective unless the resulting product continues to meet the Specifications. If a proposed change would alter the Specifications, Supplier must obtain Customer's prior written consent. Customer may refuse consent if the change would affect finished-water quality or require modification of Customer's treatment process."

## Issue 4 – One-Way Price Escalation

**Where:** Order Form § 3.3.

**Problem.** The price is fixed for the first year, then Zapeiv may increase it annually by up to 3% based on the PPI for Industrial Inorganic Chemicals. There is no corresponding downward adjustment if the index falls. Over a five-year term, this ratchet could add up to roughly 15–16% to the base price even if market conditions are flat or declining.

**Why it matters.** At an estimated 180,000 gallons/year and $1.42/gallon, annual spend is approximately $255,600. A cumulative 15% increase adds ~$38,000/year by year five. More importantly, the one-way structure means Felound bears all downside market risk while Zapeiv captures upside.

**Recommended fix.** Make the adjustment symmetric:

> "Each adjustment will be based on the percentage change, over the twelve (12) months ending three (3) months before the adjustment date, in the Producer Price Index for Industrial Inorganic Chemicals, Not Elsewhere Classified, published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The unit price will be adjusted upward or downward by that percentage, provided that no single adjustment (upward or downward) may exceed three percent (3%) of the unit price in effect immediately before the adjustment."

## Issue 5 – Pro Rata Allocation During Force Majeure

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 14.2.

**Problem.** If a force majeure event affects Zapeiv's ability to perform, it must allocate available supply "at least pro rata with its other customers." For a water treatment plant, pro rata allocation is not a workable standard. If Zapeiv can supply only 60% of total demand, Felound receives 60% of its coagulant—but the plant cannot treat water at 60% coagulant dose without compromising turbidity removal and, potentially, public health compliance.

**Why it matters.** Felound's obligation to deliver safe drinking water is non-discretionary. A pro rata allocation that leaves the plant short of coagulant creates a regulatory and public-health crisis for which Felound bears the consequences, while Zapeiv's obligation is merely to allocate "pro rata."

**Recommended fix.** Add a priority provision:

> "During a Force Majeure Event affecting Supplier's ability to perform, Supplier will allocate its available supply to Customer in an amount sufficient to meet the Plant's minimum daily treatment requirements (as stated in the applicable Statement of Work) before allocating supply to other customers, to the extent Supplier's available supply permits. If Supplier cannot meet Customer's minimum daily treatment requirements for more than seventy-two (72) consecutive hours, Customer may terminate this Agreement without liability under Section 14.3."

This pairs with the existing 30-day termination right in § 14.3 and shortens the trigger for a water-critical supply.

## Issue 6 – Asymmetric Assignment Right

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 15.2.

**Problem.** Customer may not assign without Supplier's consent. Supplier may assign freely to any Affiliate or to any successor to all or part of its business, without Customer's consent.

**Why it matters.** If Zapeiv is acquired by, or merges into, an entity with inferior manufacturing capability, weaker financials, or a different quality culture, Felound is stuck with the new supplier for the remainder of the term (and any renewal terms). There is no right to object or terminate.

**Recommended fix.** Add a consent right or termination trigger:

> "Supplier may assign this Agreement to an Affiliate or to a successor to all or substantially all of its business only if the assignee (a) has demonstrated manufacturing capability and quality systems substantially equivalent to Supplier's, (b) assumes all of Supplier's obligations under the Supply Documents in writing, and (c) is not in bankruptcy or insolvency. Customer may terminate this Agreement without liability if Supplier assigns to a party that does not meet the foregoing conditions."

## Issue 7 – No Service-Level Penalties for Late or Failed Delivery

**Where:** SOW § 4.3–4.4 (delivery windows and lead times); Supply Agreement § 4.3 (supply shortfall remedy); Supply Agreement § 6.3 (rejection for nonconformity only).

**Problem.** The SOW commits Zapeiv to deliver within three business days of PO acceptance and to confirm deliveries 24 hours in advance. The only remedies for a missed delivery are: (a) the general supply-shortfall cover right in § 4.3 (which reimburses the price differential for replacement product but does not compensate for operational disruption), and (b) the force majeure termination right after 30 days. There are no liquidated damages, service credits, or escalating remedies for repeated or chronic late deliveries that fall short of the 30-day force majeure threshold.

**Why it matters.** A late delivery that forces the plant to draw down inventory below the 3,000-gallon minimum (SOW § 5.4) creates an operational risk. Repeated late deliveries erode the buffer and increase the probability of a stockout. Without a financial penalty, Zapeiv has limited incentive to prioritize Felound's deliveries over other customers.

**Recommended fix.** Add a service-credit provision to the SOW:

> "If Supplier fails to deliver a conforming Product by the confirmed delivery date (other than due to a Force Majeure Event), Customer will be entitled to a service credit of [X]% of the value of the affected delivery for each business day of delay, up to a maximum of [Y]% of the monthly invoice value. Service credits will be applied against the next invoice. If Supplier misses the confirmed delivery date on three (3) or more occasions in any consecutive six-month period, Customer may terminate this Agreement without liability."

## Issue 8 – Transition Assistance Limited to 90 Days and Unavailable If Customer in Breach

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 12.4.

**Problem.** Transition assistance (continued supply at current prices) is available for up to 90 days, but only if the agreement "expires or terminates for any reason other than Customer's uncured material breach." Two concerns:

- 90 days may be insufficient to qualify a replacement coagulant supplier (NSF certification, jar testing, regulatory notification, operator training).
- If Felound is found to have an "uncured material breach" (even a minor one that Zapeiv characterizes as material), it loses transition assistance entirely.

**Why it matters.** A water treatment plant cannot simply switch coagulants overnight. The 90-day window is a floor, not a ceiling, on the transition period Felound actually needs. Losing it due to a disputed "material breach" determination is a significant risk.

**Recommended fix.**

> "If this Agreement expires or terminates for any reason, Supplier will, at Customer's request, continue supplying Products at the then-current prices for up to one hundred eighty (180) days so that Customer can transition to a replacement supplier without interrupting treatment operations. This obligation applies regardless of the reason for termination, including termination for Customer's breach."

[Flag for client: confirm whether 180 days is realistic given the qualification timeline for a replacement PACl supplier.]

## Issue 9 – Emergency Delivery Standard Is Weaker Than Standard Delivery

**Where:** SOW § 4.4.

**Problem.** Standard deliveries are due within three business days of PO acceptance—an absolute obligation. Emergency deliveries (those "Customer designates as such") are subject only to "commercially reasonable efforts" to deliver within 24 hours. This means Zapeiv has no hard obligation for the very deliveries that matter most in an operational emergency (e.g., tank level drops below minimum, sudden demand spike).

**Why it matters.** The purpose of designating a delivery as "emergency" is to get product faster. If the contractual standard is only "commercially reasonable efforts," Zapeiv can argue it did everything it could and still miss the deadline without breach.

**Recommended fix.**

> "For emergency requests that Customer designates as such in writing, Supplier will deliver within twenty-four (24) hours. If Supplier cannot meet this deadline, it will notify Customer within four (4) hours of the request with the expected delivery time and the reason for the delay."

If Zapeiv resists an absolute 24-hour commitment, at minimum add: "Failure to deliver within forty-eight (48) hours of an emergency request constitutes a material breach of this SOW."

## Issue 10 – Liability Cap May Be Inadequate for Water-Utility Risks

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 10.2 (cap: greater of 12 months' payments or $2 million); § 10.3 (carve-outs).

**Problem.** The cap is the greater of trailing 12-month payments (~$256,000 at estimated volumes) or $2,000,000. The carve-outs in § 10.3 preserve indemnification obligations (§ 9), breach of confidentiality (§ 13), gross negligence/fraud/willful misconduct, and Customer's payment obligation. This means the cap applies to, e.g., ordinary negligence claims not rising to the indemnity level, breach of the supply commitment in § 4.2, or failure to provide transition assistance.

For a drinking-water utility, even ordinary negligence by the supplier (e.g., a loading error that introduces an off-spec product that doesn't trigger the warranty because it's within the "as in effect from time to time" data sheet) could result in regulatory penalties, public-health response costs, or plant downtime well in excess of $2 million.

**Why it matters.** The cap is a backstop. The indemnity and warranty provisions should catch most product-failure scenarios, but the gap between "ordinary negligence" and "gross negligence" is wide, and the $2 million ceiling may not cover a serious but non-grossly-negligent incident.

**Recommended fix.** Increase the cap or add a water-utility-specific carve-out:

> "The cap in Section 10.2 will not apply to any claim arising from a Product that causes or contributes to a violation of the Safe Drinking Water Act or New York State drinking-water regulations at any Facility."

Alternatively, raise the fixed-dollar floor from $2 million to $5 million.

## Issue 11 – Telemetry Data Ownership and Portability Not Addressed

**Where:** SOW § 5.2 (telemetry installed at Supplier's cost; Customer gets read-only access); § 6.1 (telemetry units remain Supplier's property); § 6.3 (removal on termination).

**Problem.** The SOW is silent on who owns the historical tank-level data generated by the telemetry system, whether Customer can export it, and what happens to the data when Supplier removes its equipment on termination. If Felound wants to hand historical usage data to a replacement supplier for transition planning, it may have no contractual right to do so.

**Why it matters.** Historical feed-rate data is valuable for right-sizing a replacement supplier's delivery schedule and for regulatory reporting. Losing access to it at termination creates an unnecessary transition gap.

**Recommended fix.** Add to SOW § 5.2:

> "All data generated by the telemetry system, including historical tank-level records, is the property of Customer. Upon expiration or termination of the Supply Agreement, Supplier will deliver to Customer a complete export of all such data in a standard, machine-readable format within fifteen (15) days."

## Issue 12 – Supply Commitment Tied to Non-Binding Forecast

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 4.2 (Supplier must maintain capacity for forecast + 20%); § 3.3 (forecasts are "non-binding" and "do not obligate Customer").

**Problem.** Zapeiv's supply-commitment obligation is calibrated to Felound's forecast. But the forecast is expressly non-binding. If Felound's actual usage runs 30% above forecast (e.g., due to a change in source-water quality increasing coagulant demand), Zapeiv's contractual capacity obligation was set at only forecast + 20%, and it may argue it is not in breach for failing to meet the higher actual demand.

**Why it matters.** The +20% buffer is meant to protect Felound against demand spikes. If the baseline (forecast) is non-binding and can be set low, the buffer is illusory.

**Recommended fix.** Decouple the supply commitment from the non-binding forecast:

> "During the Term, Supplier will maintain enough manufacturing capacity, inventory and logistics resources to meet Customer's actual requirements for the Products, as evidenced by accepted Purchase Orders, plus a safety stock equivalent to twenty percent (20%) of Customer's trailing twelve-month actual usage."
