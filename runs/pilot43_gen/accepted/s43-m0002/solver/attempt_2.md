# MEMORANDUM

**Re:** Zapeiv Chemical Corp. – PACl Supply Deal: Issues and Recommended Revisions

## Summary

The three-document package (Supply Agreement, Statement of Work, Order Form) contains several provisions that are materially adverse to Felound or internally inconsistent. The most serious problem is that the master Supply Agreement imposes a 100% exclusivity obligation covering *all* water treatment chemicals at *all* of Felound's facilities, while the SOW and Order Form scope the deal to a single product (PACl) at a single plant (Ostler Creek). Under the stated order of precedence, the broader master-agreement term controls. Additional issues include a unilateral "Specifications" definition, a 10-year auto-renewal structure with a narrow exit window, asymmetric assignment rights, and a force-majeure allocation that is inadequate for a drinking-water operation.

Issues are ranked below by significance.

---

## Tier 1 – Critical Issues

### 1. Exclusivity Scope: Master Agreement Locks in 100% of All Chemicals at All Facilities

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 4.1; Order Form § 4.1–4.3; Supply Agreement § 17.2 (Order of Precedence).

**Why it matters:** Supply Agreement § 4.1 provides:

> "During the Term, Customer shall purchase one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of **all Water Treatment Chemicals** for use at **any Facility** exclusively from Supplier, and shall not buy any Water Treatment Chemicals for use at any Facility from any third party."

"Water Treatment Chemicals" is defined in § 1.12 to include coagulants, flocculants, disinfectants, pH adjusters, and corrosion inhibitors. "Facility" is defined in § 1.3 to include any plant, pumping station, or related installation owned or operated by Customer or its Affiliates.

The Order Form (§ 4.1–4.3) narrows the exclusive product to PACl and the covered site to Ostler Creek. But § 17.2 of the Supply Agreement states that in case of conflict, the Supply Agreement prevails over the Order Form. As drafted, Felound would be contractually barred from buying *any* water treatment chemical—sodium hypochlorite, caustic soda, orthophosphate, ferric chloride, anything—from any other supplier at any of its plants for the duration of the term (potentially 10+ years with renewals).

This is almost certainly not the commercial deal the parties intended. Zapeiv is being engaged to supply PACl at one plant. The master-agreement language appears to be a template that was not conformed to the actual scope.

**Recommended fix:** Amend Supply Agreement § 4.1 to limit exclusivity to the Exclusive Product at the Covered Site, as defined in the applicable Order Form. Proposed language:

> "4.1 **Exclusive Supply.** During the Term, Customer shall purchase one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of the Exclusive Product for use at the Covered Site, as identified in the applicable Order Form, exclusively from Supplier, and shall not buy the Exclusive Product for use at the Covered Site from any third party. This Section 4.1 does not restrict Customer's purchase of other Water Treatment Chemicals or its purchase of the Exclusive Product for use at Facilities other than the Covered Site."

Add corresponding definitions for "Exclusive Product" and "Covered Site" in § 1 of the Supply Agreement, cross-referencing the Order Form.

### 2. "Specifications" Defined as Supplier's Data Sheets "As in Effect From Time to Time"

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 1.8; SOW § 3.1.

**Why it matters:** Section 1.8 defines:

> "**Specifications** means, for each Product, Supplier's standard product data sheets for that Product, **as in effect from time to time**."

This makes the product requirements a moving target controlled unilaterally by Supplier. If Zapeiv revises its data sheet to lower the minimum Al₂O₃ content, widen the basicity range, or drop the NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 reference, the "Specifications" change automatically—without Felound's consent. The SOW § 3.1 sets specific requirements (NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certified, minimum 10.0% Al₂O₃, 70% basicity), but under the order of precedence (§ 17.2), the master agreement's definition of "Specifications" governs the term wherever it appears, including in the rejection and warranty provisions.

For a drinking-water utility, the chemical specification is a regulatory matter. NYSDOH and the SDWA require that treatment chemicals meet specific standards. Felound cannot accept a product whose compliance criteria can be altered unilaterally by the supplier.

**Recommended fix:** Amend § 1.8 to lock in the specifications as set forth in the applicable SOW and to require mutual written agreement for any change. Proposed language:

> "1.8 '**Specifications**' means, for each Product, the product requirements set forth in the applicable Statement of Work as in effect on the Effective Date. The Specifications may be amended only by written agreement of the Parties."

Alternatively, if Supplier insists on the ability to update data sheets, add: "Any update to Supplier's standard product data sheets that would result in a material reduction in product performance or compliance with Applicable Law shall not constitute a change to the Specifications without Customer's prior written consent."

### 3. Auto-Renewal for Successive Five-Year Terms with a 30-Day Exit Window

**Where:** Order Form §§ 2.2, 2.3.

**Why it matters:** The Initial Term is five years (§ 2.1). At the end of the Initial Term, the agreement "will renew automatically for successive five-year terms" (§ 2.2). The only way out is a non-renewal notice given "no earlier than 120 and no later than 90 days before the then-current term expires" (§ 2.3). That is a 30-day window.

If Felound misses that window—even by one day—it is locked in for another five years. Over time, this creates a de facto perpetual agreement. For a municipal water authority that may face budget constraints, regulatory changes, or the desire to re-bid, a 10-year (or 15-year, or 20-year) lock-in with a 30-day exit window is a significant risk.

**Recommended fix:** Two changes:

(a) Widen the non-renewal notice window. Proposed: "no earlier than 180 and no later than 120 days before the then-current term expires" (a 60-day window).

(b) Add a cap on the number of renewal terms. Proposed: "The Supply Agreement shall renew for up to two (2) Renewal Terms. Thereafter, it expires unless the Parties agree in writing to extend."

Alternatively, if Supplier insists on unlimited renewals, at minimum add a price-reset mechanism at each renewal (e.g., price reverts to then-market or is subject to re-negotiation) so that the renewal is not on stale economics.

## Tier 2 – Significant Issues

### 4. Asymmetric Assignment Rights

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 15.2.

**Why it matters:** Customer may not assign without Supplier's consent, but:

> "Supplier may assign or transfer this Agreement, in whole or in part, **without the consent of Customer**, to any Affiliate or to any successor to all or part of its business."

This means Zapeiv could sell its water-treatment-chemicals business to a competitor or an unknown entity, and Felound would be contractually bound to the new owner on the same terms. For a drinking-water supplier, the identity and financial strength of the counterparty matters. Felound has no right to object, terminate, or renegotiate.

**Recommended fix:** Require Customer consent for Supplier's assignment, with a carve-out for assignments to Affiliates that maintain equivalent financial capacity. Proposed language:

> "Supplier may not assign or transfer this Agreement, in whole or in part, without Customer's prior written consent, which consent shall not be unreasonably withheld. Notwithstanding the foregoing, Supplier may assign this Agreement to an Affiliate without Customer's consent, provided that the Affiliate assumes all of Supplier's obligations hereunder and provides Customer with evidence of financial capacity (including insurance) at least equivalent to Supplier's on the Effective Date. Any assignment to a non-Affiliate successor in a sale of all or substantially all of Supplier's business requires Customer's prior written consent."

### 5. Force Majeure: Pro Rata Allocation Is Inadequate for a Drinking-Water Plant

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 14.2.

**Why it matters:** During a Force Majeure Event affecting Supplier, the only allocation obligation is:

> "Supplier will allocate its available supply to Customer **at least pro rata** with its other customers."

If Zapeiv serves 10 water utilities and a force majeure event cuts capacity by 50%, Felound receives 50% of its needs. A drinking-water treatment plant cannot operate at 50% coagulant dose—treatment efficiency drops, turbidity rises, and the plant risks violating NYSDOH compliance limits. The "at least pro rata" language gives Supplier no incentive to prioritize Felound.

**Recommended fix:** Add a priority obligation. Proposed language:

> "During a Force Majeure Event, Supplier will allocate its available supply to Customer in proportion to Customer's confirmed Purchase Orders relative to Supplier's total confirmed orders, but in no event less than eighty percent (80%) of Customer's confirmed requirements. If Supplier cannot meet this threshold, Supplier will notify Customer immediately and the Parties will work in good faith to identify alternative supply. Customer's rights under Section 4.3 (Supply Shortfall) apply to any shortfall below 100% of confirmed requirements."

---

### 6. Supply Commitment Tied to Non-Binding Forecast Creates a Coverage Gap

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§ 3.3, 4.1, 4.2.

**Why it matters:** Section 4.2 requires Supplier to maintain capacity for "Customer's forecasted requirements under Section 3.3, plus twenty percent (20%)." But § 3.3 makes the forecast "non-binding" and states it "does not obligate Customer to purchase any quantity." Meanwhile, § 4.1 (as currently drafted) obligates Customer to buy 100% from Supplier.

The gap: if Felound's actual demand exceeds forecast + 20% (e.g., due to a change in raw water quality, a population growth spike, or a plant expansion), Supplier has no contractual obligation to cover the excess. Yet under § 4.1, Felound cannot buy the shortfall from a third party without breaching the exclusivity clause. Section 4.3 (Supply Shortfall) only triggers if Supplier "cannot or does not deliver conforming Products against an **accepted** Purchase Order"—it does not cover the situation where Supplier simply has no capacity because the order exceeded forecast + 20%.

Even if Issue 1 is fixed (narrowing exclusivity to PACl at Ostler Creek), this gap still matters: Felound would be forced to either (a) accept delivery delays or (b) buy from a third party and pay the § 4.3 reimbursement differential, which is administratively burdensome.

**Recommended fix:** Add a firm minimum supply obligation. Proposed language:

> "4.2 **Supply Commitment.** During the Term, Supplier will maintain enough manufacturing capacity, inventory and logistics resources to meet Customer's forecasted requirements under Section 3.3, plus twenty percent (20%) of that forecast. In addition, Supplier will use commercially reasonable efforts to accommodate Purchase Orders for quantities up to one hundred fifty percent (150%) of the most recent forecast. If Supplier cannot deliver against a Purchase Order for quantities exceeding forecast plus twenty percent (20%), Supplier will notify Customer within one (1) business day of acceptance, and the Parties will negotiate in good faith an alternative delivery schedule."

### 7. Transition Assistance Period (90 Days) May Be Insufficient

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 12.4.

**Why it matters:** On expiration or termination (other than for Customer's uncured material breach), Supplier will continue supplying for "up to ninety (90) days." For a drinking-water utility, transitioning to a new coagulant supplier involves:

- Identifying and qualifying an alternate supplier with NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certification;
- Conducting jar tests and pilot runs to confirm treatment performance;
- Notifying NYSDOH and obtaining any required approvals;
- Setting up logistics, tank compatibility, and safety data sheets.

This process can easily exceed 90 days, particularly if the alternate product is a different chemistry (e.g., switching from PACl to ferric chloride). If the 90-day bridge expires before Felound is ready, it faces a supply gap at a drinking-water plant.

**Recommended fix:** Extend the transition period and add a tail. Proposed language:

> "12.4 **Transition Assistance.** If this Agreement expires or terminates for any reason other than Customer's uncured material breach, Supplier will, at Customer's request, continue supplying Products at the then-current prices for up to one hundred eighty (180) days. If Customer demonstrates that it has initiated a good-faith transition to a replacement supplier but has not completed it within 180 days, the Parties will negotiate in good faith an extension of up to an additional ninety (90) days on the same terms."

### 8. Basicity Specification Lacks a Tolerance or Range

**Where:** SOW § 3.1.

**Why it matters:** The specification reads: "NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certified PACl, minimum 10.0% Al₂O₃, **70% basicity**." This is ambiguous. Is 70% the minimum? The target? The exact required value? NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 for PACl typically specifies basicity as a range (e.g., 70–90%). If the intent is "minimum 70%," that should be stated. If the intent is "70% ± 2%," that should be stated. As written, a delivery at 75% basicity could be argued to either conform or not conform, depending on interpretation.

This matters because basicity directly affects coagulant performance, dosage, and sludge characteristics. A shift in basicity can change treatment efficiency at the plant.

**Recommended fix:** Clarify the basicity specification. Proposed language:

> "3.1 **PACl Product Requirements.** Each delivery of the PACl Product must meet the following requirements: NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certified PACl, minimum 10.0% Al₂O₃ (as determined by the certificate of analysis), basicity of 70% ± 3% (i.e., 67% to 73%), unless Customer and Supplier agree in writing to a different range for a specific lot."

**Question for client:** What basicity range does Felound's current treatment process require? The plant operators should confirm the acceptable range based on their jar test data and current operating experience.

## Tier 3 – Moderate Issues

### 9. Telemetry Failure Restoration Period Creates Inventory Blind Spot

**Where:** SOW § 6.1; SOW § 4.4.

**Why it matters:** If a telemetry unit fails, Supplier has three (3) business days to restore it. Delivery lead time is also three (3) business days (§ 4.4). In the worst case, Felound could be blind on tank levels for 3 days while a delivery is in transit for another 3 days—a 6-day window with no visibility. Combined with the minimum inventory floor of 3,000 gallons (≈ 6 days of supply at the estimated 180,000 gal/year rate), this is a tight margin.

**Recommended fix:** Add a manual check obligation during telemetry outages. Proposed language:

> "If a telemetry unit is out of service, Supplier will perform a manual tank-level check at the Plant within twenty-four (24) hours and will notify the Plant's operations supervisor of the reading. Supplier will continue manual checks daily until the telemetry unit is restored."

---

### 10. Minimum Inventory Floor Is Low for a Drinking-Water Plant

**Where:** SOW § 5.4.

**Why it matters:** The floor is 3,000 gallons. At an estimated 180,000 gallons/year (≈ 493 gallons/day), this represents roughly 6 days of supply. If a delivery is delayed (weather, truck breakdown, Supplier issue), Felound could hit the floor with no buffer. For a drinking-water plant, running out of coagulant is a compliance event.

**Recommended fix:** Raise the minimum inventory floor. Proposed: "Supplier will schedule deliveries so that combined PACl inventory at the Plant does not fall below 5,000 gallons." This provides approximately 10 days of buffer.

**Question for client:** What is the plant's actual average daily feed rate, and what is the maximum daily rate during peak turbidity events? The minimum inventory should be set based on peak (not average) usage.

---

### 11. Emergency Delivery Is Only "Commercially Reasonable Efforts"

**Where:** SOW § 4.4.

**Why it matters:** For emergency requests, Supplier will "use commercially reasonable efforts to deliver within twenty-four (24) hours." This is not a firm commitment. If the emergency is a tank running low due to a telemetry failure or an unexpected turbidity spike, Felound needs a guarantee, not an effort.

**Recommended fix:** Make the 24-hour emergency delivery a firm obligation with a defined trigger. Proposed language:

> "4.4 **Lead Time.** Supplier will deliver within three (3) business days after acceptance of a Purchase Order. If Customer designates a Purchase Order as an emergency (by telephone or email to Supplier's account manager, confirmed in writing within four (4) hours), Supplier will deliver within twenty-four (24) hours of the designation. Supplier will not reject an emergency Purchase Order under Section 3.2 of the Supply Agreement."

---

### 12. No Interest on Supplier's Late Refunds or Credits

**Where:** Supply Agreement § 5.5; § 6.3.

**Why it matters:** Section 5.5 charges Customer 1% per month on late undisputed payments. But there is no corresponding provision requiring Supplier to pay interest on late refunds, credits, or reimbursement amounts (e.g., the § 4.3 supply-shortfall reimbursement or the § 6.3 rejection costs). If Supplier delays a refund, Customer has no contractual lever.

**Recommended fix:** Add a reciprocal interest provision. Proposed language:

> "5.6 **Late Refunds and Reimbursements.** Any amount that Supplier is obligated to refund, credit, or reimburse to Customer under this Agreement and that is not paid within fifteen (15) days after the obligation arises will accrue interest at the same rate provided in Section 5.5."

---

### 13. On-Site Jar Testing Limited to Two Visits Per Year

**Where:** SOW § 8.4.

**Why it matters:** Supplier provides on-site jar testing "up to two (2) times per calendar year at no additional charge." If raw water quality shifts seasonally (e.g., algal blooms in summer, high turbidity in spring runoff), Felound may need more frequent dosage optimization. Two visits per year may be insufficient, and additional visits would presumably be billable (the SOW is silent on the rate).

**Recommended fix:** Increase the included visits or clarify the rate for additional visits. Proposed language:

> "Supplier will provide on-site jar testing support at the Plant up to four (4) times per calendar year at no additional charge. Additional on-site jar testing will be provided at Supplier's then-standard professional services rate, which Supplier will disclose to Customer in writing upon request. Telephone support remains unlimited during business hours."

## Questions for the Client

The following cannot be resolved from the documents alone:

1. **Facility count and chemical portfolio.** How many facilities does Felound operate, and what water treatment chemicals does it currently purchase and from whom? This determines the practical impact of the § 4.1 exclusivity issue and whether the fix proposed in Issue 1 is sufficient.

2. **Regulatory transition timeline.** What is the NYSDOH process and typical timeline for changing a primary coagulant at a permitted drinking-water treatment plant? This informs whether the 90-day (or proposed 180-day) transition period in Issue 7 is adequate.

3. **Actual feed rates.** What is the plant's average and peak daily PACl feed rate? The SOW's minimum inventory floor (Issue 10) and the telemetry blind-spot analysis (Issue 9) depend on this.

4. **Basicity tolerance.** What basicity range does the current treatment process tolerate? The specification in Issue 8 should be set based on plant operating data, not just the NSF standard.

5. **Insurance adequacy.** The $10M pollution legal liability limit in Supply Agreement § 11.1(c) appears appropriate, but the client should confirm whether its own risk management or bonding requirements call for a higher limit or additional coverage (e.g., contractual liability, umbrella).
