**Re:** Zapeiv Chemical Corp. Supply Agreement – Issue List for Felound Regional Water Authority Services, Inc.

## Summary

I reviewed the three-document deal package (Supply Agreement, Statement of Work, and Order Form) for the proposed polaluminum chloride (PACl) supply arrangement between Felound and Zapeiv. The documents contain several significant issues, the most critical of which is a scope conflict that, as drafted, would lock Felound into an exclusive purchasing obligation far broader than the commercial deal actually contemplates. Below are the issues in order of significance.

---

## 1. Exclusivity Scope Conflict – Supply Agreement §4.1 vs. Order Form §4.1–4.2

**Where:** Supply Agreement §4.1; Order Form §§4.1, 4.2, 4.3; SOW §2.2.

**Issue:** Supply Agreement §4.1 requires Customer to purchase "one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of **all Water Treatment Chemicals** for use at **any Facility** exclusively from Supplier." The defined term "Water Treatment Chemicals" (§1.12) sweeps in coagulants, flocculants, disinfectants, pH adjusters, and corrosion inhibitors. "Facility" (§1.3) means any water treatment plant, pumping station, or related installation owned or operated by Customer or its Affiliates.

The Order Form, by contrast, limits the deal to a single product and a single site: §4.1 identifies the "Exclusive Product" as PACl coagulant only; §4.2 identifies the "Covered Site" as the Ostler Creek Water Treatment Plant only. The SOW (§2.2) confirms the single-site scope.

Under the order of precedence in Supply Agreement §17.2, the Supply Agreement controls over the Order Form. As drafted, Felound would be contractually barred from buying disinfectants, pH adjusters, corrosion inhibitors, or any other water treatment chemical from any other supplier at any of its plants for the duration of the Term (five years, auto-renewing in five-year blocks).

**Why it matters:** This is almost certainly not the commercial deal Felound is trying to make. Felound operates multiple municipal plants (the SOW covers only one). Locking 100% of all chemical categories at all facilities to a single supplier for five-plus years eliminates Felound's ability to shop, hedge against supply disruption, or respond to price changes in categories unrelated to PACl. It also exposes Felound to significant leverage risk: if Zapeiv raises prices on PACl, Felound has no credible threat to source elsewhere for its other chemicals.

**Recommended fix:** Conform Supply Agreement §4.1 to the scope actually negotiated. Replace the current language with:

> "During the Term, Customer shall purchase one hundred percent (100%) of its requirements of the Exclusive Product for use at the Covered Site exclusively from Supplier, and shall not buy the Exclusive Product for use at the Covered Site from any third party."

Then define "Exclusive Product" and "Covered Site" by reference to the Order Form (as §4.3 of the Order Form already does). Delete or narrow the "any Facility" and "all Water Treatment Chemicals" language.

## 2. "Specifications" Defined as Supplier's Data Sheets "As in Effect From Time to Time" – Supply Agreement §1.8

**Where:** Supply Agreement §1.8; SOW §3.1.

**Issue:** Supply Agreement §1.8 defines "Specifications" as "Supplier's standard product data sheets for that Product, **as in effect from time to time**." This means the product standard against which every delivery is measured (§6.3 rejection right; §7.1 warranty) can be changed unilaterally by Supplier at any time, without Customer consent.

SOW §3.1 sets out fixed, objective requirements: NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certification, minimum 10.0% Al₂O₃, 70% basicity. But under the §17.2 precedence rule, the Supply Agreement's floating definition of "Specifications" governs. Supplier could issue a revised data sheet lowering the Al₂O₃ minimum or changing the basicity target, and the new numbers would become the contractual standard for all future deliveries.

**Why it matters:** For a drinking-water utility, the chemical specification is not a commercial preference—it is tied to treatment performance, regulatory compliance (NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 is a health-based standard), and process stability. Unilateral spec changes could degrade treatment performance, increase sludge volumes, or create regulatory exposure, all without Felound's agreement.

**Recommended fix:** Amend §1.8 to lock the specification at signing and require mutual written consent for changes:

> "'Specifications' means, for each Product, the product requirements set forth in the applicable Statement of Work as of the Effective Date. The Specifications may be changed only by written agreement of both Parties."

Alternatively, if Supplier's data sheets are to remain the reference, add: "provided that no change to the Specifications shall reduce any performance parameter stated in the Statement of Work without Customer's prior written consent."

## 3. Consequential Damages Exclusion Inadequate for a Public Water Supplier – Supply Agreement §10.1

**Where:** Supply Agreement §§10.1, 10.2, 10.3; §9.1.

**Issue:** Section 10.1 excludes all indirect, incidental, consequential, special, and punitive damages, and lost profits. Section 10.2 caps total liability at the greater of trailing 12-month payments or $2,000,000. Section 10.3 carves out indemnification obligations (§9), confidentiality breaches (§13), gross negligence/fraud/willful misconduct, and payment obligations.

The gap: if Supplier delivers PACl that is contaminated or substandard through ordinary (not gross) negligence, and the result is a boil-water advisory, emergency water trucking, public health notification, regulatory investigation, or treatment process damage, much of that cost would be characterized as consequential or indirect. The §9.1 indemnity covers *third-party* claims (e.g., a resident sues Felound), but Felound's own operational costs—alternative water supply, process re-start, regulatory response labor—are not third-party claims and would fall inside the §10.1 exclusion.

**Why it matters:** Felound is a municipal drinking-water provider. The single worst-case scenario in this deal is nonconforming or contaminated chemical reaching the distribution system. The financial and reputational consequences of that event (boil-water notices, public trust erosion, regulatory sanctions) can far exceed $2 million. The current structure leaves Felound bearing that risk.

**Recommended fix:** Add a carve-out in §10.3 for the specific risks of this deal:

> "Sections 10.1 and 10.2 do not apply to: … (e) Customer's reasonable costs of responding to a contamination event, boil-water advisory, or regulatory enforcement action caused by a Product that breaches the warranty in Section 7.1 or the Specifications; or (f) Customer's costs of obtaining substitute water supply during any period in which Products are unavailable or nonconforming."

At minimum, raise the cap in §10.2 to a figure that reflects the real-world exposure (e.g., $10,000,000, aligned with the pollution legal liability insurance limit in §11.1(c)).

## 4. Automatic Five-Year Renewals with a 30-Day Non-Renewal Window – Order Form §§2.2, 2.3

**Where:** Order Form §§2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4.

**Issue:** The Initial Term is five years (§2.1). At the end of each term, the agreement auto-renews for another five years (§2.2). The only way out is a non-renewal notice delivered in a window that is exactly 30 days wide: no earlier than 120 and no later than 90 days before the current term expires (§2.3). If Felound misses that window by a single day, it is locked in for another five years on the then-current terms.

**Why it matters:** Five-year auto-renewals with a 30-day notice window create a severe administrative trap. Felound would need a reliable internal calendar and approval process triggered more than two years before each renewal date. For a public entity with procurement cycles, budget approvals, and potential changes in management, the risk of missing the window is real. The consequence—five more years of exclusivity at a single plant with a single supplier—is disproportionate to the administrative error.

**Recommended fix:** Two options (either or both):

- **Widen the window.** Change §2.3 to: "no earlier than 180 and no later than 60 days before the then-current term expires." This gives a 120-day window.
- **Shorten the renewal term.** Change §2.2 to one-year renewal terms instead of five-year. This reduces the stakes of missing the notice window and gives Felound more frequent re-evaluation points.

Additionally, consider adding: "If a Party fails to give timely non-renewal notice, the other Party may, within thirty (30) days after the expiration of the notice window, provide the first Party with written notice of the missed deadline, and the first Party shall have an additional thirty (30) days to cure by delivering the non-renewal notice."

## 5. Asymmetric Assignment Rights – Supply Agreement §15.2

**Where:** Supply Agreement §15.2.

**Issue:** Customer may not assign without Supplier's prior written consent. Supplier may assign "in whole or in part, without the consent of Customer, to any Affiliate or to any successor to all or part of its business."

**Why it matters:** Felound is entering a multi-year exclusive supply relationship with a specific manufacturer based on its quality systems, NSF certification, and operational track record. If Zapeiv is acquired by, or merges into, an entity with a different quality culture, smaller scale, or weaker financial position, Felound has no say. The "successor to all or part of its business" language is particularly broad—it could capture a carve-out sale of Zapeiv's water-treatment-chemicals division to an unknown buyer.

**Recommended fix:** Make assignment mutual and add a materiality standard:

> "Neither Party may assign this Agreement without the other Party's prior written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld. Notwithstanding the foregoing, Supplier may assign this Agreement to a successor in interest to all or substantially all of Supplier's business, provided that the successor (a) is a entity of at least equivalent financial and operational capability, (b) assumes all of Supplier's obligations under the Supply Documents in writing, and (c) holds current NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certification for the Products. Customer may assign this Agreement to an Affiliate or to a successor in interest to all or substantially all of Customer's business without Supplier's consent."

## 6. Transition Assistance Period May Be Insufficient – Supply Agreement §12.4

**Where:** Supply Agreement §12.4; SOW §3.2 (NSF certification requirement).

**Issue:** If the agreement ends (other than for Customer's uncured breach), Supplier must continue supplying for up to 90 days at then-current prices. For a water utility, 90 days may not be enough to: (a) identify and qualify a replacement coagulant supplier; (b) obtain NSF/ANSI/CAN 60 certification for the substitute product (if it is not already certified); (c) complete jar testing and pilot trials; (d) obtain any required regulatory approvals or notifications; and (e) execute a new supply agreement.

**Why it matters:** If the transition period expires before Felound has a qualified alternative, Felound faces either a supply gap at a drinking-water plant or the need to negotiate with Zapeiv from a position of urgency—undermining the purpose of the exit.

**Recommended fix:** Extend the transition period and add a qualification-support obligation:

> "If this Agreement expires or terminates for any reason other than Customer's uncured material breach, Supplier will, at Customer's request, continue supplying Products at the then-current prices for up to one hundred eighty (180) days so that Customer can move to a replacement supplier without interrupting treatment operations. During the transition period, Supplier will provide reasonable cooperation to support Customer's qualification of a replacement supplier, including providing historical performance data, lot records, and technical documentation as reasonably requested."

## 7. Liability Cap May Be Inadequate – Supply Agreement §10.2

**Where:** Supply Agreement §10.2; §11.1(c).

**Issue:** The cap is the greater of trailing 12-month payments or $2,000,000. At the estimated volume of 180,000 gallons/year at $1.42/gallon (Order Form §§3.1, 3.5), annual spend is approximately $255,600. So the cap would be $2,000,000 for the foreseeable future. Meanwhile, Supplier is required to carry $10,000,000 in pollution legal liability insurance (§11.1(c)), suggesting the parties themselves recognize that a pollution event could generate $10M+ in exposure.

**Why it matters:** The mismatch between the insurance requirement and the liability cap means that in a serious contamination event, Felound's contractual recovery is capped at $2M while the actual loss (and the insurance available to cover it) is an order of magnitude higher. Felound would be forced to pursue the insurance carrier directly, which is slower and less certain than a contractual claim.

**Recommended fix:** Align the cap with the insurance:

> "EACH PARTY'S TOTAL LIABILITY ARISING OUT OF THIS AGREEMENT WILL NOT EXCEED THE GREATER OF (a) THE AMOUNTS PAID AND PAYABLE BY CUSTOMER UNDER THIS AGREEMENT DURING THE TWELVE (12) MONTHS BEFORE THE EVENT GIVING RISE TO THE CLAIM AND (b) TEN MILLION DOLLARS ($10,000,000)."

## 8. Inventory Replenishment Mechanism Has a Gap – SOW §§5.3, 5.4

**Where:** SOW §§5.3, 5.4; Supply Agreement §3.2.

**Issue:** Section 5.3 provides that when combined tank inventory hits 6,000 gallons, Supplier notifies Customer and "proposes a delivery date." Customer then issues a Purchase Order. Section 5.4 obligates Supplier to "schedule deliveries so that combined PACl inventory at the Plant does not fall below 3,000 gallons." But the mechanism for achieving §5.4 depends on Customer issuing the PO. If Customer's operations team is slow to issue the PO (e.g., due to internal approval delays, staffing gaps, or a dispute about quantity), inventory can drop below 3,000 gallons through no fault of Supplier's—and yet §5.4 is worded as an obligation on Supplier.

Conversely, if Supplier simply does not deliver on time after the PO is accepted, §5.4 gives Customer a breach claim, but the remedy is unclear (the SOW does not specify a service level or penalty for falling below the minimum).

**Why it matters:** Running below 3,000 gallons at a plant using ~180,000 gallons/year (roughly 493 gallons/day) means less than 6 days of supply. A further delay risks running the tank dry, which would interrupt drinking-water treatment.

**Recommended fix:** Add an auto-delivery right or a deemed-PO mechanism:

> "If Customer does not issue a Purchase Order within two (2) business days after Supplier's replenishment notification under this Section 5.3, Supplier may deliver the proposed quantity to the Plant, and Customer shall pay for the delivered Product at the unit price in the Order Form. Supplier will use commercially reasonable efforts to ensure that combined inventory does not fall below 3,000 gallons. If inventory falls below 3,000 gallons due to Supplier's failure to deliver against an accepted Purchase Order, Supplier will [specify remedy: e.g., deliver emergency replacement within 24 hours at no additional charge, and reimburse Customer for any resulting operational costs]."

## 9. Rejection Period May Be Too Short for Latent Defects – Supply Agreement §6.3

**Where:** Supply Agreement §6.3.

**Issue:** Customer must reject nonconforming product within 10 business days of delivery, or within 10 business days of *discovering* a nonconformity that reasonable inspection would not reveal. The second limb helps, but "discovery" is fact-intensive and can be disputed. For a coagulant, some problems (e.g., gradual build-up of undesirable byproducts, inconsistent basicity across a tank that only manifests during low-flow conditions) may take weeks to identify operationally.

**Why it matters:** If the rejection window closes before Felound identifies a problem, it loses its contractual right to reject and must rely on the warranty (§7.1) and indemnity (§9.1) instead, which have different procedural requirements and may be subject to the liability cap.

**Recommended fix:** Extend the discovery period:

> "…within ten (10) business days after delivery or, for nonconformities that reasonable inspection would not reveal, within thirty (30) days after discovering the nonconformity, provided that in no event later than the expiration of the shelf life stated on the certificate of analysis for the applicable lot."

## 10. Force Majeure Allocation May Leave Customer Short – Supply Agreement §14.2

**Where:** Supply Agreement §14.2.

**Issue:** During a Force Majeure Event affecting Supplier, Supplier must "allocate its available supply to Customer at least pro rata with its other customers." If Zapeiv serves many municipal and industrial customers and a disruption (e.g., a raw material shortage, a plant fire) reduces its output by 50%, Felound gets 50% of its pro rata share. That may not be enough to keep the Ostler Creek plant running.

**Why it matters:** A drinking-water plant cannot operate at 50% coagulant dose. Under-dosing leads to turbidity violations and potential MCL exceedances. Pro rata allocation is a fair commercial principle for general suppliers but is inadequate for a critical-infrastructure input.

**Recommended fix:** Add a critical-customer priority:

> "During a Force Majeure Event, Supplier will allocate its available supply to Customer in an amount sufficient to maintain the minimum inventory level stated in Section 5.4 of the Statement of Work, before allocating supply to other customers. Any remaining available supply will be allocated pro rata among Supplier's other customers."

## 11. Specifications Change Notification – SOW §3.4

**Where:** SOW §3.4.

**Issue:** Supplier must give 60 days' notice of a change in manufacturing location, raw material source, or process. This is good, but the clause does not state what happens if Customer objects to the change. As drafted, 60 days' notice appears to be the entire obligation—after that, the change proceeds.

**Why it matters:** A change in raw material source or process could alter the product's performance characteristics even if it still meets the stated specs (e.g., different impurity profile, different polymerization distribution). For a water utility, an untested change in coagulant source can affect floc formation, sludge dewatering, and byproduct levels.

**Recommended fix:** Add a consent right:

> "Supplier will give Customer at least sixty (60) days' prior written notice of any change in the manufacturing location, raw material source, or manufacturing process for the PACl Product. Customer may, within thirty (30) days after receiving such notice, object to the change in writing if Customer reasonably determines that the change could affect Product performance or regulatory compliance. If Customer objects, Supplier will either (a) provide additional data or testing results sufficient to address Customer's concern, or (b) continue manufacturing under the prior process for so long as Customer remains a customer under the Supply Documents."

## 12. Order of Precedence Amplifies the Above Issues – Supply Agreement §17.2

**Where:** Supply Agreement §17.2.

**Issue:** The precedence order is: Supply Agreement → SOW → Order Form. This means the broadest, most Supplier-favorable definitions in the master agreement (e.g., "Specifications" in §1.8, exclusivity in §4.1) override the more specific, Customer-favorable terms in the SOW and Order Form. Several of the issues above (¶¶1, 2) are direct consequences of this structure.

**Why it matters:** The precedence rule is standard, but it makes it essential that the master agreement's definitions and obligations are conformed to the actual deal scope. As drafted, the master agreement is written for a much larger, broader relationship than the one the SOW and Order Form describe.

**Recommended fix:** In addition to the specific fixes above, add a savings clause:

> "To the extent any provision of the Supply Agreement is broader in scope than the Exclusive Product or the Covered Site as defined in the Order Form, such provision shall be deemed limited to the Exclusive Product and the Covered Site."

## Questions for the Client

The following cannot be resolved from the documents alone:

1. **Plant count and chemical categories.** How many facilities does Felound operate, and what categories of water treatment chemicals does it currently purchase? This determines the real-world impact of the §4.1 exclusivity overreach and whether Zapeiv is even capable of supplying all categories.

2. **Jury trial waiver policy.** Does Felound, as a New York municipal entity, have a policy or legal constraint regarding jury trial waivers (Supply Agreement §16.4)? Some public authorities are prohibited from waiving certain constitutional rights by contract.

3. **Replacement supplier qualification timeline.** Based on Felound's experience, how long does it typically take to qualify a new coagulant supplier (NSF certification, jar testing, pilot runs, regulatory notification)? This bears on whether 90 days (current) or 180 days (proposed) is the right transition period.

4. **Current chemical spend and sourcing.** What is Felound's current annual spend on water treatment chemicals across all plants, and from how many suppliers? This helps assess whether Zapeiv's broad exclusivity ask was a negotiating position or a drafting error, and whether Felound would actually be giving up meaningful sourcing flexibility.

5. **Telemetry data ownership.** The SOW (§5.2) gives Customer "read-only access to the telemetry portal." Upon termination, Supplier removes its equipment (§6.3). Does Felound need historical tank-level data for its own records or regulatory reporting? If so, the SOW should require Supplier to export and deliver all historical data before removing the telemetry units.

## Note on the Ostrevka Agreement

The matter folder also contains a Laboratory Instrument Maintenance Agreement between Felound and Ostrevka Analytical Instruments LLC (effective January 15, 2026). This is a separate commercial relationship covering maintenance of Felound's central water quality laboratory instruments. It does not create direct issues in the Zapeiv deal package, but it is tangentially relevant: SOW §8.2 contemplates that "Customer's laboratory" will perform acceptance testing on Zapeiv deliveries. If the instruments used for that testing (e.g., titrators for basicity, ICP for aluminum content) are covered under the Ostrevka agreement, a prolonged instrument outage could impair Felound's ability to exercise its rejection rights under Supply Agreement §6.3. I would recommend confirming with the client that the Ostrevka SLAs (particularly the 5-business-day restore target in §3.2(c) and the loaner provision in §3.5) are adequate to support the Zapeiv acceptance-testing workflow.
