## CRITICAL ISSUES

### 1. Escrow Agreement § 4.6 Caps All Recovery at $4.85 Million – Contradicts APA § 9.7 and the LOI

**Where:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement, § 4.6 ("Exclusive Source of Recovery"); APA, § 9.7 ("Payments; Escrow") and § 11.10 ("Order of Precedence"); LOI, § 6.

**The problem.** Escrow Agreement § 4.6 provides:

> "The Escrow Fund is Buyer's sole and exclusive source of recovery for all Losses of the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **including Losses arising from Excluded Liabilities**."

APA § 9.7 provides the opposite:

> "Losses payable to a Buyer Indemnitee under this Article IX shall be satisfied **first** from the Escrow Fund, to the extent available, in accordance with the Escrow Agreement, **and thereafter directly by Seller**."

APA § 11.10 states that in a conflict, "the Escrow Agreement shall control with respect to matters concerning the Escrow Fund." Section 4.6 is squarely a matter concerning the Escrow Fund, so it would override § 9.7.

The practical effect: Givand's maximum recovery for *any* post-closing indemnification claim—breach of reps, Excluded Liabilities, Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities—is $4,850,000. Once the escrow is released at 18 months (Escrow Agreement § 3.1), Givand has **no recourse at all** against Seller, even for a multi-million-dollar environmental cleanup that manifests in year three.

This directly contradicts the LOI, § 6, which states the cap "would not apply to fraud, breaches of fundamental representations, or retained liabilities, including pre-closing environmental liabilities." The APA's own § 9.5(b) carves Excluded Liabilities out of the cap, but the Escrow Agreement's "sole and exclusive source" language renders that carve-out meaningless.

**Why it matters.** The Facility has operated a battery recycling and refining plant since at least 2021. The Disclosure Schedule (§ 3.11) discloses only two Phase I ESAs (2020 and 2026) and states "No other exceptions." Phase I ESAs are desk reviews; they do not confirm the absence of contamination. For a facility handling lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese, electrolyte solutions, solvents and acids (all defined as Hazardous Materials in APA § 1.1), the risk of a latent environmental condition is real. A single significant Release could generate cleanup costs far exceeding $4.85 million.

**Recommended fix.** Delete § 4.6 of the Escrow Agreement in its entirety, or replace it with language that tracks APA § 9.7:

> "The Escrow Fund is the first source of recovery for Losses under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement. To the extent the Escrow Fund is insufficient to satisfy a Loss, the indemnified Party may pursue the indemnifying Party directly for the balance. Nothing in this Agreement limits or caps the indemnifying Party's obligations under the Purchase Agreement."

**Negotiating position.** This is non-negotiable from Givand's perspective. The LOI already agreed that the cap does not apply to Excluded Liabilities. Seller's counsel likely drafted § 4.6 to protect the Escrow Agent from interpleader risk, but the standard solution is to make the escrow the *first* source of recovery, not the *sole* source. If Seller resists, a fallback is to carve Excluded Liabilities and Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities out of § 4.6, so the "sole source" language applies only to general rep breaches subject to the cap.

### 2. APA § 1.1 Defines "Business" to Exclude Refining – Contradicts the LOI, the Disclosure Schedule, and Givand's Stated Deal Rationale

**Where:** APA, § 1.1 (definition of "Business"); LOI, §§ 1 and 3; Disclosure Schedule, § 2 (Schedule 2.1(a), Item 7) and § 8 (Schedule 3.9, Item 1).

**The problem.** APA § 1.1 defines:

> "**Business** means the business of collection, discharge and mechanical processing of spent lithium-ion batteries into black mass, **excluding any refining of black mass**, as conducted by Seller at the Facility as of the date of this Agreement."

The LOI, § 1, defines Business as "the business of collection, discharge and shredding of spent lithium-ion batteries **and hydrometallurgical refining of black mass**." LOI § 3 states explicitly: "Buyer's interest in the Transaction rests on acquiring an integrated operation that takes spent batteries from intake through to battery-grade refined products."

The Disclosure Schedule lists the "Hydrometallurgical refining line" as Item 7 of the tangible assets (Schedule 2.1(a)) and the Pellancourt Supply Agreement—which is for "Battery-grade lithium carbonate, cobalt sulfate and nickel sulfate produced at the Facility"—as the Business's most valuable contract (~$21M/year, Schedule 3.9, Item 1).

**Why it matters.** The defined term "Business" is used throughout the APA to scope key protections:

| Provision | Reference to "Business" | Effect of Excluding Refining |
|---|---|---|
| § 2.1 (Purchased Assets) | Assets "used primarily in the Business" | Refining line equipment arguably excluded from Purchased Assets |
| § 1.1 (MAE) | "materially adverse to the Business" | A major refining-line failure or permit revocation may not be an MAE |
| § 6.1(a) (Conduct of Business) | "conduct the Business only in the ordinary course" | Seller can make non-ordinary-course changes to the refining operation pre-closing |
| § 6.7(a) (Non-Compete) | "engage in the Business" | Seller can compete in refining (or a refining-inclusive business) post-closing |
| § 3.6 (Sufficiency of Assets) | "continued conduct of the Business" | No representation that assets are sufficient for refining |

The non-compete issue is particularly acute. If "Business" excludes refining, Seller (or its principals) could launch a competing refining operation the day after closing without breaching § 6.7. Given that the LOI's stated rationale is the integrated model, this guts a key protective covenant.

**Recommended fix.** Amend the "Business" definition in APA § 1.1 to:

> "**Business** means the business of collection, discharge, mechanical processing of spent lithium-ion batteries into black mass, **and hydrometallurgical refining of black mass into battery-grade products**, as conducted by Seller at the Facility as of the date of this Agreement."

This aligns the APA with the LOI and with the assets and contracts actually being transferred.

**Negotiating position.** This should be framed as a scrivener's error or drafting oversight, not a substantive change. The LOI, the asset schedule, the contract schedule, and the permit schedule all include refining. If Seller resists, ask for an explanation of the intent. If there is a legitimate reason (e.g., Seller is retaining the refining IP and tolling it to a third party), the deal economics need to be revisited with the client.

### 3. Pellancourt Consent Omitted from Required Consents – Givand Loses Its Walk-Away Right on the Deal's Most Valuable Contract

**Where:** Disclosure Schedule, § 12 (Schedule 6.2(c)); APA, §§ 6.2(c), 7.2(d), and 8.1(b); LOI, § 9(c); Disclosure Schedule, § 8 (Schedule 3.9, Item 1).

**The problem.** LOI § 9(c) lists as a closing condition "receipt of all third-party consents required to assign the material contracts of the Business, **including the consent of Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc.** under the Pellancourt Agreement."

But Disclosure Schedule § 12 (Schedule 6.2(c)) states: "The only Required Consents are: (1) the written consent of [the landlord]... and (2) the approval of the Nevada Division of Environmental Protection..." Pellancourt is absent.

APA § 7.2(d) makes closing conditional on "each of the Required Consents ha[s] been obtained." Because Pellancourt consent is not a Required Consent, Givand has **no closing condition** protecting it. Givand could be forced to close (or walk and lose its deposit/fees) without Pellancourt's consent.

Meanwhile, Schedule 3.9, Item 1 confirms that under Pellancourt Supply Agreement § 16.2, a sale of substantially all assets requires Pellancourt's prior written consent, and under § 16.3, if the transfer occurs without consent, Pellancourt may terminate on 30 days' notice. The schedule further notes: "No consent has been requested or obtained as of the date of the Agreement."

**Why it matters.** The Pellancourt Agreement represents approximately $21 million in annual revenue (roughly 43% of the Purchase Price in annual value). LOI § 3 states that Givand's "valuation assumes that the Pellancourt Agreement... will continue in full force after closing." If Givand closes without consent and Pellancourt terminates, the deal's economic foundation collapses.

Additionally, APA § 6.4 provides that "Seller shall not be required to pay any consideration to obtain any consent other than customary administrative fees." If Pellancourt demands a fee (e.g., a breakage payment or increased pricing) as a condition of consent, Seller has no obligation to pay it, and Givand has no condition to walk.

The outside date is December 31, 2026 (APA § 8.1(b)). With no consent request made as of September 22, 2026, there are roughly 10 weeks to obtain consent, negotiate any conditions, and close. This is tight.

**Recommended fix.** Add Pellancourt's consent to Schedule 6.2(c) as a Required Consent. This restores the closing condition that the LOI contemplated.

Proposed addition to Schedule 6.2(c):

> "3. The prior written consent of Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc. to the assignment of the Cathode Feedstock Supply Agreement dated January 10, 2025, pursuant to Section 16.2 thereof."

**Secondary fix.** Amend APA § 6.4 to require Seller to use "reasonable best efforts" (rather than "commercially reasonable efforts") to obtain the Pellancourt consent, and to pay reasonable consideration (not just "customary administrative fees") if Pellancourt conditions its consent on a fee. Alternatively, add a specific covenant: "Seller shall request Pellancourt's consent within five Business Days after the date of this Agreement and shall use reasonable best efforts to obtain it on terms no less favorable to Buyer than those set forth in the existing agreement."

**Negotiating position.** Frame as restoring the LOI's agreed terms. Seller's counsel likely omitted Pellancourt from Schedule 6.2(c) to avoid giving Givand a walk-right, but this was expressly in the LOI. If Seller insists on keeping it out of Required Consents, demand at minimum: (a) a specific covenant to request consent within a stated number of days, (b) a condition that Givand may terminate if consent is not obtained by a date certain (e.g., 30 days before the outside date), and (c) deletion of the "customary administrative fees" limitation in § 6.4 as it applies to Pellancourt.

## SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### 4. APA § 11.4 Allows Seller to Assign Indemnity and Non-Compete Obligations Without Buyer's Consent

**Where:** APA, § 11.4 ("Assignment").

**The problem.** Section 11.4 provides:

> "Seller may assign this Agreement and any of its rights and obligations under it, **including its obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7 and Section 6.8** and the Transition Services Agreement, **to any Person without the consent of Buyer**. Upon any such assignment and the assumption in writing by the assignee of the assigned obligations, Seller shall be released from those obligations."

This means Seller can transfer its entire indemnification obligation (Article IX), its non-compete (Section 6.7), and its transition services obligation (Section 6.8) to any entity—potentially an empty shell with no assets—walk away, and Givand's recourse is against that shell.

**Why it matters.** The indemnity escrow is $4.85 million. If Seller assigns its indemnity obligations to a new entity and is released, and that entity has no assets beyond the escrow (which is already addressed in Issue 1), Givand's post-closing protection evaporates. The non-compete is similarly undermined: if Seller assigns § 6.7 to an affiliate that then operates a competing business, the restriction is meaningless.

**Recommended fix.** Require Buyer's consent for any assignment of Seller's obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7, or Section 6.8, and prohibit Seller's release upon assignment. Proposed language:

> "Seller may not assign its obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7, Section 6.8, or the Transition Services Agreement without Buyer's prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld. No assignment of such obligations shall release Seller from liability thereunder unless the assignee is an entity with tangible net worth not less than $[●] and Buyer consents in writing to the release."

**Negotiating position.** If Seller insists on assignment flexibility (e.g., for a planned reorganization), accept assignment to an Affiliate with a minimum net worth threshold and require that Seller remain jointly and severally liable for a tail period (e.g., 24 months post-assignment).

### 5. Escrow Release at 18 Months Does Not Align with Environmental Rep Survival (6 Years)

**Where:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement, § 3.1; APA, § 9.1.

**The problem.** APA § 9.1 provides that environmental representations (Section 3.11) survive until the **sixth anniversary** of the Closing Date. The Escrow Agreement releases the full escrow balance at **18 months** (§ 3.1). Even setting aside Issue 1 (the "sole source" problem), the structure creates a gap: from month 18 through year 6, Givand has a valid claim under the environmental reps but no security. Givand must pursue Seller directly, and if Seller has distributed the escrow proceeds (which it will have, since the escrow is released to Seller at 18 months), collection becomes a credit risk.

**Why it matters.** Environmental liabilities at a battery recycling/refining facility often do not manifest for years. A Release that occurred in 2024 might not be detected until 2028 or later. The 18-month escrow release provides no meaningful security for the 6-year environmental tail.

**Recommended fix.** Tiered escrow release:
- Release 70% of the escrow ($3,395,000) at 18 months (covering general rep claims).
- Hold the remaining 30% ($1,455,000) until the earlier of (a) the sixth anniversary of the Closing Date, or (b) the resolution of any pending environmental claim.

Alternatively, if Seller resists a longer hold, require Seller to maintain a pollution legal liability insurance policy (with a limit of not less than $10 million) for the full 6-year environmental survival period, with Givand named as an additional insured or loss payee, and deliver a certificate at closing.

**Negotiating position.** The tiered release is the cleaner fix. If Seller pushes back on the 30% hold, the insurance requirement is a reasonable fallback. Flag to the client that the 6-year environmental survival is only as good as Seller's credit at year 4–6.

### 6. APA § 6.4 Limits Seller's Obligation on Consents to "Commercially Reasonable Efforts" and Prohibits Payment of Consideration

**Where:** APA, § 6.4 ("Efforts to Obtain Required Consents").

**The problem.** Section 6.4 states:

> "Seller shall use **commercially reasonable efforts** to obtain each of the Required Consents before the Closing, on terms reasonably satisfactory to Buyer. Seller shall not be required to pay any consideration to obtain any consent other than **customary administrative fees**."

"Commercially reasonable efforts" is a lower standard than "reasonable best efforts" or "use all reasonable efforts." It permits Seller to weigh the cost and difficulty of obtaining a consent against other business considerations and potentially decide not to pursue it vigorously. The "customary administrative fees" limitation means that if a counterparty (e.g., Pellancourt, if added as a Required Consent per Issue 3) demands a meaningful fee or economic concession as a condition of consent, Seller has no obligation to pay it.

**Why it matters.** In the context of the Nevada DEP permit transfers (a Required Consent), the agency process may take months. If Seller's "commercially reasonable efforts" are passive (e.g., filing the application but not following up, not attending hearings, not providing supplemental information promptly), the outside date (December 31, 2026) could expire without permits transferred, and Givand would have to terminate or waive.

**Recommended fix.** Elevate the standard to "reasonable best efforts" and delete or narrow the "customary administrative fees" limitation:

> "Seller shall use **reasonable best efforts** to obtain each of the Required Consents before the Closing, on terms reasonably satisfactory to Buyer. Seller shall pay any reasonable and bona fide fee or consideration requested by a consenting party as a condition of its consent, not to exceed $[●] in the aggregate without Buyer's prior written consent."

**Negotiating position.** If Seller resists "reasonable best efforts," accept "commercially reasonable efforts" but add a specific timeline covenant: "Seller shall submit all applications and requests for Required Consents within ten (10) Business Days after the date of this Agreement and shall respond to any supplemental information requests within five (5) Business Days of receipt."

## MODERATE ISSUES

### 7. Wilmington Office Lease § 7.2 Prohibits Storing or Displaying Battery Components or Black Mass

**Where:** Wilmington Office Lease, § 7.2 ("Hazardous Materials").

**The problem.** Section 7.2 provides:

> "Tenant shall not store or display at the Premises any batteries, battery components, black mass or other recycling materials, other than consumer batteries used in ordinary office equipment."

The Permitted Use is "General office use, including sales, administrative and customer meeting functions."

**Why it matters.** If Givand intends to use the Wilmington office for customer-facing activities that involve displaying product samples (e.g., black mass, refined lithium carbonate, or battery components for prospective offtake customers), this provision prohibits it. This could limit Givand's ability to use the office as a sales and business development hub for the acquired business.

**Recommended fix.** Request an amendment to § 7.2 to permit "display of product samples in quantities not exceeding [●] and not requiring hazardous materials storage or handling," or add an exception for "sealed product samples displayed for customer demonstration purposes in quantities that do not trigger hazardous materials regulation."

**Priority.** Low. Confirm with the client whether the Wilmington office will be used for any product-display or customer-demonstration functions. If it is strictly back-office/administrative, this is a non-issue.

### 8. Wilmington Office Lease § 14.1 – SNDA Delivery Deadline Has Passed

**Where:** Wilmington Office Lease, § 14.1 ("Subordination").

**The problem.** Section 14.1 requires Landlord to "deliver an SNDA from the current mortgagee of the Property within sixty (60) days after the Effective Date." The Effective Date is March 2, 2026, making the deadline approximately May 1, 2026. We are now past that date.

**Why it matters.** Without a delivered SNDA, the lease is subordinate to the mortgage but Givand lacks the non-disturbance protection. If the mortgagee forecloses, Givand's lease could be extinguished without the contractual assurance of non-disturbance.

**Recommended fix.** Request confirmation from the landlord (or its property manager) that the SNDA was delivered. If not, demand immediate delivery. If the mortgagee refuses to enter into an SNDA, negotiate a direct agreement or require the landlord to provide a letter of credit or other security for Givand's relocation costs in the event of a foreclosure.

**Priority.** Housekeeping. Assign to the paralegal to confirm delivery and file the SNDA in the lease binder.

## ADDITIONAL OBSERVATIONS (No Action Required, But Noted)

- **APA § 9.4 (Basket):** The $485,000 threshold is a true "tipping" basket (once exceeded, Seller is liable from the first dollar). This is favorable to Givand and consistent with market for a deal of this size. No change needed.

- **APA § 6.8 (Transition Services at Seller's Cost):** Favorable to Givand. The LOI contemplated the same. No issue.

- **APA § 2.3 (Assumed Liabilities):** Properly limited to post-closing obligations under Assigned Contracts and ordinary-course trade payables. Pre-closing contract breaches are excluded. No issue.

- **APA § 2.4(f) (Excluded Liabilities – Litigation):** All liabilities from litigation "pending or threatened as of the Closing Date" are Excluded Liabilities. Combined with the Escrow Agreement issue (Issue 1), Givand's recovery for pre-closing litigation is capped at the escrow. This is subsumed by Issue 1.

- **Disclosure Schedule, § 1.4:** "The inclusion of any item in this Disclosure Schedule is not an admission that the item is material." Standard boilerplate; no action needed, but be aware it could be used by Seller to argue that disclosed items (e.g., the Phase I ESAs) do not constitute an admission of environmental problems.
