## Issues Requiring Client Discussion Before We Respond to Seller

Before we turn these comments around, I'd recommend confirming the following with Givand:

1. **Refining scope.** The APA's definition of "Business" excludes hydrometallurgical refining, but the LOI includes it and the deal economics (Pellancourt offtake, patent, refining line) are built around it. We should confirm with the client that this is a drafting error and not a deliberate carve-out. (See Issue 3 below.)

2. **Environmental comfort level.** Buyer is closing with only Phase I ESAs (2020 and 2026 update) and no Phase II. The LOI contemplated Phase II sampling. Combined with the escrow-exclusive-recovery problem (Issue 1), Givand's practical recovery for pre-closing contamination is capped at $4.85M. The client should confirm whether it wants to (a) delay closing to complete a Phase II, (b) negotiate a larger or longer escrow, or (c) accept the risk.

3. **Pellancourt consent status.** The LOI made Pellancourt's consent an express closing condition. The APA drops it. We should confirm whether the client has had any preliminary discussions with Pellancourt and whether it expects consent to be forthcoming. If not, the client needs to understand that as drafted, it can be forced to close without the contract that underpins its valuation.

## Issue List (Most Significant First)

### 1. Escrow Agreement Makes the $4.85M Escrow the Sole and Exclusive Source of Recovery for All Non-Fraud Indemnity Claims

**Documents:** Indemnity Escrow Agreement §4.6; APA §9.7, §11.10

**The problem.** Escrow Agreement §4.6 provides:

> "The Escrow Fund is Buyer's sole and exclusive source of recovery for all Losses of the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, including Losses arising from Excluded Liabilities. This Section 4.6 does not apply to Losses arising from Fraud."

This directly contradicts APA §9.7, which states that losses "shall be satisfied first from the Escrow Fund, to the extent available, in accordance with the Escrow Agreement, **and thereafter directly by Seller**." Under APA §11.10 (Order of Precedence), the Escrow Agreement "shall control with respect to matters concerning the Escrow Fund." Section 4.6 is squarely a matter concerning the Escrow Fund, so it overrides §9.7.

**Why it matters.** The practical effect is that Givand's total recovery for *any* non-fraud indemnity claim—breach of a general rep, breach of a covenant, an Excluded Liability, or a Pre-Closing Environmental Liability—is capped at $4,850,000, full stop. This renders meaningless:

- The 6-year survival period for environmental reps (APA §9.1(b)). The escrow is released at 18 months (Escrow Agreement §3.1). Any environmental claim arising in years 2–6 has a surviving rep but no source of recovery.
- The uncapped indemnity for Excluded Liabilities (APA §9.5(b)). A single significant environmental cleanup or a large pre-closing tax assessment could easily exceed $4.85M, and Givand would absorb the excess.
- The LOI's stated intent (LOI §6) that the cap "would not apply to … retained liabilities, including pre-closing environmental liabilities."

**Recommended fix.** Delete Escrow Agreement §4.6 in its entirety. Replace with language confirming that the Escrow Fund is the *first* source of recovery but not the *exclusive* source, consistent with APA §9.7. Proposed language:

> "The Escrow Fund shall be the first source of recovery for Losses of the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement. The Escrow Fund shall not limit or otherwise affect Buyer's right to recover from Seller directly for any Losses in excess of the Escrow Fund, except as expressly provided in the Purchase Agreement."

If Seller resists, a fallback position: limit §4.6 to claims for breach of general representations and warranties (i.e., the claims subject to the 10% cap in APA §9.5(a)), and carve out Excluded Liabilities, Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities, and covenant breaches so that those remain recoverable from Seller directly beyond the escrow.

### 2. Pellancourt Consent Omitted from Required Consents

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 6.2(c); APA §7.2(d); LOI §9(c), §3

**The problem.** LOI §9(c) expressly lists "the consent of Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc. under the Pellancourt Agreement" as a closing condition. LOI §3 states that "Buyer's valuation assumes that the Pellancourt Agreement, and the refining capacity that supplies it, will continue in full force after closing."

In the APA, however, Schedule 6.2(c) (Required Consents) lists only two items: (1) landlord consent for the Facility Lease and (2) Nevada DEQ permit transfers. The Pellancourt consent is absent.

Under the Pellancourt Supply Agreement (Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 3.9, Item 1), Section 16.2 requires Pellancourt's prior written consent for "any sale, assignment or other transfer of all or substantially all of the assets of Seller's lithium-ion battery recycling business." Without consent, Section 16.3 allows Pellancourt to terminate on 30 days' written notice. The agreement is worth approximately $21M annually.

**Why it matters.** Because the Pellancourt consent is not a Required Consent, it is not a condition to Buyer's closing obligation under APA §7.2(d). Givand could be contractually compelled to close—and pay $43.65M in cash—without the single most valuable contract in the deal. Post-closing, Pellancourt could terminate within 30 days, destroying the revenue base that justified the purchase price. The indemnity for loss of the contract would be subject to the 10% cap (APA §9.5(a)) and, per Issue 1, effectively limited to the escrow.

**Recommended fix.** Add the Pellancourt consent as item 3 on Schedule 6.2(c). This makes it a Required Consent and therefore a condition to Buyer's closing obligation under §7.2(d). No proposed language change to the APA body is needed—§7.2(d) already conditions closing on "each of the Required Consents."

If Seller resists (e.g., argues it's not a "consent" but a "notice"), the fallback is to add a specific condition in §7.2: "the Pellancourt Supply Agreement remains in full force and effect and has not been terminated or given notice of termination."

### 3. Definition of "Business" Excludes Refining

**Documents:** APA §1.1; LOI §1, §3; Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 2.1(a) Item 7, Schedule 3.8 Item 2, Schedule 3.9 Item 1

**The problem.** The APA defines "Business" as:

> "the business of collection, discharge and mechanical processing of spent lithium-ion batteries into black mass, **excluding any refining of black mass**, as conducted by Seller at the Facility as of the date of this Agreement."

The LOI defines "Business" to include "hydrometallurgical refining of black mass." The LOI further states that the refining capacity supplying Pellancourt is a "key reason for the Transaction."

Yet the Purchased Assets include the hydrometallurgical refining line (Schedule 2.1(a), Item 7), the refining patent (Schedule 3.8, Item 2), and the Pellancourt offtake agreement for refined products (Schedule 3.9, Item 1). Givand is buying the refining assets but the operative definition of "Business" excludes the refining operations.

**Why it matters.** Numerous provisions key off "the Business":

| Provision | Effect of Excluding Refining |
|---|---|
| §6.7 (Non-compete) | Seller could arguably start a competing refining operation during the 5-year Restricted Period |
| §3.11 (Environmental reps) | Seller's environmental compliance rep may not cover the refining line's operations (leach reactors, solvent extraction, acid storage) |
| §6.1 (Conduct of Business) | Seller could shut down or neglect the refining line pre-closing without breaching the ordinary-course covenant |
| §1.1 (MAE definition) | A material problem at the refining line might not constitute an MAE to "the Business" |
| §6.8 (Transition Services) | Scope of regulatory reporting and other services may not cover refining |

**Recommended fix.** Delete "excluding any refining of black mass" from the definition in §1.1. The definition should read:

> "'Business' means the business of collection, discharge and mechanical processing of spent lithium-ion batteries into black mass and the hydrometallurgical refining of black mass into battery-grade products, as conducted by Seller at the Facility as of the date of this Agreement."

This aligns the APA with the LOI and with the actual assets being purchased.

### 4. Seller May Assign Its Indemnity, Non-Compete, and Transition Services Obligations Without Buyer Consent and Be Released

**Documents:** APA §11.4

**The problem.** Section 11.4 provides:

> "Seller may assign this Agreement and any of its rights and obligations under it, including its obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7 and Section 6.8 and the Transition Services Agreement, to any Person without the consent of Buyer. Upon any such assignment and the assumption in writing by the assignee of the assigned obligations, Seller shall be released from those obligations."

**Why it matters.** This is extraordinarily one-sided. Seller can:

- Assign its entire indemnification obligation (Article IX) to a newly formed, asset-free entity and be released. Givand's recourse for any non-fraud claim is then limited to (a) the escrow (which, per Issue 1, is the *exclusive* source of recovery) and (b) a judgment against an empty shell.
- Assign the 5-year non-compete (§6.7) to an entity with no operational capability or incentive to honor it.
- Assign the transition services obligation (§6.8) to a third party that may not have the institutional knowledge to perform.

The LOI is silent on assignment, and this provision appears to have been added by Seller's counsel in the definitive documents.

**Recommended fix.** At minimum, require Buyer's prior written consent (not to be unreasonably withheld) for any assignment of Seller's obligations under Article IX, §6.7, or §6.8. Proposed language:

> "Seller may not assign its obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7, or Section 6.8 (or the Transition Services Agreement) without Buyer's prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed. Any permitted assignment shall not relieve Seller of its obligations unless the assignee is an entity with tangible net worth not less than $[●] and assumes the obligations in writing."

A stronger position: make Seller's indemnity and non-compete obligations entirely non-assignable.

### 5. No Phase II Environmental Site Assessment Completed

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 3.11; LOI §9(a); APA §6.3

**The problem.** Schedule 3.11 discloses only two Phase I ESAs (November 2020 and July 2026 update). No Phase II (i.e., no soil or groundwater sampling) has been performed. The LOI contemplated that Buyer's due diligence "may include Phase II sampling at the Facility." APA §6.3 gives Buyer access for "environmental assessments of the Facility (including, with Seller's consent not to be unreasonably withheld, invasive sampling)," but there is no indication that sampling was actually conducted.

**Why it matters.** The Facility has operated a battery discharge and refining operation (brine immersion tanks, acid leach reactors, solvent extraction, wastewater pretreatment) since at least 2021. The Phase I ESAs identify recognized environmental conditions but cannot confirm or rule out subsurface contamination. If contamination exists, the cleanup cost for a facility of this type could easily exceed the $4.85M escrow. Per Issue 1, Givand's recovery for Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities is limited to the escrow.

**Recommended fix (in order of preference):**

- **Best:** Make completion of a Phase II ESA (to Buyer's satisfaction) a condition to closing under §7.2. Add to §7.2: "Buyer has completed such environmental assessments, including invasive sampling, as Buyer reasonably deems necessary."
- **Alternative:** Extend the escrow release date for environmental claims to align with the 6-year survival period in §9.1(b). For example, hold back a portion of the escrow (e.g., $2M) for 6 years specifically for environmental claims.
- **Minimum:** Fix Issue 1 so that environmental claims are recoverable from Seller beyond the escrow, and negotiate a specific environmental indemnity with a higher or no cap.

### 6. Governing Law Mismatch Between APA and Escrow Agreement

**Documents:** APA §11.7 (Delaware); Escrow Agreement §9.1 (New York), §9.2 (Manhattan jurisdiction)

**The problem.** The APA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Delaware Court of Chancery. The Escrow Agreement is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan. Because the Escrow Agreement controls "matters concerning the Escrow Fund" (APA §11.10), disputes about whether a claim is payable, the scope of §4.6, or the mechanics of release will be litigated in New York under New York law, while the underlying indemnity claim is governed by Delaware law.

**Why it matters.** This creates split proceedings and potential for inconsistent interpretations. For example, a New York court deciding whether the Escrow Fund has been "exhausted" may apply different principles than a Delaware court deciding whether a claim falls within Article IX.

**Recommended fix.** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and jurisdiction to Delaware (APA §11.7 and §11.8). If the Escrow Agent (Ostrander Trust Company, N.A.) requires New York law for regulatory reasons, at minimum add a provision that the Escrow Agreement shall be construed consistently with the APA and that in the event of any ambiguity, the APA's Delaware governing law shall apply to the interpretation of indemnity claims.

### 7. City of Sparks Permits Not Listed as Required Consents

**Documents:** Disclosure Schedule, Schedule 3.10 Items 5–6, Schedule 6.2(c)

**The problem.** The City of Sparks Business License (Item 5) and the Fire Department Hazardous Materials Operational Permit (Item 6) are marked "Not transferable; Buyer to obtain." They are not listed on Schedule 6.2(c) as Required Consents and therefore are not conditions to closing.

**Why it matters.** The Fire Department HazMat permit is likely required to operate the battery storage and discharge areas. If Givand cannot obtain it promptly after closing, it may be in violation of local fire codes while operating the Facility. The Business License is administrative, but the HazMat permit may involve an inspection and could take weeks or months.

**Recommended fix.** Add both permits to Schedule 6.2(c) as Required Consents, or at minimum add the Fire Department HazMat permit. Alternatively, add a post-closing covenant requiring Seller to cooperate in obtaining the permits and a condition that Seller has submitted all necessary applications before closing.

### 8. Wilmington Office Lease – SNDA Delivery Should Be Verified

**Documents:** Wilmington Office Lease §14.1

**The problem.** Section 14.1 requires Landlord to deliver an SNDA from the current mortgagee "within sixty (60) days after the Effective Date." The Effective Date is March 2, 2026, so the deadline was approximately May 1, 2026. The subordination is effective *only if* the SNDA is delivered.

**Why it matters.** If the SNDA was not delivered, the lease is subordinate to the mortgage without the non-disturbance protection. In a foreclosure scenario, Givand's leasehold could be extinguished without the right to remain in possession. This is Givand's headquarters, so losing it would be operationally disruptive.

**Recommended fix.** Confirm with the client whether the SNDA was received. If not, send a notice to Landlord under §13.3 (Landlord Default) demanding delivery. This is a housekeeping item but should be resolved before we are distracted by the APA negotiation.

### 9. Wilmington Office Lease – Hazardous Materials Restriction

**Documents:** Wilmington Office Lease §7.2

**The problem.** Section 7.2 prohibits Tenant from storing or displaying "any batteries, battery components, black mass or other recycling materials, other than consumer batteries used in ordinary office equipment."

**Why it matters.** If Givand intends to use the Wilmington office for customer meetings involving product samples, technical presentations with battery components, or any operational function that involves physical materials from the business, this restriction could be problematic. It's a standard office lease provision, but given the nature of Givand's business, it's worth flagging.

**Recommended fix.** If the client anticipates any need to display or store samples, negotiate a limited exception: "provided that Tenant may store and display non-hazardous product samples in quantities not exceeding [●] and in a manner consistent with the Permitted Use." If no such need is anticipated, no action required.

### 10. 18-Month Survival Period for General Representations Is Aggressive

**Documents:** APA §9.1

**The problem.** General reps and warranties survive for 18 months post-closing. Market practice for a transaction of this size and complexity (industrial assets, environmental exposure, integrated supply chain) is 21–24 months. The 18-month period aligns exactly with the escrow release date, leaving no buffer.

**Why it matters.** If a breach of a general rep (e.g., financial statements, condition of assets, material contracts) is discovered at month 19, the rep has expired and the claim is time-barred. For a $48.5M acquisition of complex industrial equipment, 18 months may be insufficient to discover all issues.

**Recommended fix.** Extend survival for general reps to 24 months. If Seller insists on 18 months, at minimum extend the survival for §3.4 (Financial Statements) and §3.6 (Condition of Assets) to 24 months, as these are the reps most likely to have latent breaches.

### 11. Section 9.9 (Exclusive Remedies) Combined with Escrow-Exclusive Recovery Eliminates Post-Closing Remedies

**Documents:** APA §9.9, §9.7; Escrow Agreement §4.6

**The problem.** Section 9.9 makes Article IX the exclusive post-closing remedy (except for fraud, specific performance, and the working capital adjustment). Section 9.7 routes all Article IX recovery through the escrow first. Escrow Agreement §4.6 makes the escrow the *sole* source of recovery. The combined effect: after the escrow is released or exhausted, Givand has no remedy for any non-fraud post-closing breach, period.

**Why it matters.** This is the structural consequence of Issues 1 and 4. Even if we fix the assignment issue (Issue 4) and the exclusive-recovery issue (Issue 1) separately, we should confirm that the interplay of these three provisions is addressed holistically.

**Recommended fix.** Addressed through the fixes in Issues 1 and 4. Additionally, consider adding to §9.9 an express carve-out: "Notwithstanding the foregoing, Buyer shall retain all rights and remedies at law or in equity for Seller's breach of Section 6.7 (Non-Competition) or Section 6.8 (Transition Services), including the right to seek injunctive relief."
