## ISSUES REQUIRING CLIENT INPUT BEFORE WE RESPOND TO SELLER

Before we send comments, I recommend raising the following with Givand:

1. **Refining scope.** The APA's definition of "Business" excludes refining, but the Purchased Assets include the entire refining line, the refining patent, and the Pellancourt offtake contract. 3. **Pellancourt relationship.** The client should confirm whether it has had any direct contact with Pellancourt regarding the consent, and whether it has any commercial relationship or leverage with Pellancourt that could facilitate the consent. Our negotiating position on making this a closing condition depends on how realistic it is to obtain.

## ISSUES IN ORDER OF SIGNIFICANCE

### 1. Definition of "Business" Excludes Refining – Fundamental Drafting Error

**Where:** APA § 1.1 ("Business" definition) vs. LOI § 1; APA §§ 2.1(a) (Item 7), 2.1(f), 3.8, 6.7, 1.1 ("Material Adverse Effect")

**The problem.** The APA defines "Business" as "the business of collection, discharge and mechanical processing of spent lithium-ion batteries into black mass, **excluding any refining of black mass**." The LOI defined it as "collection, discharge and shredding of spent lithium-ion batteries **and hydrometallurgical refining of black mass**." Yet the Purchased Assets include the hydrometallurgical refining line (Schedule 2.1(a), Item 7), the refining patent (Schedule 3.8, Item 2), and the Pellancourt Supply Agreement for refined products (Schedule 3.9, Item 1, ~$21M/year).

**Why it matters.** The defined term "Business" is used throughout the APA to scope key protections:

- **Non-compete (§ 6.7):** Seller is restricted from "engaging in the Business" for five years. If "Business" excludes refining, Seller (or its assignee under § 11.4) could open a competing refining operation the day after closing.
- **Material Adverse Effect (§ 1.1):** A MAE must be adverse "to the Business." If the refining line breaks down or the Pellancourt contract is lost, Seller could argue this doesn't affect "the Business" as defined.
- **Conduct of Business covenant (§ 6.1):** Seller's obligation to operate in the ordinary course applies to "the Business." Refining operations may fall outside.
- **Environmental reps (§ 3.11):** Reference to "the Business or the Facility" may still capture refining, but the ambiguity creates dispute risk.

**Recommendation.** Delete "excluding any refining of black mass" from the "Business" definition in § 1.1. The definition should read:

> "Business" means the business of collection, discharge, mechanical processing and hydrometallurgical refining of spent lithium-ion batteries, as conducted by Seller at the Facility as of the date of this Agreement.

This aligns the APA with the LOI and with the actual scope of the Purchased Assets. This is not a negotiating ask—it's a correction of a drafting error. Frame it that way to Seller.

### 2. Pellancourt Consent Omitted from Required Consents and Closing Conditions

**Where:** APA § 6.2(c) and § 7.2(d); Disclosure Schedule § 12 (Schedule 6.2(c)); LOI § 9(c); Disclosure Schedule § 8 (Schedule 3.9, Item 1)

**The problem.** The LOI expressly made "the consent of Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc. under the Pellancourt Agreement" a closing condition (§ 9(c)). The APA's closing condition (§ 7.2(d)) requires only that "each of the Required Consents has been obtained." "Required Consents" are defined in § 6.2(c) as those "set forth on Schedule 6.2(c)." Schedule 6.2(c) lists only (1) the landlord's consent to the Facility Lease assignment and (2) the Nevada DEQ permit transfers. The Pellancourt consent is absent.

Meanwhile, Schedule 3.9, Item 1 confirms that the Pellancourt Supply Agreement requires consent for "any sale, assignment or other transfer of all or substantially all of the assets of Seller's lithium-ion battery recycling business" (§ 16.2), and that without consent, Pellancourt may terminate on 30 days' notice (§ 16.3). The schedule further states: "No consent has been requested or obtained as of the date of the Agreement."

**Why it matters.** The Pellancourt contract is worth approximately $21M/year—nearly half the Purchase Price on an annual run-rate basis. The LOI states it is "a key reason for the Transaction" and that "Buyer's valuation assumes that the Pellancourt Agreement... will continue in full force after closing." As drafted, Givand must close even if Pellancourt refuses consent or terminates. Givand would acquire a $48.5M refining operation with no offtake for its primary product.

**Recommendation.** Add the Pellancourt consent to Schedule 6.2(c) as a Required Consent. This automatically makes it a closing condition under § 7.2(d). Proposed addition to Schedule 6.2(c):

> 3. The prior written consent of Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc. to the assignment of the Cathode Feedstock Supply Agreement dated January 10, 2025, pursuant to Section 16.2 thereof.

Additionally, consider adding a specific condition in § 7.2 that the Pellancourt Supply Agreement remains in full force and effect at Closing and has been validly assigned to Buyer. If Seller resists making it a hard condition (perhaps arguing it will "use commercially reasonable efforts"), the fallback should be: (a) an express covenant in § 6.4 that Seller will request the consent within 10 business days of signing, and (b) a specific termination right for Buyer if the consent is not obtained by a date 15 days before the outside date.

**Flag to client:** Confirm whether Givand has any independent relationship with Pellancourt that could support the consent request.

### 3. Escrow Agreement Makes Escrow Fund the "Sole and Exclusive Source of Recovery" for Excluded Liabilities – Including Environmental

**Where:** Escrow Agreement § 4.6; APA § 9.7, § 9.5(b), § 9.1(b), § 11.10; APA § 2.4(a)

**The problem.** APA § 9.7 provides that losses "shall be satisfied first from the Escrow Fund, to the extent available... and **thereafter directly by Seller**." APA § 9.5(b) expressly carves Excluded Liabilities (including Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities) out of both the basket and the cap. APA § 9.1(b) gives environmental reps a six-year survival period.

But Escrow Agreement § 4.6 provides: "The Escrow Fund is Buyer's **sole and exclusive source of recovery** for all Losses of the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement, **including Losses arising from Excluded Liabilities**."

APA § 11.10 (Order of Precedence) states the APA prevails over the Escrow Agreement "except that the Escrow Agreement shall control with respect to matters concerning the Escrow Fund." Section 4.6 is arguably a "matter concerning the Escrow Fund" because it defines the relationship between the Fund and Buyer's recovery rights.

**Why it matters.** If § 4.6 controls, then:
- Givand's recovery for pre-closing environmental liabilities is capped at $4,850,000, regardless of the actual loss (which for a hazardous materials facility could be tens or hundreds of millions).
- After the Release Date (18 months post-closing), the escrow is paid to Seller. If an environmental condition is discovered in year 3 or year 5 (within the 6-year survival), Givand has **no secured remedy and potentially no remedy at all**, because § 4.6 eliminates the "thereafter directly by Seller" language in APA § 9.7.
- The same applies to all other Excluded Liabilities: pre-closing taxes, employee liabilities, litigation, indebtedness.

This directly contradicts the LOI (§ 6), which contemplated that the cap "would not apply to... retained liabilities, including pre-closing environmental liabilities."

**Recommendation.** Delete or revise Escrow Agreement § 4.6. It should read:

> The Escrow Fund is the first source of recovery for Losses of the Buyer Indemnitees under Article IX of the Purchase Agreement. This Section 4.6 does not limit Buyer's right to recover from Seller directly to the extent the Escrow Fund is insufficient, except that for Losses arising from breaches of representations and warranties (other than Fundamental Representations), Buyer's aggregate recovery (from the Escrow Fund and Seller directly) shall not exceed the cap in Section 9.5(a) of the Purchase Agreement. This Section 4.6 does not apply to Losses arising from Fraud, Excluded Liabilities, or Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities.

Alternatively, if Seller insists on the exclusive-remedy structure for general reps, carve out Excluded Liabilities and Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities expressly:

> This Section 4.6 does not apply to (a) Losses arising from Fraud, (b) Losses arising from Excluded Liabilities (including Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities), or (c) Losses arising from breach of Fundamental Representations.

**Flag to client:** This is the single most financially significant issue in the document set. The client needs to understand that as drafted, its environmental protection is worth at most $4.85M and expires in 18 months.

### 4. Seller May Assign Indemnity, Non-Compete, and Transition Services Obligations to Any Person Without Buyer Consent

**Where:** APA § 11.4

**The problem.** Section 11.4 provides: "Seller may assign this Agreement and any of its rights and obligations under it, including its obligations under Article IX, Section 6.7 and Section 6.8 and the Transition Services Agreement, to any Person **without the consent of Buyer**. Upon any such assignment and the assumption in writing by the assignee of the assigned obligations, Seller shall be released from those obligations."

Buyer, by contrast, "may not assign this Agreement or any of its rights or obligations under it without the prior written consent of Seller."

**Why it matters.**
- **Indemnity (Article IX):** Seller can assign its indemnification obligations to an empty shell company and walk away. Combined with Issue 3 (escrow as exclusive remedy), this means Givand's only recovery is the $4.85M escrow, and after 18 months, potentially nothing. Even the "thereafter directly by Seller" language in § 9.7 becomes meaningless if Seller has assigned and been released.
- **Non-compete (§ 6.7):** Seller can assign the non-compete to an affiliate or third party. The restriction binds the assignee, but if the assignee has no assets or operations, the remedy is theoretical. More importantly, Seller itself is *released*—so Seller's principals could potentially argue they are no longer restricted.
- **Transition services (§ 6.8 / TSA):** Seller can assign its obligation to provide accounting, IT, logistics, and regulatory reporting services to any person. Givand could find itself receiving critical transition services from an unknown third party with no skin in the game.

**Recommendation.** Revise § 11.4 to require Buyer's consent for Seller's assignment of indemnification and non-compete obligations:

> Seller may not assign its obligations under Article IX or Section 6.7 without Buyer's prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld. Seller may assign its obligations under Section 6.8 and the Transition Services Agreement to an Affiliate of Seller that has the operational capability to perform such services, upon at least thirty (30) days' prior written notice to Buyer.

If Seller resists, the minimum concession should be: (a) no assignment of Article IX obligations to a Person that is not an Affiliate of Seller, and (b) Buyer's consent required for any assignment of § 6.7.

### 5. No Condition on Completion of Environmental Due Diligence (Phase II)

**Where:** LOI § 9(a); APA Article VII (Conditions to Closing); APA § 6.3

**The problem.** The LOI made closing subject to "completion of Buyer's due diligence, including... environmental diligence (which may include Phase II sampling at the Facility), **to Buyer's satisfaction**." The APA contains no equivalent condition. Section 7.2 lists only: (a) reps true, (b) covenants performed, (c) no MAE, (d) Required Consents, (e) deliverables. There is no "satisfaction with due diligence" condition.

APA § 6.3 gives Buyer access for "environmental assessments of the Facility (including, with Seller's consent not to be unreasonably withheld, invasive sampling)," but this is a pre-closing covenant, not a condition to close.

**Why it matters.** If Phase II sampling reveals significant contamination (e.g., solvent plumes, heavy metal leaching, electrolyte residues), Givand has no walk right. Its only recourse is to close and then assert a claim under § 3.11—but that claim is subject to the escrow-exclusive-remedy issue (Issue 3) and the 18-month release.

**Recommendation.** Add to § 7.2:

> (f) Buyer has completed its environmental due diligence, including any Phase II environmental site assessment, to its reasonable satisfaction, or Buyer has waived this condition in writing.

If Seller resists a full satisfaction condition, the fallback is: "Buyer has not identified, through its environmental due diligence, any condition at the Facility that would reasonably be expected to result in Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities exceeding $[X] in the aggregate."

**Flag to client:** Confirm status of Phase II. If it hasn't been done, this condition is essential. If it has been done and results are clean, this becomes less critical (but the escrow issue remains).

### 6. Environmental Representations Are Thin and the Disclosure Schedule Does Not Identify Specific RECs

**Where:** APA § 3.11; Disclosure Schedule § 10 (Schedule 3.11)

**The problem.** Section 3.11 represents (a) material compliance with Environmental Laws, (b) no Release requiring investigation or remediation, (c) no notice of violation, and (d) all reports made available. Schedule 3.11 discloses only two Phase I ESAs (2020 and 2026) and states "No other exceptions."

The Phase I ESAs are in the data room but not reproduced in the Disclosure Schedule. If either Phase I identified Recognized Environmental Conditions (RECs)—which is common at industrial facilities handling hazardous materials—those RECs are not specifically excepted from § 3.11(b). This creates ambiguity: did Seller "disclose" the RECs by making the report available under § 3.11(d), or does § 3.11(b) remain unqualified?

Additionally, there is no representation regarding:
- Pending or threatened regulatory inspections, audits, or enforcement actions
- Compliance with specific permit conditions (e.g., wastewater discharge limits, air emission limits)
- Status of any remedial action or monitoring requirements
- Off-site disposal compliance (the APA defines Pre-Closing Environmental Liabilities to include off-site transport/disposal, but there's no rep specifically addressing disposal vendor compliance)

**Recommendation.**
- Require Seller to add to Schedule 3.11 a specific identification of all RECs noted in the Phase I ESAs, and confirm whether any require Phase II follow-up.
- Add to § 3.11: "(e) there are no pending or threatened inspections, audits, enforcement actions or remedial orders by any Governmental Authority relating to the Facility or the Business; and (f) all Hazardous Materials generated by the Business have been transported and disposed of in compliance with Environmental Laws by licensed handlers."
- If Phase II has not been completed, add it as a condition (see Issue 5).

### 7. Intellectual Property Representations Do Not Address Ownership or Validity of the Refining Patent

**Where:** APA § 3.8; Disclosure Schedule § 7 (Schedule 3.8)

**The problem.** Section 3.8 represents only that "to Seller's knowledge, the conduct of the Business does not infringe the intellectual property rights of any Person." There is no representation that:
- Seller owns the patent (U.S. Patent No. 11,684,219) free and clear of any license, security interest, or co-ownership claim
- The patent is valid and enforceable
- No third party has asserted or threatened to assert infringement against the patent
- The patent is not subject to any maintenance fee delinquency or derivation proceeding

The patent is described in Schedule 3.8 as "used in the hydrometallurgical refining line"—it is core technology.

**Recommendation.** Expand § 3.8:

> Seller owns or has the right to license all intellectual property used in the Business, free and clear of all Encumbrances. U.S. Patent No. 11,684,219 is valid, in full force and effect, and all maintenance fees have been paid. To Seller's knowledge, no Person has asserted or threatened to assert that the Business infringes any intellectual property rights of such Person, and no Person has asserted or threatened to assert that the patent is invalid or unenforceable.

### 8. Split Governing Law and Forum (Delaware vs. New York)

**Where:** APA §§ 11.7–11.8 (Delaware law; Chancery Court); Escrow Agreement §§ 9.1–9.2 (New York law; Manhattan courts)

**The problem.** The APA is governed by Delaware law with exclusive jurisdiction in the Court of Chancery. The Escrow Agreement is governed by New York law with exclusive jurisdiction in Manhattan state or federal courts. Given Issue 3 (the escrow agreement's exclusive-remedy provision potentially overriding the APA's indemnity structure), a dispute about the scope of the escrow's coverage could require litigation in two forums under two bodies of law.

**Recommendation.** Conform the Escrow Agreement to Delaware law and Delaware forum (APA §§ 11.7–11.8). If the Escrow Agent (Ostrander Trust Company, N.A.) requires New York law for its own comfort, at minimum add a provision that any dispute between Buyer and Seller regarding the interpretation or application of the Escrow Agreement to their indemnification rights under the APA shall be governed by Delaware law and submitted to the Delaware forum.

### 9. Working Capital Target Lacks a Defined Formula

**Where:** APA § 2.7; Disclosure Schedule § 3 (Schedule 2.7)

**The problem.** Schedule 2.7 sets target working capital at $3,200,000 and says it is "calculated using the same accounting principles, practices and methods as the unaudited balance sheet of the Business as of June 30, 2026." But "working capital" is never defined. There is no specification of which balance sheet items are included or excluded (e.g., is restricted cash included? Are deferred revenue or accrued vacation included? How is inventory valued—FIFO, weighted average?).

**Why it matters.** In a facility with significant inventory (spent batteries, black mass, refined products, reagents), the working capital calculation can swing by millions depending on assumptions. A $3.2M target with no defined formula is a recipe for a post-closing dispute under § 2.7.

**Recommendation.** Add a definition of "Working Capital" in § 1.1:

> "Working Capital" means, as of any date, current assets (excluding cash and cash equivalents and any restricted cash) minus current liabilities (excluding any portion of the Indemnity Escrow Amount and any debt to Seller or its Affiliates), calculated in accordance with GAAP consistently applied.

And attach the June 30, 2026 balance sheet (or the relevant working capital components) as an exhibit to Schedule 2.7 so both parties are working from the same baseline.

### 10. No Specific Covenant Protecting the Pellancourt Relationship Pre-Closing

**Where:** APA § 6.1 (Conduct of Business); APA § 6.4 (Efforts to Obtain Required Consents)

**The problem.** Section 6.1 requires Seller to conduct the Business in the ordinary course and not to "amend, terminate or waive any material right under any Assigned Contract." But it does not specifically prohibit Seller from:
- Negotiating a renewal or amendment with Pellancourt on different terms
- Disclosing the transaction to Pellancourt in a way that could jeopardize the consent
- Failing to perform under the Pellancourt agreement (e.g., missing a delivery) in a way that could give Pellancourt an independent termination right

Section 6.4 requires Seller to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to obtain Required Consents, but as noted in Issue 2, the Pellancourt consent is not currently a Required Consent.

**Recommendation.** In addition to making the Pellancourt consent a Required Consent (Issue 2), add to § 6.1:

> (f) not take any action that would reasonably be expected to impair, diminish or terminate the Pellancourt Supply Agreement or Seller's relationship with Pellancourt Cathode Materials, Inc., including by failing to perform any obligation under such agreement or by communicating with Pellancourt regarding the transaction without Buyer's prior written approval of the substance of such communication.

### 11. Facility Lease Renewal Options Not Expressly Addressed

**Where:** APA § 2.1(d); Disclosure Schedule § 6 (Schedule 3.7)

**The problem.** The Facility Lease has two five-year renewal options (expiring February 28, 2036, with options to 2046). The APA includes "Seller's leasehold interest in the Facility" as a Purchased Asset (§ 2.1(d)) and requires delivery of "an assignment of the Facility lease" (§ 5.2(c)). But neither the APA nor the Disclosure Schedule expressly states that the renewal options are included in the assignment.

**Why it matters.** The renewal options are valuable— they give Givand the right to remain in a purpose-built 112,000 SF facility through 2046 at below-market rent (the current rent of $96,320/month ≈ $10.33/SF/year is likely below market for industrial space in Sparks with hazmat capabilities). If the options don't transfer, Givand faces relocation risk in 2036.

**Recommendation.** Add to § 2.1(d): "Seller's leasehold interest in the Facility, **including all renewal options thereunder**." Also confirm in the lease assignment document that the options are expressly assigned.

### 12. No Representation on Insurance or Pending Claims

**Where:** APA Article III (generally); APA § 2.2(d) (Excluded Assets)

**The problem.** Seller's insurance policies are Excluded Assets (§ 2.2(d)). There is no representation that:
- Seller has maintained adequate insurance during the relevant period
- There are no pending or threatened claims under Seller's insurance policies
- No insurer has reserved rights or denied coverage for any pre-closing event

If a pre-closing environmental event gives rise to a claim that was reported to Seller's insurer, the policy (an Excluded Asset) remains with Seller. But if the claim also implicates the Facility (which Givand now occupies), Givand could be drawn into the dispute.

There are no pending or, to Seller's knowledge, threatened claims under any insurance policy of Seller relating to the Business or the Facility. No insurer has issued a reservation of rights or denied coverage with respect to any such claim.

**The problem.** The lease prohibits Givand from storing "any batteries, battery components, black mass or other recycling materials, other than consumer batteries used in ordinary office equipment" at the Wilmington office. As a battery recycling company, Givand may need to store reference samples, bring small quantities of material for customer meetings or investor presentations, or house lab equipment that processes small samples.

**Why it matters.** This is not a deal-document issue, but it is a practical constraint on Givand's post-closing operations that the client should be aware of. If Givand plans to use the Wilmington office for any function involving battery materials (even small quantities for demonstration or testing), it will be in breach of the lease.

**Recommendation.** Not a negotiation item with Seller. The lease was signed March 2, 2026, and the restriction was presumably negotiated with knowledge of Givand's business.

### 14. Escrow Tax Treatment – Buyer Reports 100% of Earnings but Receives Only 40% Distribution

**Where:** Escrow Agreement §§ 8.1, 8.3

**The problem.** Section 8.1 provides that "Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Fund until it is disbursed" and "all earnings on the Escrow Fund shall be reported as income of Buyer." Section 8.3 requires the Escrow Agent to distribute to Buyer only "40% of the earnings reported as Buyer's income" annually. The remaining 60% of earnings stays in the Escrow Fund.

If the escrow is released to Seller at 18 months (less pending claims), Seller effectively receives the benefit of 60% of the earnings that Buyer reported as income. Buyer pays tax on money that ultimately benefits Seller.

**Why it matters.** On $4.85M in a typical money market yield environment (assume ~4-5%), annual earnings would be approximately $194,000–$242,500. Buyer reports 100% as income but receives only 40% (~$77,600–$97,000). The net tax cost to Buyer of the undistributed 60% is modest in absolute dollars but represents an unnecessary transfer of economic value to Seller.

**Recommendation.** Revise § 8.3 to distribute 100% of earnings to Buyer annually, with the understanding that the distributed earnings reduce the Escrow Fund balance (and thus the security for Buyer's claims). Alternatively, provide that upon release of the Escrow Fund to Seller, Seller shall pay Buyer an amount equal to the tax on earnings that were not distributed to Buyer. This is a lower-priority item but worth raising.

### 15. No Specific Remedy for Seller's Failure to Provide Transition Services

**Where:** APA § 6.8; APA § 9.2(b)

**The problem.** Section 6.8 requires the parties to enter into a TSA at Closing for "up to nine months" of "accounting, IT, logistics and regulatory reporting services... at Seller's cost." The APA does not specify:
- Service levels or performance standards
- What happens if Seller fails to provide services (beyond general indemnity for covenant breach under § 9.2(b))
- Whether Buyer can engage a third party and charge Seller for the cost
- The scope of "regulatory reporting" (which permits? what filings?)

The TSA itself is not in the folder and is described only as "to be entered into at Closing."

**Why it matters.** For a complex industrial facility with environmental permits requiring ongoing regulatory reporting, a gap in transition services could result in missed filings, permit violations, or operational disruption. The general indemnity for covenant breach is subject to the basket ($485,000) and potentially the escrow-exclusive-remedy issue.

**Recommendation.** Before Closing, negotiate the TSA with specific service levels, a step-in right for Buyer (allowing Buyer to engage a replacement provider at Seller's cost if Seller fails to perform), and a liquidated damages provision for failure to provide regulatory reporting services. At minimum, add to § 6.8:

> If Seller fails to provide any service under the Transition Services Agreement, Buyer may engage a third party to provide such service and shall be entitled to recover from Seller the reasonable cost of such third-party services, in addition to any other remedy available under this Agreement.

### 16. Outside Date May Be Insufficient for Permit Transfers

**Where:** APA § 8.1(b) (outside date: December 31, 2026); Disclosure Schedule § 9 (Schedule 3.10)

**The problem.** The three Nevada Environmental Permits (air quality, hazardous waste, wastewater) all require "transfer approval" from the Nevada Division of Environmental Protection. The outside date is December 31, 2026—approximately 3 months from signing. State environmental permit transfers can take 60–120 days or longer, depending on the complexity of the facility and the regulator's workload.

If the permits are not transferred by December 31, either party can terminate under § 8.1(b) (unless the failure is due to the terminating party's breach). If Seller has done everything in its power but the regulator is slow, neither party is in "breach," and Buyer could be forced to terminate a deal it wants to close.

**Recommendation.** Add an extension mechanism: "If the only condition not satisfied as of the outside date is the transfer of the Nevada Environmental Permits, and Seller has submitted all required applications and is using diligent efforts to obtain approval, the outside date shall be extended for up to sixty (60) additional days." Alternatively, move the outside date to February 28, 2027.

### 20. No "Bring-Down" of Environmental Reps at Closing Beyond General Standard

**Where:** APA § 7.2(a); APA § 3.11

**The problem.** Section 7.2(a) requires Seller's reps to be "true and correct in all material respects" at Closing (with Fundamental Reps true in all respects). Environmental reps are not Fundamental Reps, so the bring-down standard is "material respects." For environmental matters, "material" is doing a lot of work. A $500,000 contamination issue might not be "material" to the Business as a whole (which is generating $21M+/year from Pellancourt alone), yet it could be significant for Givand's risk calculus.

**Recommendation.** Consider adding environmental reps to the Fundamental Representations definition (§ 1.1) so they must be true "in all respects" at Closing. If Seller resists, at minimum add a specific condition: "No Release of Hazardous Materials at the Facility has occurred since the date of this Agreement that has not been disclosed to Buyer in writing."
