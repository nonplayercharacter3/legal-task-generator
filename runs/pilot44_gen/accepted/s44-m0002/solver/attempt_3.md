# MEMORANDUM

## Summary

I have reviewed the Stock Purchase Agreement ("SPA"), the Escrow Agreement, the prior draft of the Escrow Agreement, the IP Assignment Agreement, and the Talin Board Consent. Below are the issues I identified, in order of importance to Talin. The most critical problem is a significant scope mismatch between the SPA's definition of "Company IP" and the IP Assignment Agreement's definition, which—combined with the SPA's order-of-precedence clause—means Talin may not be receiving the fan contact database, ticketing software, or any unregistered IP that Nasund Brands LLC holds. I also flag a survival period that expires before the 2027 Lanternfall Festival, a capped indemnity that may be inadequate, and several other issues.

## Issue 1 (Critical): IP Assignment Agreement Does Not Convey the Full Scope of "Company IP" Defined in the SPA

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Company IP")
- SPA § 4.10 (IP representation)
- SPA § 11.9 (order of precedence)
- IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Company IP")
- IP Assignment Agreement Exhibit A
- IP Assignment Agreement Recital B

**The Problem:**

The SPA defines "Company IP" broadly as *all* Intellectual Property used in the Company's business, "including without limitation" (SPA § 1.2):
- (a) the LANTERNFALL marks and all other trademarks, service marks, logos and trade dress used in connection with the Lanternfall Festival;
- (b) the Company's fan contact database; and
- (c) the Company's ticketing software.

The IP Assignment Agreement, however, redefines "Company IP" as **"solely the registered trademarks and service marks set out in Exhibit A, together with all registrations and pending applications for them"** (IP Assignment § 1.1). Exhibit A lists six registered marks and one pending application. It does not include the fan contact database, the ticketing software, any unregistered marks, trade dress, domain names, social media accounts, copyrights, or any other category of IP.

SPA § 11.9 provides: "If there is any conflict between this Agreement and any Ancillary Agreement, this Agreement shall prevail, **except that the IP Assignment Agreement shall govern the conveyance of Intellectual Property**." This means that for the actual transfer of IP, the narrow definition in the IP Assignment Agreement controls over the broader definition in the SPA.

IP Assignment Recital B confirms that Nasund Brands LLC "holds record title to certain intellectual property used in connection with the business of Assignee, including in connection with the annual Lanternfall Festival." The word "certain" is doing a lot of work here—it acknowledges that Nasund Brands holds IP used in the business but does not specify all of it.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

If Nasund Brands LLC holds the fan contact database, the ticketing software, domain names, social media accounts, or any other IP used in the Company's business, none of it is being assigned under the current IP Assignment Agreement. Talin would be paying $84 million for a company whose core operational assets (fan data, ticketing platform) remain with the Seller's affiliate. The SPA representation in § 4.10—"Upon consummation of the IP Assignment Agreement, the Company will own all Company IP held by Nasund Brands LLC"—would be false at closing if Nasund Brands retains any non-registered IP. But Talin's remedy for that breach would be subject to the $8.4 million cap (SPA § 9.5(a)) and the 120-day survival period (SPA § 9.1; see Issue 2).

**Recommended Fix:**

The IP Assignment Agreement should be conformed to the SPA's definition of Company IP. I recommend replacing IP Assignment § 1.1's definition of "Company IP" with the SPA's definition, and expanding Exhibit A to include all IP held by Nasund Brands LLC that is used in the Company's business. At minimum, the assignment should cover:

> "**Company IP**" means all Intellectual Property used in the business of the Company, including without limitation: (a) the LANTERNFALL marks and all other trademarks, service marks, logos and trade dress used in connection with the Lanternfall Festival, whether registered or unregistered; (b) the Company's fan contact database; (c) the Company's ticketing software; (d) all domain names and social media accounts used in connection with the Company's business; and (e) all copyrights, trade secrets, and other intellectual property used in or relating to the Company's business, in each case to the extent held by Assignor.

Additionally, I recommend adding a representation in the IP Assignment Agreement that Assignor identifies *all* IP it holds that is used in or relates to the Company's business, and that the Assigned Rights include all such IP. If Nasund Brands LLC does not hold the fan database or ticketing software (i.e., the Company already owns them), this should be confirmed in diligence and reflected in the Disclosure Schedule.

**Open Question:** We need to confirm through diligence who currently holds record title to the fan contact database and the ticketing software. If the Company already owns them, the scope mismatch is less severe (though still a drafting error that should be fixed). If Nasund Brands LLC holds them, this is a deal-critical gap.

## Issue 2 (High): Survival Period Expires Before the 2027 Lanternfall Festival

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.1 (survival)
- SPA § 9.4 (claims procedure; waiver for late notice)
- Escrow Agreement § 4.1 (Escrow Release Date = 120 days after Closing)
- Board Consent § 1.5(a) (Keelhaven operates under existing management through the 2027 festival in August 2027)

**The Problem:**

SPA § 9.1 provides that Seller's and Buyer's representations and warranties "shall survive the Closing until the Escrow Release Date." The Escrow Release Date is 120 days after the Closing Date (Escrow Agreement § 4.1). If closing occurs on December 1, 2026, the Escrow Release Date is approximately March 31, 2027.

SPA § 9.4 provides that "[n]o claim may be made under Section 9.2(a) or Section 9.3(a) unless a Claim Notice for it is delivered before the expiration of the applicable survival period under Section 9.1. Any claim for which a Claim Notice is not so delivered is **irrevocably waived**."

The 2027 Lanternfall Festival occurs in August 2027—approximately five months *after* the survival period expires. The Board Consent confirms that Keelhaven will continue to operate under its existing management through that festival (Board Consent § 1.5(a)).

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

The Lanternfall Festival is the Company's primary revenue driver (Board Consent § 1.2). Many pre-closing issues will not manifest until the festival or in its aftermath:
- A talent agreement that was in breach before closing (e.g., an artist's guarantee was not properly funded, or a rider obligation was not met) may not become apparent until the artist performs or demands payment in August 2027.
- A venue lease or sponsorship agreement that contains a hidden liability or an unenforceable term may not surface until the festival is being produced.
- A tax issue related to 2026 festival operations (e.g., unremitted admissions taxes) may not be identified until a post-closing audit or tax inquiry.
- A data privacy issue in the fan database (SPA § 4.11) may only become apparent when the database is used for the 2027 festival marketing.

In each of these scenarios, Talin would discover the problem after the survival period has expired, and its claim would be "irrevocably waived."

**Recommended Fix:**

I recommend extending the survival period for fundamental representations (organization, capitalization, authority, tax, litigation) to at least 18 months after closing, and for other representations to at least 12 months. Alternatively, and more simply, we could extend the survival period for all representations to 24 months after closing, which would cover the 2027 festival and the subsequent fiscal year-end (June 30, 2027) audit referenced in the Board Consent (§ 1.5(d)).

Proposed language for SPA § 9.1:

> "The representations and warranties of Seller contained in Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.5, 4.13 and 4.14 shall survive the Closing until the first anniversary of the Closing Date. The representations and warranties of Seller contained in all other Sections of Article 4 shall survive the Closing until the second anniversary of the Closing Date. The representations and warranties of Buyer shall survive the Closing until the first anniversary of the Closing Date. The covenants and agreements of the parties shall survive the Closing until fully performed in accordance with their terms."

If Seller resists, a fallback position is to extend survival for all Seller reps to 18 months (through approximately June 1, 2028), which would cover the 2027 festival and the post-festival settlement period.

Correspondingly, the Escrow Release Date in the Escrow Agreement should be extended to match.

## Issue 3 (High): Indemnification Cap May Be Inadequate

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.5(a) (cap = Escrow Amount = $8,400,000)
- SPA § 9.7 (exclusive remedy)
- SPA § 2.1 (Purchase Price = $84,000,000)

**The Problem:**

SPA § 9.5(a) caps Seller's aggregate liability for breach of representations and warranties (SPA § 9.2(a)) at the Escrow Amount of $8,400,000—10% of the Purchase Price. SPA § 9.7 makes the indemnification provisions the "sole and exclusive remedy" after closing, except for fraud and specific performance.

This means that if Seller makes a material misrepresentation that causes Talin losses exceeding $8.4 million (e.g., the Company's financial statements are materially inaccurate, a major talent agreement is in breach, or the IP is not as represented), Talin's maximum recovery is $8.4 million unless it can prove fraud.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

For an $84 million acquisition of a company whose value is heavily concentrated in a single annual festival and its associated talent commitments, a 10% cap may not provide adequate protection. The financial statements rep (SPA § 4.5) covers only two fiscal years (ended June 30, 2025 and 2026). If the Company's June 30, 2026 balance sheet materially overstates assets or understates liabilities, the resulting loss could easily exceed $8.4 million. Similarly, if the undisclosed liabilities rep (SPA § 4.6) is breached by a material amount, Talin's recovery is still capped.

Note that the cap does *not* apply to:
- Breach of Seller's covenants (SPA § 9.2(b))—uncapped
- Pre-Closing Taxes (SPA § 9.2(c))—uncapped
- Unpaid Indebtedness or Transaction Expenses (SPA § 9.2(d))—uncapped
- Fraud (SPA § 9.5(c))—uncapped

So the cap is most concerning for breaches of the financial statement, IP, and talent agreement representations.

**Recommended Fix:**

I recommend increasing the cap to 20% of the Purchase Price ($16,800,000) or, alternatively, carving out the financial statement representation (SPA § 4.5) and the IP representation (SPA § 4.10) from the cap entirely. A middle-ground position would be to set the general cap at $8.4 million but provide a separate, higher cap (e.g., $20 million) for breaches of Sections 4.5, 4.6, and 4.10.

Proposed language:

> "(a) The aggregate liability of Seller for claims under Section 9.2(a) shall not exceed $8,400,000; provided, however, that the aggregate liability of Seller for claims under Section 9.2(a) arising from any inaccuracy in or breach of Section 4.5, 4.6 or 4.10 shall not exceed $20,000,000. The aggregate liability of Buyer for claims under Section 9.3(a) shall not exceed the Escrow Amount."

## Issue 4 (High): Buyer's Indemnity for Post-Closing Show Liabilities Is Uncapped

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 9.3(b)
- SPA § 9.5(a) (cap applies only to § 9.3(a), not § 9.3(b))
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Post-Closing Show Liabilities")

**The Problem:**

SPA § 9.3(b) provides that Buyer (Talin) shall indemnify Seller for "any Post-Closing Show Liabilities, including any Post-Closing Show Liabilities for which Seller or any of its Affiliates is or becomes liable as guarantor, co-promoter or otherwise."

"Post-Closing Show Liabilities" is defined as "all Liabilities and obligations under the Talent Agreements in respect of Shows scheduled to occur after the Closing Date, including artist guarantees, deposits, advances, bonuses, rider and production obligations, and settlement obligations, whether arising before or after the Closing Date" (SPA § 1.1).

Critically, SPA § 9.5(a) caps only "the aggregate liability of Buyer for claims under Section 9.3(a)"—it does *not* cap § 9.3(b). This means Talin's indemnity to Seller for post-closing show liabilities is **uncapped**.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

In a stock purchase, the Company (now owned by Talin) assumes all post-closing obligations. So it is conceptually correct that Talin bears post-closing show liabilities. However, the indemnity in § 9.3(b) goes further: it makes Talin liable to *Seller* for post-closing show liabilities "for which Seller or any of its Affiliates is or becomes liable as guarantor, co-promoter or otherwise." This could create a situation where:

1. A talent agreement for a post-closing show names the Company as promotor and Seller (or an affiliate) as co-promoter or guarantor.
2. The show is a financial failure.
3. The artist demands payment from Seller as co-promoter/guarantor.
4. Seller pays the artist and then seeks indemnification from Talin under § 9.3(b)—with no cap.

This effectively makes Talin the backstop for any financial failure of post-closing shows where Seller has any contractual exposure, regardless of whether the failure was caused by pre-closing conduct or post-closing operational decisions made by Talin.

**Recommended Fix:**

I recommend two changes:

First, limit § 9.3(b) to post-closing show liabilities that arise from *pre-closing* acts or omissions:

> "(b) any Post-Closing Show Liabilities to the extent arising from any act or omission of the Company or Seller occurring on or before the Closing Date, including any such Post-Closing Show Liabilities for which Seller or any of its Affiliates is or becomes liable as guarantor, co-promoter or otherwise."

Second, if Seller insists on broader language, cap the § 9.3(b) indemnity at the Escrow Amount or a stated dollar amount, and provide that it does not apply to the extent the Post-Closing Show Liability results from Talin's own operational decisions after closing.

## Issue 5 (Moderate): "Ordinary Course" Carve-Out in Undisclosed Liabilities Representation

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 4.6
- SPA § 6.1 (conduct of business covenant)

**The Problem:**

SPA § 4.6 represents that the Company has no Liabilities "except those reflected or reserved against in the June 30, 2026 balance sheet, and those incurred in the ordinary course of business since that date."

The "ordinary course" carve-out means that any liability the Company incurs between June 30, 2026 and the Closing Date (up to December 1, 2026—a five-month period) in the "ordinary course" is not a breach of this representation. For a concert promoter, the ordinary course includes signing talent agreements, paying deposits, and incurring production costs. If the Company signs a new talent agreement with a $500,000 guarantee in August 2026 (within the ordinary course), that liability is carved out of the § 4.6 rep, even if it was not disclosed to Talin.

The conduct of business covenant (SPA § 6.1) restricts new Talent Agreements only if the guarantee exceeds $150,000. So a $500,000 talent deal signed between signing and closing would require Buyer's consent, but a $149,000 deal would not—and would be carved out of the undisclosed liabilities rep.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

Talin is relying on the June 30, 2026 balance sheet and the Disclosure Schedule to understand the Company's liabilities. The ordinary course carve-out creates a gap: liabilities incurred in the ordinary course between June 30 and closing that are not reflected in the balance sheet and not specifically disclosed are not covered by the rep. While the conduct of business covenant provides some protection (and its breach would not be subject to the cap under § 9.5(a)), the $150,000 threshold for talent agreements is low enough that many deals could fall within it.

**Recommended Fix:**

I recommend narrowing the ordinary course carve-out to require that such liabilities be "reflected in the Company's books and records and disclosed to Buyer in the Disclosure Schedule or in writing prior to the Closing Date." Alternatively, lower the talent agreement threshold in § 6.1(a) to $100,000 and add a requirement that all new Talent Agreements entered into between signing and closing be delivered to Buyer within five Business Days of execution.

## Issue 6 (Moderate): Board Consent Describes IP Assignment More Broadly Than the Actual Agreement

**Documents and Sections:**
- Board Consent § 1.3(c)
- IP Assignment Agreement § 1.1 and Exhibit A

**The Problem:**

Board Consent § 1.3(c) states that the IP Assignment Agreement is "assigning to Keelhaven **the intellectual property used in Keelhaven's business**." This is a broad description that encompasses all IP used in the business. However, the actual IP Assignment Agreement only assigns registered trademarks and pending applications (see Issue 1).

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

If the Board approved the transaction based on the understanding that all IP used in Keelhaven's business would be assigned, but the actual agreement only assigns registered marks, there is a potential corporate governance issue. The Authorized Officers are authorized to execute the agreement "with such changes as the Authorized Officer executing it approves" (Board Consent § 2.2), which provides some cover. But if the narrow scope of the IP Assignment was not specifically brought to the Board's attention, the approval may not be airtight. This is primarily an internal governance concern, but it could become relevant if the transaction is later challenged.

**Recommended Fix:**

Before closing, either (a) fix the IP Assignment Agreement to cover all Company IP (as recommended in Issue 1), or (b) if the narrower scope is acceptable (because the Company already owns the fan database and ticketing software), document that determination in a supplemental board consent or a memo to the Board file explaining why the registered-marks-only assignment is sufficient.

## Issue 7 (Moderate): Working Capital Adjustment Mechanics

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 2.4
- SPA § 4.5 (Financial Statements)

**The Problem:**

The working capital adjustment mechanism has several features that could work against Talin:

1. **Target of $2,500,000.** For a company that produces a major festival and promotes concerts year-round, working capital can be highly volatile depending on where in the cycle the closing falls. Closing on December 1, 2026 is after the 2026 festival (August) but before the 2027 festival. The Company may have significant ticket pre-sale liabilities or talent deposits outstanding that depress working capital, or it may have collected ticket revenue in advance that inflates it.

2. **Estimated Working Capital delivered only 3 Business Days before Closing** (SPA § 2.4(a)). This gives Talin very little time to verify the estimate or identify discrepancies.

3. **Actual calculation due within 90 days after Closing** (SPA § 2.4(b)). If Talin's calculation differs from Seller's estimate, the dispute is referred to "an independent accounting firm mutually agreed by the parties." If the parties cannot agree on the firm, there is no further mechanism specified.

4. **The adjustment is made against the Purchase Price at Closing** (SPA § 2.3(d)), meaning Talin pays the adjusted amount at closing based on Seller's estimate, and any true-up happens after closing.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

If the Estimated Working Capital is above target, Talin pays more at closing. If it's below, Talin pays less. The risk is that Seller has an incentive to present the estimate on the high side (to increase the purchase price Talin pays at closing, with the true-up coming later). More importantly, if the working capital is volatile and the 90-day true-up reveals a significant shortfall, Talin may have already paid too much at closing and must chase Seller for the difference.

**Recommended Fix:**

- Extend the delivery deadline for the Estimated Working Capital to at least 10 Business Days before Closing, to give Talin time to review.
- Add a mechanism for resolving disputes about the selection of the independent accounting firm (e.g., if the parties cannot agree within 10 days, each party selects a firm and the two firms select a third).
- Consider adding a collar or band (e.g., no adjustment if working capital is within $250,000 of target) to reduce trivial disputes, if Talin is comfortable with that trade-off.

## Issue 8 (Moderate): Non-Compete Is Narrow

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 6.6

**The Problem:**

The non-compete restricts Seller from promoting or producing "any multi-day music festival within 250 miles of the Lanternfall Festival site" for three years. This does not restrict:
- Single-day concerts or festivals
- Multi-day festivals beyond 250 miles
- Any other type of live entertainment (theaters, arenas, tours)
- Sponsorship or production services for competitors

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

The primary value of the acquisition is the Lanternfall Festival brand and its audience. If Seller (or its affiliates) can launch a competing single-day festival or a multi-day festival just outside the 250-mile radius, they could divert artists, sponsors, and fans. The 250-mile radius may also be insufficient if the festival draws from a wider geographic area.

**Recommended Fix:**

Expand the non-compete to cover:
- Any music festival (whether multi-day or single-day) within 250 miles; and
- Any multi-day music festival anywhere in the United States (or at least within a 500-mile radius).

Alternatively, add a non-solicitation covenant restricting Seller from soliciting the Company's artists, sponsors, and venue partners for two years post-closing.

## Issue 9 (Lower): Escrow Agent Fees Split Equally

**Documents and Sections:**
- Escrow Agreement § 6.8
- Escrow Agreement Schedule A

**The Problem:**

The Escrow Agent's fees (acceptance fee of $3,500, annual administration fee of $5,000, disbursement fees of $50 per wire after the first ten, plus out-of-pocket expenses) are split equally between Buyer and Seller (Escrow Agreement § 6.8). The escrow exists primarily to protect Buyer's indemnification rights, yet Buyer bears half the cost.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

This is a relatively small dollar amount (approximately $8,500+ in fees), but it is conceptually inconsistent with the purpose of the escrow. It is also worth noting that the prior draft had the fees marked "[Escrow Agent to confirm]" (prior draft, Schedule A), while the current version has them confirmed. The fees appear reasonable, but the split should be revisited.

**Recommended Fix:**

Request that Seller bear 100% of the Escrow Agent's fees, on the theory that the escrow secures Seller's indemnification obligations. If Seller resists, a fallback is 70/30 in Seller's favor.

## Issue 10 (Lower): Tax Reporting on Escrow Earnings

**Documents and Sections:**
- Escrow Agreement § 3.4

**The Problem:**

"Buyer shall be treated as the owner of the Escrow Fund, and all earnings shall be reported as income of Buyer, until disbursement." This means Talin is taxable on the interest/investment earnings generated by the $8.4 million escrow, even though the funds are held as security for Seller's indemnification obligations and may ultimately be paid to Seller.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

This is a standard provision and not unusual, but it is a cost to Talin. On $8.4 million in a money market fund, even at modest rates, the taxable earnings could be several thousand dollars per year. If the escrow is held for more than 12 months (which it would be if the survival period is extended per Issue 2), the tax cost increases.

**Recommended Fix:**

This is a minor point. If we extend the escrow period (per Issue 2), we could negotiate for Seller to reimburse Talin for the tax on escrow earnings attributable to the extended period. Alternatively, this can be left as-is given the modest dollar amount.

## Issue 12 (Lower): Knowledge Standard for Seller

**Documents and Sections:**
- SPA § 1.1 (definition of "Knowledge of Seller")

**The Problem:**

"Knowledge of Seller" means "the actual knowledge of the managing members of Seller and of the chief executive officer, chief financial officer and head of talent buying of the Company, in each case after reasonable inquiry."

This is a multi-person knowledge standard that requires "actual knowledge" (not constructive knowledge) and "reasonable inquiry." The inclusion of "after reasonable inquiry" is somewhat helpful to Talin because it imposes an affirmative duty to investigate. However, the standard is limited to four specific individuals (managing members of Seller + CEO, CFO, and head of talent buying of the Company). If a material fact is known by other employees (e.g., a venue operations manager who knows about a permit issue), it may not be imputed to Seller.

**Why It Matters for Talin:**

Several representations are qualified by "to the Knowledge of Seller" (e.g., § 4.8, § 4.10, § 4.13, § 4.14). If the relevant knowledge sits with someone outside the defined group, the representation may not be breached even though the Company had the information.

**Recommended Fix:**

Expand the knowledge group to include "the senior officers of the Company responsible for the subject matter of the applicable representation" or, at minimum, add the head of operations/venue management. Alternatively, change "actual knowledge" to "knowledge" (which in many jurisdictions includes constructive knowledge) for the Company-level knowledge component.

## Missing Documents

The following items are referenced in the deal documents but are not in the matter folder:

1. **Disclosure Schedule** – Referenced throughout the SPA (e.g., §§ 4.4, 4.8, 4.9, 4.10). We cannot assess the accuracy of the representations without seeing what has been disclosed. This is critical for evaluating whether the IP representation (§ 4.10) discloses that Nasund Brands holds the fan database or ticketing software.

2. **Payoff Letters** – Required under SPA § 7.2(c) as a condition to closing. We should review these to confirm the Indebtedness being paid off and to ensure no surprises.

3. **Financial Statements** – SPA § 4.5 references audited balance sheets as of June 30, 2025 and 2026. We should review these to assess the working capital target and to identify any red flags.

4. **Revolving Credit Facility Agreement** – The Board Consent (§ 1.6, § 4.2) references borrowings of up to $50 million under Talin's existing facility. We should confirm the facility has sufficient availability and that no covenant is triggered by the transaction.

5. **Evidence of Ownership of Fan Database and Ticketing Software** – As noted in Issue 1, we need to confirm who holds these assets. If they are held by the Company, no assignment is needed. If held by Nasund Brands LLC, the IP Assignment must be expanded.

6. **Talent Agreements** – SPA § 4.9 references a schedule of post-closing talent agreements. We should review the actual agreements to assess the magnitude of Post-Closing Show Liabilities (relevant to Issue 4).

7. **Venue Leases and Sponsorship Agreements** – Referenced in SPA § 4.8 as Material Contracts. These should be reviewed for change-of-control provisions, assignment restrictions, or other issues.

## Recommended Priorities for Negotiation

In order of priority, I recommend we focus on:

1. **Fix the IP Assignment Agreement** to cover all Company IP (Issue 1). This is a deal-critical item. If Nasund Brands holds the fan database or ticketing software, Talin is not getting them under the current documents.
2. **Extend the survival period** to at least 18 months, or carve out the 2027 festival from the waiver (Issue 2).
3. **Increase the indemnification cap** or carve out key reps from the cap (Issue 3).
4. **Limit or cap the Post-Closing Show Liabilities indemnity** (Issue 4).
5. **Tighten the ordinary course carve-out** in the undisclosed liabilities rep (Issue 5).
6. **Expand the non-compete** (Issue 8).

Items 5–12 are lower priority and can be addressed in cleanup if time permits.
