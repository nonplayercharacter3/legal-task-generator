## Summary

Vepous faces three interlocking problems that arose in the last six weeks: (1) its parent's pending sale to Larchfield Mobility Partners, L.P. constitutes a Change of Control under the Supply Agreement that Milon has already flagged as a material breach; (2) Amendment No. 1, signed in June 2026, quietly narrowed the uncapped-liability exclusions so that product-defect bodily injury claims are now subject to a roughly $1.85 million cap; and (3) Milon's proposed assignment of the Agreement to Rookwood Transit Supply, LLC (an affiliate of a potential competitor) will strip away the Parent Guaranty as a practical credit support. These issues compound each other: if Milon (or post-assignment, Rookwood) terminates for the Change of Control, Vepous owes liquidated damages of $3.7 million to $5.55 million depending on timing, and it loses its exclusive supply relationship for brake, steering, suspension and child-check alarm components with no guarantor backstop.

I have identified twelve issues, ordered by importance to the client.

## Issue 1 – Change of Control Breach: Larchfield Transaction Triggers Section 14.3

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 1.4 (definition of "Change of Control")
- Supply Agreement § 14.3 (consequences of unconsented Change of Control)
- Milon Notice Letter §§ 3.1–3.3, 4.1 (Milon's express reservation of rights)

**Analysis:**

Section 1.4 defines Change of Control to include "a sale, transfer or issuance of a controlling interest in Customer or in any entity that directly or indirectly controls Customer." Vepous Transit Holdings, Inc. is Customer's parent; Larchfield's acquisition of a controlling interest in Vepous Transit Holdings is squarely within the definition. Milon's notice letter confirms that no consent was requested or given (Notice Letter § 3.3).

Section 14.3 makes an unconsented Change of Control a material breach for which the 30-day cure period does not apply. Milon (or post-assignment, Rookwood) may then:

(a) terminate the Agreement on 30 days' written notice; and

(b) recover liquidated damages equal to the Annual Minimum Purchase Commitment ($1,850,000) for each remaining Contract Year of the Initial Term, less invoiced purchases in the then-current year.

The Initial Term runs through February 28, 2030. If the Larchfield Transaction closes during Contract Year 2 (March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027), three full Contract Years remain (CY3, CY4, CY5), yielding liquidated damages of approximately **$5,550,000** (less whatever Vepous has already purchased in CY2). If closing occurs in CY3, the figure drops to approximately **$3,700,000**.

Milon has expressly reserved these rights (Notice Letter § 4.1) and stated they will pass to Rookwood on the Assignment Date (December 1, 2026). Milon is not required to terminate now; it can wait until after the assignment and let Rookwood exercise the right. This gives Vepous a narrow window to act before December 1.

**Why it matters:** This is the single largest financial exposure in the file. A termination would also strand Vepous without a supply source for safety-critical components (brakes, child-check alarms) on 30 days' notice, creating operational and regulatory risk for a school bus operator.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Immediately open a consent negotiation with Milon** (and, given the pending assignment, with Rookwood as well). The goal is to obtain written consent to the Larchfield Transaction under § 14.3, or a negotiated waiver of the liquidated damages in exchange for a commitment (e.g., a shortened term, a reduced AMC, or a termination fee at a fixed lower amount).

2. **Do not respond to Milon's information request (Notice Letter § 5.1) with the closing date until a consent strategy is set.** Providing the closing date gives Milon/Rookwood the timing to issue a termination notice the moment the transaction closes. If a response is required to preserve the relationship, provide only a range (e.g., "expected to close in Q1 2027") and state that Vepous is in discussions regarding consent.

3. **Prepare a fallback position.** If consent is refused, assess whether to (a) challenge the liquidated damages clause as an unenforceable penalty under New York law (see Issue 11), or (b) negotiate a buyout of the remaining term at a discount.

4. **Confirm with the client** the expected closing date of the Larchfield Transaction and whether Larchfield has been informed of the supply agreement issue.

## Issue 2 – Amendment No. 1 Capped Product-Defect Bodily Injury Claims

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 11.1 (exclusion of consequential damages)
- Supply Agreement § 11.2 (cap: 12 months' fees)
- Supply Agreement § 11.3 (original exclusions from cap – now superseded)
- Amendment No. 1 § 3.1 (revised § 11.3)
- Amendment No. 1 § 3.2 (applies to claims made on or after June 16, 2026)

**Analysis:**

Original § 11.3(a) excluded **all** Section 10.1 indemnities from the cap, expressly including "10.1(b) product-defect bodily injury claims." This meant that if a brake, steering component, or child-check alarm failed and caused bodily injury or death, Supplier's indemnification obligation was uncapped.

Amendment No. 1 § 3.1 replaced § 11.3(a) with a narrower exclusion covering only "Supplier's indemnification obligations for third-party claims arising **solely** under Section 10.1(a)" — i.e., IP infringement claims. The result:

- **Section 10.1(b)** (bodily injury, death, or property damage from a Product Defect, including brake/steering/suspension/child-check alarm failures) is now **subject to the § 11.2 cap** of fees paid in the preceding 12 months (approximately $1,850,000).
- **Section 10.1(c)** (Supplier's breach of regulatory compliance or gross negligence/willful misconduct) is also now capped, except to the extent it constitutes gross negligence, fraud, or willful misconduct under § 11.3(d).

For a school bus operator, a single serious accident caused by a brake failure could generate claims well in excess of $1.85 million. The Amendment effectively transferred that catastrophic risk to Vepous.

The Amendment states it applies to "all claims made on or after the Amendment Date" (June 16, 2026). Any claim arising from a defect that manifests after that date is capped.

**Why it matters:** This is a material reduction in Vepous's protection against the most severe risk in the relationship — injury to children from component failure. It was buried in a pricing amendment and may not have received the scrutiny it warranted.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Raise this as a negotiation point in the consent discussion (Issue 1).** Vepous's leverage is limited, but the ask should be: restore the original § 11.3(a) language so that § 10.1(b) claims remain uncapped, or at minimum raise the cap for bodily injury claims to a stated dollar amount (e.g., $10 million) tied to Supplier's insurance limits.

2. **Confirm that Milon's (and post-assignment, Rookwood's) product liability insurance (required at $5 million per occurrence under § 12.1) is sufficient to cover the gap between the cap and a worst-case claim.** If the insurance is $5M and the cap is $1.85M, Vepous has a $3.15M uninsured gap for a single occurrence.

3. **If the client cannot restore the uncapped treatment, require Rookwood to maintain product liability insurance of no less than $10 million per occurrence as a condition of accepting the assignment.**

4. **Document the client's awareness** of this change for the file. If the client's business team negotiated the Amendment without flagging the § 11.3 change, that is an internal process issue to note.

## Issue 3 – Parent Guaranty Becomes Ineffective Upon Assignment to Rookwood

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 15.3 (requirement of Parent Guaranty as condition to Agreement)
- Parent Guaranty § 1.2 (definition of "Guaranteed Obligations" = Supplier's obligations)
- Parent Guaranty § 2.1–2.3 (scope of guaranty)
- Parent Guaranty § 6.2 (notification if Guarantor ceases to own majority of Supplier)
- Milon Notice Letter § 2.1–2.3 (assignment to Rookwood)

**Analysis:**

The Parent Guaranty from Milon Industrial Holdings, Inc. guarantees "all obligations and liabilities of **Supplier** to Customer under the Supply Agreement" (Guaranty § 1.2). It is a guaranty of payment and performance, with Guarantor as primary obligor (Guaranty § 2.1, § 3.3).

When Supplier assigns the Agreement to Rookwood under § 14.2, Supplier's obligations transfer to Rookwood. The Guaranty does not by its terms extend to Rookwood's obligations. There is no provision in the Guaranty that tracks an assignment of the underlying Agreement. Post-assignment, the Guaranty covers only Supplier's residual obligations (e.g., pre-assignment Purchase Orders under Notice Letter § 2.5), which will be minimal.

Additionally, if Milon sells its distribution business to Rookwood, Milon Industrial Holdings may cease to own a majority of Milon Fleet Parts Corp., triggering the notification covenant in Guaranty § 6.2. While that event does not release the Guarantor, it signals the parent is divesting and may not be in a position (or willing) to honor the Guaranty for any residual obligations.

**Why it matters:** Vepous entered the Agreement with a credit enhancement — a parent guaranty from Milon Industrial Holdings. The assignment to Rookwood, a subsidiary of an unrelated company (Kestrel Student Coach, Inc.), eliminates that enhancement. Vepous would be looking solely to Rookwood's balance sheet for performance of safety-critical supply obligations, warranty claims, indemnities, and recall costs.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Require, as a condition to accepting the assignment, that Rookwood deliver a replacement guaranty** from Kestrel Student Coach, Inc. (or Rookwood's parent) on terms no less favorable than the existing Parent Guaranty. Propose language:

> "As a condition to Customer's consent to the assignment contemplated by Supplier's notice dated October 19, 2026, Rookwood Transit Supply, LLC shall cause its parent entity, [Kestrel Student Coach, Inc. / applicable parent], to deliver to Customer a guaranty of Rookwood's obligations under the Agreement, in form and substance no less favorable than the Parent Guaranty executed by Milon Industrial Holdings, Inc. dated March 1, 2025, including the provisions of Sections 2 (Guaranty), 3 (Nature of Guaranty), 4 (Waivers and Consents), and 6 (Covenants) thereof."

2. **Alternatively, require Rookwood to post a letter of credit or surety bond** in an amount equal to 12 months of the Annual Minimum Purchase Commitment ($1,850,000) as security for its obligations.

3. **Do not accept the written assumption instrument from Rookwood** (referenced in Notice Letter § 2.3) without first securing the replacement credit support.

4. **Send a letter to Milon Industrial Holdings, Inc.** (cc'd on the Notice Letter) requesting confirmation of its continued ownership of Milon Fleet Parts Corp. post-transaction and its position on the Guaranty.

## Issue 4 – Assignment to Potential Competitor: Data and Competitive Risk

**Documents and Sections:**
- Milon Notice Letter § 2.2 (Rookwood is affiliate of Kestrel Student Coach, Inc.)
- Milon Notice Letter § 2.4(c) (transfer of Customer's pricing, PO history, forecasts, volumes, depot delivery info, and traceability records to Rookwood)
- Supply Agreement § 3.1 (exclusivity extends to Affiliates)
- Supply Agreement § 9 (confidentiality)
- NDA § 1.1(b)–(c) (fleet size, maintenance records, route, school district contract information)

**Analysis:**

The name "Kestrel Student Coach, Inc." strongly suggests a student transportation operator — a direct competitor to Vepous. If confirmed, the assignment transfers to a competitor's affiliate:

- Vepous's unit pricing and volume data
- Purchase Order history
- Rolling 12-month forecasts
- Covered Depot locations and delivery patterns
- Traceability records (which reveal fleet composition and part usage by vehicle)
- Potentially route and school district contract information (covered by both the NDA and § 9)

While § 9 and the NDA impose confidentiality obligations, the practical reality is that the data now sits within the competitive orbit of a rival. The NDA's disclosure period ends January 12, 2027 (NDA § 7.1), and its obligations then run for three more years (NDA § 7.2). Section 9 of the Supply Agreement survives for three years post-termination (§ 9.3). These provide some protection, but they are remedies after the fact, not preventives.

Additionally, § 3.1's exclusivity obligation extends to Customer's Affiliates. If the Larchfield Transaction brings new entities into Vepous's Affiliate group, their fleet component purchases must also flow through the supplier. Milon's request for the post-transaction Affiliate list (Notice Letter § 5.1(b)) is likely aimed at confirming the scope of this obligation.

**Why it matters:** Competitive intelligence risk is real but secondary to the financial and operational issues. It should, however, inform the negotiation strategy: Vepous can use the competitor angle to resist the assignment or to extract concessions (e.g., a data-separation protocol, a shortened term, or a consent fee).

**Recommendation:**

1. **Confirm with the client whether Kestrel Student Coach, Inc. is a competitor** (or has any student transportation operations on Long Island or elsewhere).

2. **If confirmed, raise the competitive concern in the consent/assignment negotiation.** Vepous is not contractually required to consent to the assignment (§ 14.2 does not require Customer consent), but it can make consent to the broader transaction (including the Change of Control) conditional on:
   - A data-separation protocol: Rookwood shall wall off Vepous's commercial data from any entity within the Kestrel group that operates student transportation.
   - A contractual non-compete or non-use covenant: Rookwood and its Affiliates shall not use Vepous's Confidential Information to compete with Vepous in pupil transportation services.

3. **If the client is unwilling to accept a competitor's affiliate as its exclusive supplier**, this becomes a leverage point: Vepous can condition its consent to the Larchfield Transaction on the right to terminate the supply agreement at closing (with a negotiated exit fee rather than the full § 14.3 liquidated damages), allowing Vepous to source from a non-competitor going forward.

## Issue 6 – Exclusivity Overbreadth: "Fleet Components" Definition

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 1.3 (definition of "Fleet Components")
- Supply Agreement § 3.1 (exclusive supply obligation)
- Supply Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Products" limited to Schedule A items)

**Analysis:**

Section 1.3 defines "Fleet Components" as brake, steering and suspension components, child-check alarm systems, **and any part, assembly or kit that is the same as, or serves the same function as, any Product, for use on any school bus, activity bus or other passenger vehicle.**

This is broader than "Products" (which are limited to the items in Schedule A). The "serves the same function" language could capture parts not listed in Schedule A — for example, a different brand's brake pad that "serves the same function" as the MFP-BR-1100, or a suspension component for a vehicle type not currently in Vepous's fleet.

Section 3.1 prohibits Vepous and its Affiliates from purchasing Fleet Components from any third party or having them manufactured. If the definition is read broadly, Vepous could be in breach by purchasing a functionally equivalent part from another supplier, even if that part is not in Schedule A.

**Why it matters:** In a school bus operation, maintenance needs can be unpredictable. If Vepous's shop identifies a need for a component that is not in Schedule A but "serves the same function" as one that is, the exclusivity clause could be read to require Vepous to buy it from Milon/Rookwood — at a price that may not exist in Schedule A. This creates both a compliance risk and a pricing leverage issue for the supplier.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Seek clarification or amendment** limiting the exclusivity obligation in § 3.1 to "Products" as defined in § 1.1 (i.e., items listed in Schedule A), or to "Fleet Components" that are specifically identified in writing by the supplier as available for supply.

2. **Proposed language** (for inclusion in a consent agreement or side letter):

> "For purposes of Section 3.1, the exclusive supply obligation shall apply only to Products listed in Schedule A (as updated by signed writing) and to Fleet Components that the Supplier has confirmed in writing are available for supply under this Agreement. Customer shall not be in breach of Section 3.1 by purchasing a part, assembly or kit from a third party if the Supplier has not confirmed in writing that it will supply that item."

3. **Internally, instruct Vepous's maintenance team** to flag any part need that is not in Schedule A before purchasing, to avoid an inadvertent breach while the definition remains as drafted.

## Issue 8 – Annual Minimum Purchase Commitment: Interaction with Termination

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 3.4 (AMC of $1,850,000 per Contract Year; shortfall invoiced within 60 days)
- Supply Agreement § 14.3 (liquidated damages on Change of Control termination)
- Amendment No. 1 § 2.4 (AMC unchanged at $1,850,000)

**Analysis:**

If the Agreement is terminated under § 14.3, Vepous owes the § 14.3 liquidated damages (AMC for remaining years). Separately, if Vepous's actual purchases in the then-current Contract Year fall below $1,850,000, Milon/Rookwood could also invoice a shortfall under § 3.4. The § 14.3 formula already deducts "the invoiced value of Products Customer has purchased in the then-current Contract Year," so there should be no double-counting for the current year. However, the interaction is not perfectly clear: does § 3.4's shortfall right survive termination? Section 13.4 lists the surviving sections (7.3, 7.4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15) and does not include § 3.4. This suggests the AMC shortfall right does not survive termination, and § 14.3's liquidated damages are the exclusive remedy for the un-purchased minimum.

**Why it matters:** Confirming that § 3.4 does not survive termination prevents a double-recovery argument by Milon/Rookwood. This is a defensive point to preserve.

**Recommendation:**

1. **In any consent agreement or side letter, confirm that § 14.3 liquidated damages are the exclusive remedy for the un-purchased minimum following a Change of Control termination,** and that § 3.4's shortfall invoicing right does not apply post-termination.

2. **Track Vepous's actual purchases against the $1,850,000 AMC** for Contract Year 2 (March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027) to know the exact number that would be deducted from any liquidated damages calculation.

## Issue 9 – Front Leaf Spring Assembly Price Increase

**Documents and Sections:**
- Amendment No. 1 § 2.1 (MFP-SU-3100: $498.00 → $512.00, a 2.81% increase)

**Analysis:**

All other products in the Amendment received price decreases. The front leaf spring assembly is the only product that increased. The Amendment provides no explanation. While 2.81% is within the § 4.2 adjustment cap (lesser of 4% or PPI change), § 4.2 applies only from the third Contract Year. In the second Contract Year, prices are supposed to be fixed (§ 4.1: "prices are fixed for the first two Contract Years"). The Amendment purports to change prices during the second Contract Year, which is permissible only by mutual agreement (which the Amendment represents).

The anomaly is not legally problematic (the parties agreed), but it is worth noting for the file and for any future negotiation: it suggests Milon identified this part as a margin priority.

**Recommendation:**

No immediate action required. Note for the file. If Vepous negotiates a consent agreement, it could request that the front leaf spring price be restored to $498.00 as a concession, given that all other prices moved in Vepous's favor.

## Issue 10 – NDA Scope and Timing

**Documents and Sections:**
- NDA § 7.1 (disclosure period: two years from January 13, 2025 = ends January 12, 2027)
- NDA § 7.2 (obligations continue three years after disclosure period ends; trade secrets protected indefinitely)
- NDA § 1.1(b)–(c) (covers fleet size, maintenance records, route, school district contract information)
- Supply Agreement § 9.1–9.3 (confidentiality; survives three years post-termination)

**Analysis:**

The NDA and § 9 of the Supply Agreement overlap but are not identical. The NDA covers information disclosed "in connection with the Purpose" (evaluating and negotiating the supply relationship) and includes categories like "school district contract" information that § 9 does not expressly mention. The NDA's disclosure period ends January 12, 2027 — meaning no new information is protected by the NDA after that date, though previously disclosed information remains protected.

The practical concern: when Rookwood receives Vepous's data under the assignment (Notice Letter § 2.4(c)), some of that data may have been disclosed during the NDA period (e.g., fleet composition, route data shared during negotiations). The NDA binds Milon and its Representatives. Rookwood, as an assignee, is not a party to the NDA. The NDA's assignment clause (§ 9.4) requires consent for assignment, except to a successor to all or substantially all of the business. If Rookwood is acquiring only Milon's "school bus replacement components distribution business" (Notice Letter § 2.2), it may not be a successor to "all or substantially all" of Milon's business, meaning the NDA may not have transferred to Rookwood.

**Why it matters:** Vepous's most sensitive operational data (routes, school district contracts, fleet composition) may lose its NDA protection upon the assignment if Rookwood is not bound by the NDA.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Require Rookwood to execute a joinder to the NDA** (or a new confidentiality agreement on equivalent terms) as a condition of the assignment.

2. **Proposed language:**

> "As a condition to Customer's consent to the assignment, Rookwood Transit Supply, LLC shall execute and deliver a joinder to the Mutual Nondisclosure Agreement dated January 13, 2025 between Customer and Supplier, agreeing to be bound by its terms as if it were an original party thereto, with respect to all Confidential Information of Customer disclosed to Supplier or transferred to Rookwood in connection with the assignment."

## Issue 11 – Enforceability of Section 14.3 Liquidated Damages (Fallback Defense)

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 14.3 (liquidated damages formula)
- New York law on liquidated damages (e.g., *Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent*, 252 N.Y. 224 (1928); *Mackintosh v. United Parcel Service*, 2012 NY Slip Op)

**Analysis:**

Under New York law, a liquidated damages clause is enforceable only if, at the time of contracting: (1) the damages from a breach were difficult to estimate, and (2) the amount set is a reasonable forecast of actual damages and not a penalty.

The § 14.3 formula — AMC for all remaining Contract Years minus current-year purchases — may overstate actual damages. If Vepous is terminated mid-CY3, Milon/Rookwood's actual loss is the profit it would have earned on future sales, not the full revenue of the AMC. The difference between revenue and profit can be substantial. A court could find the clause punitive.

However, this is a **fallback defense**, not a primary strategy. Litigating enforceability is costly, uncertain, and would not restore the supply relationship. It is useful only if negotiations break down.

**Recommendation:**

1. **Preserve the argument** by not waiving it in any correspondence. Do not acknowledge the liquidated damages figure as owed.

2. **If negotiations fail and Milon/Rookwood terminates,** Vepous's response should contest the amount, arguing that the formula exceeds a reasonable forecast of actual loss and operates as a penalty.

3. **This defense is stronger if Vepous can show that Milon/Rookwood's actual margin on the products is low** (e.g., if the products are commoditized and the supplier's profit is 10–15%, the actual loss from losing the account is far less than the full AMC).

## Issue 12 – "Vehicle-Down" Delivery: No Specific Remedy for Failure

**Documents and Sections:**
- Supply Agreement § 5.2 (one-business-day delivery for "vehicle-down" or safety-critical items)
- Supply Agreement § 5.4 (substitute purchase right if Supplier "cannot fill any accepted Purchase Order within the applicable delivery period")

**Analysis:**

Section 5.2 requires one-business-day delivery for items Vepous designates as "vehicle-down" or safety-critical. If Supplier misses that deadline, § 5.4 applies: Vepous can buy from a third party and Supplier reimburses the cost difference. However, § 5.4 does not address the operational cost of a bus being out of service (lost revenue, substitute transportation for students, potential regulatory exposure). Those would be consequential damages, which are excluded by § 11.1.

For a school bus operator, a brake failure that takes a bus out of service before the morning route is an emergency. The current remedy (cost difference on the part) does not compensate for the operational disruption.

**Why it matters:** This is a gap in Vepous's protection, but it is lower in priority than the issues above because it is a recurring operational risk rather than a one-time catastrophic exposure.

**Recommendation:**

1. **In the consent/negotiation package, request a service-level credit** for missed "vehicle-down" deliveries:

> "If Supplier fails to deliver a Product designated as 'vehicle-down' or safety-critical within one business day of acceptance, Supplier shall credit Customer $[amount] per vehicle per day of delay, in addition to Customer's rights under Section 5.4. This credit shall not be subject to the limitations in Section 11."

2. **Alternatively, require Supplier/Rookwood to maintain a dedicated emergency stock** at or near Vepous's depots (beyond the 30-day safety stock in § 5.5) specifically for brake and child-check alarm components, with a 4-hour delivery commitment for vehicle-down situations.
