### 1. Praitir Has Likely Missed the Deadline to Dispute the Closing Inventory Value

**Where:** APA §2.6(a)–(c); Board Consent Recital C–D and Resolution 2

**Facts.** Glailirt delivered the Closing Statement on March 30, 2026 (Board Consent Recital C). Section 2.6(b) gives Buyer thirty (30) days after delivery to serve a Notice of Disagreement. That deadline expired on **April 29, 2026**. The Board Consent authorizing the officers to deliver the Notice is dated **May 12, 2026**—thirteen days late.

**Why it matters.** Section 2.6(c) provides: "If Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement within the Review Period, the Closing Statement and the Closing Inventory Value set forth in it shall be final and binding on the Parties." Glailirt's Closing Statement values inventory at $1,326,450; Praitir's internal estimate is $1,048,900 (Board Consent Exhibit B). The delta is **$277,550**. If the Closing Statement is now final, Praitir owes Glailirt an additional $111,650 under §2.6(e) (the difference between the Closing Statement value and the Target Inventory Value, minus what was already paid at Closing based on the lower Estimated Closing Inventory Value of $1,214,800).

**Potential arguments (all weak):**
- *Delivery date.* The Closing Statement was sent by email on March 30. Under APA §11.2(c), email notice is effective "on the date sent…if sent during normal business hours." There is no evidence it was sent after hours. The overnight courier copy does not extend the period.
- *Fraud or bad faith.* Section 2.6(c) contains no carve-out for fraud. Section 9.5(c) carves fraud out of the Deductible and Cap, but that applies to Article IX indemnification, not to the §2.6 purchase-price adjustment mechanism. A fraud claim would be a separate cause of action, but it would need to be pleaded and proven, and it is an uphill fight against a sophisticated counterparty with its own accountants.
- *Unconscionability / penalty.* Unlikely to succeed between two commercial entities represented by counsel.

**Recommended fix.** We need to decide whether to (a) pay the $111,650 and preserve the relationship for the pending Copperline Sale, or (b) deliver the Notice of Disagreement anyway and argue that the deadline is not jurisdictional or that Glailirt waived strict compliance by continuing to negotiate. Option (b) risks a counterclaim for breach and could complicate the Copperline closing. I recommend we discuss strategy before any communication goes out to Glailirt. **This is the most time-sensitive issue in the file.**

### 2. The Copperline Sale Will Trigger Seller's Right to Terminate the Transition Services Agreement

**Where:** TSA §12.2; APA §11.4, §11.9; Board Consent Recital E, Resolution 3

**Facts.** The Transition Period is 180 days from the March 2, 2026 Closing, expiring approximately **August 29, 2026** (TSA §1.1). The Copperline Sale is expected to close on or about **July 15, 2026** (Board Consent Recital E). TSA §12.2 provides:

> "any change of control of Buyer shall be deemed an assignment…If any assignment or deemed assignment occurs without Seller's prior written consent, Seller may terminate this Agreement upon ten (10) days' notice to Buyer."

The sale of 100% of Praitir's membership interests to Copperline is unambiguously "a transfer of a majority of the equity interests or voting power of Buyer" or "a transfer of the right to direct the management and policies of Buyer."

APA §11.4 permits Buyer to assign the APA "to any purchaser of all or substantially all of the Business" without Seller's consent, but APA §11.9 provides that "the Transition Services Agreement shall control as to the Services and the Service Fees." The TSA's own §12.2 is the governing provision, and it contains no such carve-out.

**Why it matters.** If Seller terminates the TSA ten days after the Copperline closing (i.e., around July 25, 2026), Praitir loses—on roughly two weeks' notice—all transition services: production planning (A-1), QA and food safety support (A-2), procurement (A-3), logistics and distribution (A-4), ERP and IT systems access (A-5), and finance/accounting support (A-7). Praitir has not yet completed migration of any of these functions (Board Consent Resolution 5.2(b) contemplates ongoing migration reporting). A sudden loss of the ERP system alone would disrupt order intake, production scheduling, and inventory management. The loss of QA and food safety support creates regulatory and product-liability exposure.

**Recommended fix.** Before the Copperline Sale closes, Praitir must either:
- (a) obtain Seller's written consent to the change of control under TSA §12.2 (the Board has authorized officers to seek "Required Consents" under Resolution 4, but the TSA consent is **not** listed on Exhibit A to the Board Consent—only the Credit Agreement and Equipment Lease consents are); or
- (b) negotiate a TSA amendment or extension that survives the change of control; or
- (c) accelerate migration of all Services to third-party providers before July 15.

Option (a) is the most practical. I recommend we add the TSA consent to the Required Consents list and approach Glailirt well before the Copperline closing. We should also consider whether Copperline's MIPA interim covenants or closing conditions address this. **This needs to be raised with the client immediately because the Board Consent as drafted does not cover it.**

### 3. Praitir Is Contractually Obligated to Pay Occupancy Costs for the Secaucus Depot, a Facility It Did Not Acquire

**Where:** TSA §4.3; APA §1.1 ("Facilities"), §2.2(d) (Excluded Assets)

**Facts.** TSA §4.3 requires Buyer to reimburse Seller for "all rent, utilities…real estate taxes, insurance premiums allocable to the premises, and repair and maintenance costs of the **Facilities**." "Facilities" is defined in APA §1.1 as "the Bronx Plant **and the Secaucus Depot**." The Secaucus Depot is an Excluded Asset (APA §2.2(d)): Praitir did not acquire the lease, the leasehold interest, or any personal property there.

**Why it matters.** Praitir is paying for the occupancy of a New Jersey distribution depot it does not own, lease, or use. The Secaucus Depot is at 600 Meadowlands Parkway, Secaucus, NJ. Depending on the size of the facility and its cost structure, this could be a five-figure monthly drain for the full 180-day Transition Period (and any extension).

**Recommended fix.** Raise with Glailirt as a correction. The reference to "Facilities" in TSA §4.3 should be limited to the Bronx Plant, or the Secaucus Depot should be expressly excluded. If Glailirt resists, the fallback is to argue that costs "attributable to the Transition Period" for the Secaucus Depot are not attributable to the Services being provided to Praitir (TSA §4.3 requires the costs be "attributable to the Transition Period," and one could argue they are not attributable to Praitir's benefit).

### 4. Seller's Liability Under the TSA Is Capped at Total Service Fees

**Where:** TSA §10.2

**Facts.** "The aggregate liability of Seller under this Agreement shall not exceed the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement." The Monthly Service Fee is $142,000 × 6 months = $852,000, plus Pass-Through Costs and Occupancy Costs (which are also part of "Service Fees" under TSA §1.1). The total cap is likely in the range of $1.0–$1.2 million.

**Why it matters.** The Services include production planning, QA/food safety, and logistics. If a food safety failure occurs during the Transition Period because of Seller's negligent performance (e.g., a missed environmental monitoring result, a sanitation lapse), the resulting recall costs, customer credits, and regulatory penalties could far exceed the cap. The cap does not apply to fraud, gross negligence, willful misconduct, breaches of the data/confidentiality sections, or indemnification under TSA §11—but ordinary negligence in performing the Services is covered by the cap.

**Recommended fix.** This is harder to fix post-Closing. Going forward, Praitir should (a) ensure its own insurance program (product liability, recall) is in place and adequate to cover the gap; (b) document any food safety deviations promptly to preserve a gross-negligence argument if needed; and (c) if negotiating the TSA consent/extension for the Copperline Sale (Issue 2), seek to raise the cap or carve out food-safety-related losses.

### 5. Praitir Assumes All WARN Act and Severance Liabilities, Including Those Arising from Pre-Closing Conduct

**Where:** APA §6.8(b)

**Facts.** "Buyer shall be responsible for, and shall pay, perform and discharge when due, all Liabilities with respect to…all severance and separation pay, and all Liabilities under the WARN Act, in each case relating to the Business Employees, **whether arising before, on or after the Closing Date**."

**Why it matters.** If Seller made workforce-reduction decisions before Closing that trigger WARN (60-day notice requirement) or generate severance obligations, Praitir bears the cost. This is broader than the typical "post-Closing only" assumption. The Excluded Liabilities in APA §2.4(a) would normally sweep pre-Closing employment liabilities back to Seller, but §6.8(b) is a specific assumption that overrides the general exclusion (see §2.3(c), which incorporates §6.8 liabilities into the Assumed Liabilities).

**Recommended fix.** Confirm with the client whether any Business Employees were separated or had their hours reduced in the 90 days before Closing. If so, quantify the exposure and consider whether it gives rise to an indemnification claim under §9.2(b) (breach of Seller's covenants) or §9.2(d) (pre-Closing third-party claims). The representation in §4.17(c) that Seller "has not implemented any plant closing or mass layoff within the ninety (90) days before the date of this Agreement" provides a basis for a claim if that turns out to be inaccurate.

### 6. Survival Period for Representations and Warranties Is Relatively Short

**Where:** APA §9.1; TSA §1.1

**Facts.** All reps and warranties survive until "sixty (60) days after the expiration of the Transition Period." The Transition Period is 180 days. Total survival: **240 days** (approximately 8 months from Closing, i.e., until roughly November 7, 2026).

**Why it matters.** For a food manufacturing business, 8 months may not be enough to surface all issues:
- *Environmental.* Latent contamination (e.g., in the groundwater or soil at the Bronx Plant) may not be discovered within 8 months. However, environmental liabilities are Excluded Liabilities (APA §2.4(f)) and are covered by the §9.2(c) indemnification, which is not subject to the survival limit for covenants (covenants "survive the Closing indefinitely or for the period explicitly specified in them" per §9.1).
- *Product liability.* A latent defect in a product manufactured just before Closing could result in a claim filed after the survival period. But §9.2(d) covers "any third-party claim…conducted, existing or arising on or before the Closing Date," and this is a covenant-based indemnity (not a rep/warranty), so it survives indefinitely.
- *Financial statements / undisclosed liabilities.* If a material pre-Closing liability (e.g., an unpaid tax assessment or a supplier claim) surfaces after 240 days, the §9.2(a) indemnity for breach of the §4.5 rep would be time-barred.

**Recommended fix.** The structure is actually more protective than it first appears because the key pre-Closing risks (environmental, product liability, third-party claims) are covered by the covenant-based indemnities in §9.2(c) and (d), which survive indefinitely. The 240-day survival applies only to the reps and warranties in §9.2(a). The main gap is undisclosed liabilities that are not "third-party claims" (e.g., a contingent tax liability that is not yet the subject of an Action). This is a lower-priority issue given the other exposures.

**Facts.** The New York State Department of Agriculture and Markets issued a letter confirming Praitir may continue operations under Seller's existing Article 20-C food processing license "provided the licensed operation continues uninterrupted" (Email Thread Message 13). Seller agreed to keep its license in place until Praitir's license issues (Message 14).

**Why it matters.** If operations at the Bronx Plant are interrupted for any reason (equipment failure, utility outage, labor dispute, or a food safety shutdown), Praitir could lose the right to operate under the borrowed license and would need its own license in place before resuming. This creates operational fragility during the Transition Period, when Praitir is still dependent on Seller's personnel and systems.

**Recommended fix.** Confirm with the client that Praitir's own Ag & Markets license application is progressing and estimate the expected issuance date. In the interim, ensure business continuity planning covers the scenario of an operational interruption. This is an operational risk more than a legal one, but it interacts with the TSA termination risk (Issue 2): if Seller terminates the TSA and Praitir cannot maintain uninterrupted operations, the license issue compounds.
