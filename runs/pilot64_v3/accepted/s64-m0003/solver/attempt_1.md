### 1. Lapsed Review Period – Inventory Dispute Is Contractually Foreclosed

**Documents:** APA § 2.6(a)–(c); Board Written Consent, Recital C and Resolution 2

The Closing Statement was delivered by Glailirt on **March 30, 2026** (Board Consent, Recital C). Section 2.6(b) of the APA gives Buyer **thirty (30) days** after delivery to serve a Notice of Disagreement. That deadline expired on **April 29, 2026**.

Section 2.6(c) provides:

> "If Buyer does not deliver a Notice of Disagreement within the Review Period, the Closing Statement and the Closing Inventory Value set forth in it shall be final and binding on the Parties."

The Board consent authorizing the Notice of Disagreement is dated **May 12, 2026** – thirteen days after the deadline. Resolution 2.2 directs the Authorized Officers to "prepare, execute and deliver" the Notice, but the contract no longer permits it.

**Why it matters:** Glailirt has asserted that Praitir owes an additional **$111,650** (the difference between the Closing Statement value of $1,326,450 and the Estimated Closing Inventory Value of $1,214,800, measured against the Target Inventory Value on Schedule 2.6). Praitir's internal analysis (Board Consent, Recital D; Exhibit B) puts the proper value at $1,048,900 – a swing of **$277,550** from Glailirt's figure. If the Review Period has truly lapsed, Praitir is on the hook for $111,650 and has forfeited the contractual mechanism to recover what it believes is an overstatement.

**Potential arguments (all uncertain):**
- *Mutual mistake*: If both sides operated under a shared misunderstanding of the Inventory Principles (e.g., both assumed replacement cost was permissible for butter/laminating fats), a court might reform under NY law. But the APA defines "Inventory Principles" by reference to Schedule 2.6, and the Board Consent (Recital D(i)) states the Closing Statement used replacement cost "rather than at the lower of cost or net realizable value **required by** the Inventory Principles" – suggesting the Principles were clear and Seller deviated. That undercuts a mutual-mistake theory.
- *Fraud or intentional misrepresentation*: If Glailirt deliberately inflated the count, § 9.5(c) removes the Deductible and Cap, and § 9.8 preserves remedies for fraud outside the indemnification framework. But the Board Consent does not allege fraud, and the discrepancies (butter valuation, expired finished goods, branded packaging, silo variances) look more like aggressive accounting than fraud.
- *Equitable estoppel / waiver by Glailirt*: If Glailirt's conduct (e.g., agreeing to the count procedure, the role of Marlowe Veitch at the count) induced Praitir to believe the Review Period was still open, there may be an estoppel argument. This is a stretch.

**Recommended fix:** Do **not** deliver the Notice of Disagreement as authorized by Resolution 2.2 without first assessing whether it would be treated as a belated claim under § 9.1 (survival of reps) or as a standalone breach. If we do deliver it, frame it as a claim under § 9.2(a) (breach of the § 4.10 Inventory representation) rather than under § 2.6(b), and preserve the argument that § 2.6(c) does not bar a rep-and-warranty claim. Alternatively, negotiate a bilateral extension of the Review Period with Glailirt. Either way, this needs a strategy call before any communication goes to the other side.

### 2. TSA Change-of-Control Trigger – Copperline Sale Will Give Seller the Right to Terminate Transition Services

**Documents:** TSA § 12.2; Board Written Consent, Recital E, Resolution 4, Exhibit A

TSA § 12.2 provides:

> "For purposes of this Section 12.2, any change of control of Buyer shall be deemed an assignment, including … any transfer of a majority of the equity interests or voting power of Buyer … If any assignment or deemed assignment occurs without Seller's prior written consent, **Seller may terminate this Agreement upon ten (10) days' notice to Buyer**."

The Copperline Sale transfers **100% of the membership interests** of Praitir (Board Consent, Recital E). This is squarely a "change of control" under § 12.2. The expected closing is **July 15, 2026**, which falls **inside** the Transition Period (180 days from March 2 = approximately **August 29, 2026**; TSA § 1.1).

The Board's Exhibit A – Required Consents lists only:
1. Hudson Commerce Bank (Credit Agreement § 7.11)
2. Tidewater (Equipment Lease § 18.3)

**Glailirt's consent under TSA § 12.2 is not listed.**

**Why it matters:** If Seller terminates the TSA upon the Copperline closing, Praitir loses, on ten days' notice:
- Production planning and scheduling (A-1)
- Quality assurance and food safety support (A-2)
- Procurement under Seller's supply arrangements (A-3)
- Logistics and distribution / route planning (A-4)
- **Access to the ERP, order management, warehouse management, and route accounting systems (A-5)** – which Praitir does not own (Bill of Sale, Schedule 1, Part 5)
- Payroll processing (A-6, though this ends ~June 1 anyway)
- Finance and accounting support (A-7)

Service A-5 is flagged in Schedule A as a dependency: it "supports Services A-1, A-3, A-4 and A-7 and may not be terminated … while any of those Services remains in effect." In other words, the IT service is the backbone. Without it, Praitir cannot run the plant.

**Recommended fix:**
- Add Glailirt's consent under TSA § 12.2 to the Required Consents in the MIPA workstream immediately.
- The consent should be a condition to the Copperline closing, not a post-closing item.
- Negotiate the consent now, while the inventory dispute (Issue 1) gives us leverage to bundle.
- If Glailirt withholds consent as leverage on the inventory issue, escalate to the partner for a commercial decision on whether to pay the $111,650 to secure the TSA consent.

## ADDITIONAL ISSUES

### 3. TSA § 4.3 – Occupancy Costs Cover "Facilities," Which Includes the Secaucus Depot

**Documents:** TSA § 4.3; APA § 1.1 (definition of "Facilities"); APA § 2.2(d) (Secaucus Depot as Excluded Asset)

TSA § 4.3 requires Buyer to reimburse Seller for "all rent, utilities … real estate taxes, insurance premiums allocable to the premises, and repair and maintenance costs **of the Facilities**." The defined term "Facilities" (APA § 1.1) means "the Bronx Plant **and the Secaucus Depot**." The Secaucus Depot is an Excluded Asset (APA § 2.2(d)); Praitir does not own it, use it, or receive any benefit from it.

**Why it matters:** As drafted, Praitir is contractually obligated to pay Seller's occupancy costs at a facility Praitir never acquired. If the Secaucus Depot has meaningful rent, utilities, or tax costs over the 180-day Transition Period, this is a direct cash outflow with no corresponding benefit.

**Recommended fix:** Amend TSA § 4.3 to limit reimbursable occupancy costs to the Bronx Plant only, or to the premises where Services are actually performed. If Glailirt resists, quantify the Secaucus Depot costs and net them against the inventory dispute.

### 4. TSA § 4.7 – No Set-Off Against Service Fees

**Documents:** TSA § 4.7

> "Except for amounts disputed under Section 4.5, Buyer shall not set off any amount owed to it by Seller under the Purchase Agreement or any other Ancillary Agreement against the Service Fees."

**Why it matters:** Praitir pays $142,000/month in Service Fees plus Pass-Through Costs and Occupancy Costs. If Praitir has a valid claim against Glailirt (inventory overstatement, indemnification for a rep breach, etc.), it cannot net that claim against the monthly TSA invoices. It must pay the TSA in full and pursue its claim separately. This is cash-flow negative and removes a natural negotiating lever.

**Recommended fix:** Negotiate a carve-out permitting set-off for amounts that are (a) finally determined under the APA's dispute resolution mechanisms, or (b) the subject of a pending Independent Accountant determination. At minimum, secure the right to set off amounts adjudicated in Praitir's favor.

### 5. APA § 6.8(b) – Buyer Assumes All WARN Act Liabilities, Including Pre-Closing

**Documents:** APA § 6.8(b)

> "Buyer shall be responsible for, and shall pay, perform and discharge when due, all Liabilities … under the WARN Act, in each case relating to the Business Employees, **whether arising before, on or after the Closing Date**."

**Why it matters:** This is broader than market. If Praitir later reduces the Bronx Plant workforce (e.g., in connection with the Copperline Sale integration, a plant rationalization, or an economic downturn), and the reduction triggers a WARN obligation, Praitir bears the cost even if the underlying operational decision relates to pre-Closing conditions or the inherited workforce structure. For a plant of this size, a WARN violation carries 60 days of back-pay and benefits per affected employee plus potential civil penalties.

**Recommended fix:** This is a done-deal issue (Closing has occurred). Flag it for the client's operational planning: any future headcount reduction at the Bronx Plant should be evaluated for WARN exposure with the understanding that Praitir bears 100% of the risk. If the Copperline Sale triggers a restructuring, model the WARN cost now.

### 6. APA § 9.5(b) – Indemnification Cap of $1,840,000

**Documents:** APA § 9.5(b); § 9.5(c)

The cap on Seller's indemnification for rep-and-warranty breaches is **$1,840,000** (10% of the $18.4M Base Purchase Price). The Deductible is $184,000 (1%). Neither applies to Fundamental Representations (§§ 4.1, 4.2, 4.7, 4.20) or fraud (§ 9.5(c)).

**Why it matters:** For an operating food plant with environmental, food-safety, and product-liability exposure, $1.84M is a modest backstop. A single significant Listeria recall, an environmental remediation obligation, or a combustible-dust OSHA citation could exceed the cap. The Fundamental Reps (title, authority, organization, brokers) are the only uncapped categories, and they are unlikely to be the source of major operational losses.

**Recommended fix:** Done-deal. Ensure the client's post-Closing insurance program (product liability, recall, environmental) is robust. The cap makes insurance the primary protection, not the indemnity.

### 7. TSA § 10.2 – Seller's Liability Under the TSA Capped at Total Service Fees

**Documents:** TSA § 10.2

> "The aggregate liability of Seller under this Agreement shall not exceed the total Service Fees paid and payable under this Agreement."

Total Service Fees over 180 days ≈ $852,000 (Monthly Fee) plus Pass-Through Costs and Occupancy Costs. The cap does not apply to breaches of § 7 (Systems Access and Data) or § 8 (Confidentiality), indemnification under § 11, or fraud/gross negligence/willful misconduct.

**Why it matters:** If a service failure (e.g., a QA lapse under Service A-2) leads to a food-safety event, Praitir's recovery against Seller under the TSA is capped at ~$852K–$1M. The actual cost of a recall or regulatory action could be far higher. Praitir's protection would need to come from its own insurance and from the APA indemnification (subject to the $1.84M cap there).

**Recommended fix:** Done-deal. Confirm that Praitir's product liability and recall insurance (which it must obtain independently, since Seller's policies are Excluded Assets under APA § 2.2(e)) provides adequate coverage for the Transition Period.

### 8. Grease Interceptor – Potential Inconsistency with Seller's Environmental Representation

**Documents:** APA § 4.16(c); Board Written Consent, Resolution 6.1(b)

APA § 4.16(c) represents: "No underground storage tanks, **grease interceptors in disrepair**, or ammonia refrigeration systems subject to a pending corrective action are located at the Facilities."

Board Consent Resolution 6.1(b) approves capital expenditure for "the replacement of the grease interceptor serving the laminating and sanitation areas."

**Why it matters:** If the grease interceptor was in disrepair at Closing (necessitating replacement), Seller's § 4.16(c) representation was inaccurate. This would support an indemnification claim under § 9.2(a). However, the claim is subject to the $184,000 Deductible, and the cost of replacing a grease interceptor may not exceed it. Also, the representation survives until 60 days after the Transition Period expires (~October 28, 2026), so the claim is timely.

**Recommended fix:** Document the condition of the grease interceptor at Closing (photos, maintenance logs, the Phase I ESA if one was done under § 6.2). If it was in fact in disrepair, include it as an item in any negotiation with Glailirt (potentially bundled with the inventory dispute). If it was merely due for routine replacement, this is not a breach.

### 12. APA § 6.7(a) – Non-Compete Carve-Out for Seller's Retail Bakery-Café Business

**Documents:** APA § 6.7(a); APA § 2.2(d) (Secaucus Depot excluded)

The 5-year non-compete prohibits Seller from engaging in "wholesale production or sale of bread, rolls or laminated pastry products to foodservice, grocery or institutional customers" in NY, NJ, or CT. However:

> "The continued use by Seller of the Secaucus Depot for the distribution of products of **Seller's retail bakery-café business, which is not part of the Business**, shall not breach this Section 6.7(a)."

**Why it matters:** Seller retains the Secaucus Depot (an Excluded Asset) and can use it to distribute retail bakery-café products. If Seller's retail business grows or begins supplying foodservice accounts (even small ones), the line between "retail bakery-café" and "wholesale" could become blurred. The non-compete does not prevent Seller from owning an interest in a person that engages in the restricted business (subject to the 2% public-securities carve-out).

**Recommended fix:** Monitor Seller's post-Closing activities. If the retail bakery-café begins wholesale distribution, assess whether it crosses the line. The 5-year Restricted Period runs to ~March 2031, so this is a long-term watch item.
