## ISSUES REQUIRING IMMEDIATE PARTNER ATTENTION

### Issue 1: Seller Note Caps Fundamental Representation and Tax Recovery at $4.85 Million and Eliminates Direct Recourse Against Seller

**Documents:** Seller Note § 5.2; SPA §§ 9.5(c), 9.6(c), 12.10(b); LOI § 4(b)

**Description:**

Seller Note § 5.2 provides:

> "The aggregate amount set off under this Note, together with all amounts disbursed to the Buyer Indemnitees from the Escrow Funds, **shall not exceed the Cap, including claims for breach of Fundamental Representations and claims for Taxes**."

The "Cap" is defined in SPA § 9.5(a) as $4,850,000 (10% of the Purchase Price).

By contrast, SPA § 9.5(c) expressly carves Fundamental Representations, Fraud, and Pre-Closing Taxes **out** of the Cap and the Basket, and provides that "the aggregate liability of Seller for such Losses shall not exceed the **Purchase Price**" (i.e., $48,500,000).

The LOI § 4(b) confirms the parties' original agreement: "The cap and basket will not apply to claims for fraud, breach of Fundamental Representations, or pre-Closing taxes."

Compounding the problem, SPA § 12.10(b) provides that "with respect to payment, set-off and recourse under the Seller Note, **the Seller Note shall control**." The Seller Note therefore prevails over the SPA on these points.

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

If Seller breaches a Fundamental Representation (e.g., title to the Shares is defective, or Seller lacked authority) or if Pre-Closing Taxes are assessed, Saitosk's total recovery—escrow plus set-off—is limited to $4,850,000, not $48,500,000. This is a $43.65 million reduction in maximum recovery for the categories of claims the parties originally agreed would be uncapped (subject only to the Purchase Price).

**Recommended fix:**

Amend Seller Note § 5.2 to delete the phrase "including claims for breach of Fundamental Representations and claims for Taxes" and to add a carve-out consistent with SPA § 9.5(c): the Cap limitation shall not apply to Losses arising out of Fraud, any inaccuracy in or breach of any Fundamental Representation, or any Pre-Closing Taxes. For those carved-out claims, the aggregate set-off plus escrow disbursements shall not exceed the Purchase Price.

### Issue 2: Seller Note § 5.2 Makes Set-Off the "Sole and Exclusive" Recourse Beyond Escrow, Eliminating Direct Claims Against Seller

**Documents:** Seller Note § 5.2; SPA § 9.6; LOI § 4(c)

**Description:**

Seller Note § 5.2 (first sentence):

> "Set-off against this Note under Section 5.1 is the **sole and exclusive means** by which Maker and the other Buyer Indemnitees may recover from Holder any Losses in excess of the Escrow Funds."

SPA § 9.6 provides a three-tier recovery waterfall:

> "(a) first, from the Escrow Funds …; (b) second, at Buyer's election, by set-off against amounts then outstanding under the Seller Note …; and **(c) thereafter, directly from Seller**."

LOI § 4(c) confirms: "Buyer will recover indemnifiable losses first from the escrow, then at Buyer's election by set-off against the Seller Note, **and then directly from Seller**."

Because SPA § 12.10(b) gives the Seller Note control over "recourse," the Seller Note's "sole and exclusive" language overrides SPA § 9.6(c).

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

The Seller Note amortizes to zero by April 30, 2029 (three $2 million installments). General representations survive 18 months (until October 30, 2027), so the note will still have a balance during that window. However, Fundamental Representation and Tax claims survive until 60 days after the applicable statute of limitations expires (SPA § 9.1)—potentially 6–10+ years. After the note is fully paid, Saitosk will have **no recourse whatsoever** against Seller for those long-tail claims. Even before the note is paid off, if the note balance is insufficient to cover a large claim (e.g., a $10 million tax assessment), Saitosk cannot go after Seller directly.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend Seller Note § 5.2 to provide that set-off is the exclusive recourse **only to the extent the note has an outstanding balance**, and to preserve SPA § 9.6(c) (direct recourse against Seller) for any amount not recoverable by set-off because the note has been satisfied or the set-off cap has been reached. At minimum, preserve direct recourse for Fraud, Fundamental Representation, and Pre-Closing Tax claims.

### Issue 3: Escrow Agreement Excludes Fines, Penalties, and Government Payments from "Losses," Defeating the Food Safety Indemnity

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement § 1.1 (definition of "Losses"); SPA §§ 9.4, 1.1 (definition of "Losses"), 12.10(a); LOI § 5

**Description:**

The SPA defines "Losses" (SPA § 1.1) to include "fines, penalties" and other amounts. SPA § 9.4 (Food Safety Specific Indemnity) expressly covers:

> "all Losses, **including fines, penalties, recall and remediation costs and amounts paid to any Governmental Authority**, arising out of or relating to (a) the listeria finding …, (b) the warning letter issued by the FDA …, and (c) any inspection, enforcement action, recall, customer claim or third-party claim arising from the conditions described in clauses (a) and (b)."

SPA § 9.4 further provides that these claims "shall be **payable first from the Escrow Funds**."

However, the Escrow Agreement defines "Losses" (Escrow Agreement § 1.1) as:

> "losses, damages, liabilities, deficiencies, judgments, interest, awards, costs and expenses, including reasonable attorneys' fees, actually incurred by a Buyer Indemnitee, **excluding fines, penalties and other amounts paid to any Governmental Authority**."

Escrow Agreement § 4.5 provides: "The Escrow Agent shall disburse Escrow Funds to Buyer under this Article 4 **only in respect of Losses**."

SPA § 12.10(a) provides that "with respect to the release of Escrow Funds, **the Escrow Agreement shall control**."

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

The entire point of the specific food safety indemnity is to protect Saitosk from FDA-related costs. The most likely and most costly outcomes of an FDA warning letter—fines, civil monetary penalties, mandatory recall costs paid to the government, and settlement amounts paid to the FDA—are **expressly excluded** from the Escrow Agreement's definition of "Losses." The Escrow Agent is contractually prohibited from disbursing those amounts from the $4.85 million escrow.

This means that if the FDA imposes a $2 million civil penalty or mandates a recall costing $3 million in government-paid amounts, Saitosk cannot recover those from escrow. It would need to go to Seller directly (which, per Issue 2, may not be available) or absorb the loss.

The LOI § 5 clearly contemplated that "Those losses will include fines, penalties, recall and remediation costs, and amounts paid to any governmental authority" and that "[c]laims under the specific indemnity will be satisfied first from the escrow." The Escrow Agreement as drafted does not honor this.

**Recommended fix:**

Amend the Escrow Agreement's definition of "Losses" in § 1.1 to delete the exclusion of "fines, penalties and other amounts paid to any Governmental Authority," or to add a proviso that the exclusion does not apply to claims arising under SPA § 9.4 (Pre-Closing Food Safety Matters). Alternatively, add a separate category (e.g., "Food Safety Losses") that includes fines, penalties, and government payments, and permit the Escrow Agent to disburse against that category.

## OTHER SIGNIFICANT ISSUES

### Issue 4: Post-Closing Obligation to Release Seller from Guarantees May Require Saitosk to Provide Substitute Credit Support at Its Own Cost

**Documents:** SPA §§ 1.1 (definition of "Seller Guarantees"), 6.5; LOI § 6

**Description:**

SPA § 6.5: "Following the Closing, **Buyer** shall use commercially reasonable efforts to cause Seller to be released from the Seller Guarantees, including by **offering substitute credit support reasonably acceptable to the relevant counterparties**."

The Seller Guarantees cover (a) the Company's obligations under the Flight Kitchen lease and (b) the Company's obligations under its food and beverage supply contracts (SPA § 1.1).

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

Saitosk will likely need to post a letter of credit, cash collateral, or a parent guarantee to the Flight Kitchen landlord and supply contract counterparties to induce them to release Seller. The cost of that credit support (LC fees, opportunity cost of cash collateral, or the credit risk of a parent guarantee) is not allocated to Seller anywhere in the documents. This is an unquantified post-closing expense and potential credit exposure for Saitosk.

**Recommended fix:**

Negotiate an amendment to SPA § 6.5 providing that (a) Seller bears the cost of any substitute credit support provided by Buyer, or (b) the parties share the cost, or (c) at minimum, set a deadline (e.g., 180 days post-closing) after which Buyer's obligation is discharged if the counterparties have not agreed to a release.

### Issue 5: Seller Note Cross-Default with Senior Debt Creates Cascade Risk

**Documents:** Seller Note § 8.1(f)

**Description:**

Seller Note § 8.1(f) makes it an Event of Default if "Senior Debt in an aggregate principal amount exceeding **$5,000,000** is accelerated, and the acceleration is not rescinded within 30 days."

"Senior Debt" is broadly defined (Seller Note § 1.1) to include all bank and institutional lending to Maker or the Company.

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

If Saitosk's senior acquisition facility (or any other institutional debt above $5 million) is accelerated—for example, due to a technical covenant breach—the Seller Note is also in default. While the subordination in Article 4 means Senior Lenders get paid first, the default triggers the 8% default interest rate (Seller Note § 2.3) and gives Seller the right to accelerate the full $6 million note balance (Seller Note § 8.2). This creates a refinancing risk: Saitosk could face a $6 million immediate cash obligation on top of its senior debt problem.

**Recommended fix:**

Negotiate to raise the cross-default threshold (e.g., to $10 million or a percentage of Senior Debt), add a longer cure period (e.g., 60 days), or limit the cross-default to accelerations that are not subsequently rescinded or waived by the Senior Lenders.

### Issue 6: Airline Customer Consent Lapses on the Same Date as the SPA Outside Date—No Buffer

**Documents:** Airline Customer Consent Letter § 4.2; SPA § 8.1(b)

**Description:**

The Corriedale consent lapses if "the Closing has not occurred by **June 30, 2026**" (Consent § 4.2). The SPA outside date is also June 30, 2026 (SPA § 8.1(b)).

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

If the closing is delayed by even one day beyond June 30, 2026 (e.g., due to a last-minute regulatory issue or a wire delay), the Corriedale consent lapses. Re-obtaining the consent from a major airline customer after the outside date has passed could be difficult or impossible, and would likely require Seller's cooperation (which Seller may no longer be obligated to provide after termination).

**Recommended fix:**

Request that Corriedale extend the lapse date to 30–60 days beyond the SPA outside date (e.g., July 31 or August 30, 2026), or negotiate an automatic 30-day extension if the delay is not caused by Buyer.

### Issue 7: Office Lease Change-of-Control Consent—No Consequence for Landlord Non-Response

**Documents:** Office Lease §§ 11.1, 11.2; SPA § 7.1(c)

**Description:**

The office lease requires Landlord consent for a change of control exceeding 50% of voting stock (Lease § 11.2). Landlord "shall respond to a complete request within 15 Business Days" (Lease § 11.1), but the lease does not specify what happens if Landlord fails to respond within that period.

SPA § 7.1(c) makes the Ashgrove consent a condition to Buyer's closing obligation.

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

If the Landlord stalls or ignores the consent request, Saitosk cannot close (the condition is not satisfied) but also cannot terminate for Seller breach (Seller's covenant in § 6.4 is only to use "commercially reasonable efforts"). Saitosk could be stuck between the signing date and the outside date without a remedy.

**Recommended fix:**

Confirm that the consent request has been made and document the 15-Business-Day deadline. If the Landlord does not respond, consider whether silence can be treated as deemed consent under New York law (generally it cannot absent an express provision). Alternatively, negotiate with the Landlord now for a response or a short-form consent before the closing date.

### Issue 8: Seller Note Requires Monthly CFO-Certified Financials for Three Years

**Documents:** Seller Note § 7.1(a)

**Description:**

Maker (Saitosk) must deliver to Holder (Seller) "within 30 days after the end of each calendar month, the unaudited balance sheet of the Company … and the related statements of income and cash flows … **certified by the chief financial officer of Maker** as fairly presenting in all material respects the financial position and results of operations of the Company."

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

This is a monthly (not quarterly) reporting obligation that continues for the full three-year life of the note. The CFO certification creates personal accountability and could be construed as a representation that gives Seller a claim if the financials are later found to be materially misleading. It also imposes an ongoing administrative and compliance burden on Saitosk's finance team.

**Recommended fix:**

Negotiate to reduce the frequency to quarterly, or to limit the certification to "prepared in accordance with GAAP on a consistent basis" rather than "fairly presenting in all material respects." Alternatively, provide that the certification is made solely for purposes of Seller's monitoring and does not constitute a representation giving rise to a separate claim.

### Issue 9: Saitosk Is the Taxable Owner of Escrow Funds

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement §§ 6.1, 6.3

**Description:**

Escrow Agreement § 6.1: "For federal and applicable state income tax purposes, **Buyer is treated as the owner of the Escrow Funds** until they are disbursed. Buyer shall report all income earned on the Escrow Funds."

Section 6.3 provides a partial mitigation: the Escrow Agent will release 40% of annual earnings to Buyer on request.

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

Saitosk will owe income tax on the escrow earnings (presumably interest on a money market account) for up to 18 months, even though the funds are held as security for Seller's obligations and may ultimately be released to Seller. The 40% distribution in § 6.3 may not cover Saitosk's actual tax rate if it is in a higher bracket.

**Recommended fix:**

Confirm with Saitosk's tax advisors whether the 40% distribution is adequate. If not, negotiate to increase the distribution percentage or to provide that Seller reimburses Buyer for any excess tax on escrow earnings ultimately allocated to Seller upon release.

### Issue 10: Insurance Netting Reduces Recoverable Losses

**Documents:** SPA § 9.5(d)

**Description:**

"Losses shall be calculated **net of any insurance proceeds actually received** by the Indemnitee in respect of the matter, less the costs of recovery."

**Why it matters to Saitosk:**

If the Company carries insurance that covers some food-safety-related losses (e.g., product liability, property damage from a recall), Saitosk can only recover the uninsured portion from Seller. Saitosk should confirm the scope and limits of the Company's current insurance programs before closing to understand the net exposure.

**Recommended fix:**

During diligence, obtain copies of the Company's insurance policies and confirm coverage for food safety events, product liability, and recall costs. Model the net indemnification exposure after insurance.

**Bottom line:** Issues 1–3 are structural defects in the interplay between the SPA, the Seller Note, and the Escrow Agreement. As drafted, the order-of-precedence clause (SPA § 12.10) causes the ancillary documents to override the more favorable indemnification terms in the SPA, significantly reducing Saitosk's practical recovery for the most serious categories of claims (fundamental reps, taxes, and FDA-related fines/penalties). These should be corrected before signing or, if the SPA is already signed, before closing. I recommend we raise all three with Seller's counsel as a package.
