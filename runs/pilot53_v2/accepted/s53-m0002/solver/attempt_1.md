## ISSUES REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION

### 1. Escrow Agreement Excludes Fines, Penalties, and Governmental Payments from "Losses" — Defeats the Food Safety Indemnity

**Documents:** Escrow Agreement §1.1 (definition of "Losses"), §4.5; SPA §9.4, §1.1 (definition of "Losses"), §12.10(a); LOI §5.

**Issue.** The SPA defines "Losses" to include "fines, penalties, costs and expenses" (SPA §1.1) and the Food Safety Specific Indemnity expressly covers "fines, penalties, recall and remediation costs and amounts paid to any Governmental Authority" arising from the January 2026 listeria finding and FDA warning letter (SPA §9.4). SPA §9.4 further provides that these claims "shall be payable first from the Escrow Funds."

However, the Escrow Agreement defines "Losses" as "excluding fines, penalties and other amounts paid to any Governmental Authority" (Escrow Agreement §1.1), and §4.5 states the Escrow Agent "shall disburse Escrow Funds to Buyer under this Article 4 only in respect of Losses." The Escrow Agent will therefore refuse to release escrow funds for FDA fines, penalties, or remediation payments to the FDA — precisely the losses the SPA says are payable first from escrow.

Under SPA §12.10(a), "with respect to the release of Escrow Funds, the Escrow Agreement shall control." The Escrow Agreement's narrower definition thus governs, and the food safety indemnity's escrow-first mechanism is effectively disabled for the most likely category of loss (regulatory fines and penalties).

The LOI §5 confirms the parties' intent: "Those losses will include fines, penalties, recall and remediation costs, and amounts paid to any governmental authority. Claims under the specific indemnity will be satisfied first from the escrow."

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** The FDA has already issued a warning letter. If the FDA imposes fines, mandates a recall, or requires remediation, Saitosk would expect to recover those costs from the $4.85M escrow as the first source of payment. As drafted, it cannot. Saitosk would be forced to skip to set-off against the Seller Note (itself limited — see Issue 2 below) or to litigate against Seller directly.

**Recommendation.** Amend Escrow Agreement §1.1 to conform the definition of "Losses" to the SPA definition, or add a carve-out providing that, for claims under SPA §9.4 (Pre-Closing Food Safety Matters), "Losses" includes fines, penalties, and amounts paid to any Governmental Authority. Alternatively, add a specific provision in Article 4 of the Escrow Agreement authorizing disbursement for SPA §9.4 claims notwithstanding the general "Losses" definition.

### 2. Seller Note §5.2 Caps Set-Off at the General Cap and Eliminates Direct Recourse from Seller — Contradicts SPA §9.5(c) and §9.6(c)

**Documents:** Seller Note §5.2; SPA §9.5(c), §9.6, §12.10(b); LOI §4(c).

**Issue.** SPA §9.5(c) carves Fundamental Representations and Pre-Closing Taxes out of the 10% Cap, providing that "the aggregate liability of Seller for such Losses shall not exceed the Purchase Price" ($48.5M). SPA §9.6 establishes a three-tier recovery: (a) escrow, (b) set-off against the Seller Note, (c) "thereafter, directly from Seller."

Seller Note §5.2 provides two limitations that directly contradict the SPA:

- **Sole and exclusive recourse:** "Set-off against this Note under Section 5.1 is the sole and exclusive means by which Maker and the other Buyer Indemnitees may recover from Holder any Losses in excess of the Escrow Funds." This eliminates SPA §9.6(c) (direct recovery from Seller) for any claim above the escrow.
- **Cap applies to Fundamental Reps and Taxes:** "The aggregate amount set off under this Note, together with all amounts disbursed to the Buyer Indemnitees from the Escrow Funds, shall not exceed the Cap, **including claims for breach of Fundamental Representations and claims for Taxes**." This overrides SPA §9.5(c)'s higher $48.5M cap for those categories.

Under SPA §12.10(b), "with respect to payment, set-off and recourse under the Seller Note, the Seller Note shall control." The Seller Note's limitations therefore prevail.

**Practical effect.** For a breach of a Fundamental Representation (e.g., Seller did not actually own the Shares free and clear, or lacked authority to sell) or a Pre-Closing Tax liability, Saitosk's total recovery is limited to $4.85M (escrow) + $4.85M (set-off) = **$9.7M**, not the $48.5M the SPA purports to allow. And Saitosk has no right to sue Seller directly for the excess.

The LOI §4(c) states the agreed recovery structure as "first from the escrow, then at Buyer's election by set-off against the Seller Note, and then directly from Seller" — a three-tier structure the Seller Note as drafted does not preserve.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** Saitosk is paying $48.5M. If a Fundamental Rep is breached (e.g., undisclosed liens on the Shares, or a capitalization defect), the maximum recovery under the current documents is $9.7M — roughly 20% of the purchase price. For a material tax exposure (e.g., unaccrued payroll taxes), the same $9.7M ceiling applies. This is a material reduction in protection from what the SPA text and the LOI both indicate was agreed.

**Recommendation.** Amend Seller Note §5.2 to:
- Delete the phrase "including claims for breach of Fundamental Representations and claims for Taxes" so that the Cap limitation does not apply to those categories (conforming to SPA §9.5(c)); and
- Delete or revise the "sole and exclusive means" language to preserve SPA §9.6(c)'s right of direct recourse against Seller for amounts in excess of both the escrow and the note balance.

Alternatively, if the Seller's position is non-negotiable, Saitosk should require a higher escrow or a larger note to compensate for the reduced recourse, or should price the deal accordingly.

### 3. Buyer's Indemnity for Seller Guarantees Is Uncapped

**Documents:** SPA §9.3(b), §9.5(a), §6.5; LOI §6.

**Issue.** SPA §9.3(b) provides that Buyer indemnifies Seller for "any and all Losses arising under the Seller Guarantees, whether arising before or after the Closing." The Seller Guarantees (defined in SPA §1.1) cover (a) the Company's obligations under the Flight Kitchen lease and (b) the Company's obligations under its food and beverage supply contracts.

The Cap in SPA §9.5(a) applies only to "the aggregate liability of Buyer under Section 9.3(a)" (breach of Buyer's reps and covenants). Section 9.3(b) is not subject to the Cap, the Basket, or any other limitation. SPA §9.5(c) carves out Fraud, Fundamental Reps, and Pre-Closing Taxes from the limitations, but does not extend those limitations to §9.3(b) — it simply doesn't address §9.3(b) at all.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** If the Company defaults under the Flight Kitchen lease (e.g., fails to pay rent, breaches a use provision) or under a food and beverage supply contract, Seller can be called on the guarantee, and Saitosk must indemnify Seller for the full amount — uncapped. The Flight Kitchen lease is not in our folder, so we cannot assess the size of the exposure. Given that the Flight Kitchen is the Company's primary operating facility, the lease obligations are likely substantial (rent, CAM, potential early termination premiums).

Additionally, SPA §6.5 obligates Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to get Seller released from the guarantees post-closing. If those efforts fail (e.g., the landlord or supply contractors refuse substitute credit support), the guarantee exposure persists indefinitely, and Saitosk's uncapped indemnity remains in force.

**Recommendation.**
- Negotiate a cap on §9.3(b) (e.g., the Purchase Price, or a fixed dollar amount tied to the remaining lease term and supply contract values).
- Obtain and review the Flight Kitchen lease and the food and beverage supply contracts to quantify the maximum guarantee exposure.
- Consider requiring Seller to deliver evidence of release (or a letter from the guaranty counterparties confirming release) as a post-closing covenant with a specific deadline, and tying the §9.3(b) indemnity to a sunset date.

### 4. Governing Law and Jurisdiction Inconsistency Across Ancillary Agreements

**Documents:** SPA §12.8 (New York / Manhattan); Seller Note §10.5 (New York / Manhattan); Bill of Sale §6.2 (New York / Manhattan); Escrow Agreement §11.2 (Delaware / Court of Chancery); Office Lease §14.8 (New York / Queens County or EDNY).

**Issue.** The Escrow Agreement is governed by Delaware law and subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the Delaware Court of Chancery (or Superior Court). All other transaction documents are governed by New York law. The Office Lease adds a further wrinkle by specifying Queens County or EDNY rather than Manhattan.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** A dispute over escrow disbursement (e.g., the "Losses" definition issue in Item 1 above) would be litigated in Delaware under Delaware law, while the underlying indemnification claim would be analyzed under New York law in Manhattan. This creates the risk of inconsistent interpretations, increased litigation cost, and potential forum-shopping by Seller. The Court of Chancery also has a different procedural posture (equitable focus, no jury — though jury is waived here) than New York state or federal court.

**Recommendation.** Conform the Escrow Agreement's governing law and jurisdiction to New York / Manhattan, consistent with the SPA and the other Ancillary Agreements. If Seller insists on Delaware (perhaps because the Escrow Agent is more comfortable there), at minimum ensure the Escrow Agreement incorporates the SPA's definition of "Losses" without modification to avoid the definitional conflict in Item 1.

### 5. Seller Note Cross-Default to Senior Debt Acceleration

**Documents:** Seller Note §8.1(f), §4.1–4.2.

**Issue.** Seller Note §8.1(f) provides that an Event of Default occurs if "Senior Debt in an aggregate principal amount exceeding $5,000,000 is accelerated, and the acceleration is not rescinded within 30 days." "Senior Debt" is defined broadly to include "all indebtedness and other obligations of Maker or the Company, now or later outstanding, under any credit agreement, loan agreement, note purchase agreement or similar financing arrangement with one or more banks, institutional lenders or other financial institutions" (Seller Note §1.1).

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** Saitosk is presumably financing this acquisition with senior debt (the Seller Note is expressly subordinated to it). If the acquisition loan is accelerated for any reason (a technical default, a covenant breach, a market event), the Seller Note is immediately in default, the $6M balance accelerates (subject to the subordination in Article 4), and the default rate of 8% kicks in (Seller Note §2.3). This creates a cascading default risk: a problem with the acquisition financing triggers a default on the Seller Note, which could further damage Saitosk's credit position.

The $5M threshold is relatively low. If Saitosk's acquisition facility is, say, $30M, any acceleration of more than $5M of it triggers this default.

**Recommendation.**
- Increase the threshold (e.g., to the full principal amount of the Senior Debt, or a higher dollar figure).
- Add a cure period or require that the acceleration be "final and non-appealable" before it triggers an Event of Default under the Seller Note.
- Confirm with Saitosk's financing team the structure of the acquisition debt to ensure this cross-default is workable.

The Office Lease §11.1 requires Landlord to respond within 15 Business Days of a complete request, and §11.2 limits the factors Landlord may consider to "the financial strength of Tenant after the transaction and the continued use of the Premises for the Permitted Use." Prepare a consent request package that addresses both factors (e.g., Saitosk's financial statements, confirmation of continued permitted use).

### 7. Food Safety Indemnity Survival Period Is Ambiguous

**Documents:** SPA §9.1, §9.4, §9.8.

**Issue.** SPA §9.1 provides that representations and warranties survive 18 months (longer for Fundamental Reps and Taxes). The Food Safety Specific Indemnity in §9.4 is a standalone indemnity obligation, not a representation or warranty. Section 9.1 does not expressly state a survival period for §9.4. Section 9.8 provides for escrow release at 18 months.

If §9.4 is treated as a covenant, it survives "in accordance with its terms" (SPA §9.1, last sentence) — and its terms are silent on duration, implying indefinite survival. If it is treated as analogous to a rep/warranty, it might be read as surviving only 18 months.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** FDA enforcement actions and product liability claims related to the January 2026 listeria finding could surface well after 18 months (e.g., a latent illness claim, a delayed FDA inspection finding, or a customer termination based on the warning letter). If the indemnity is read as expiring at 18 months, Saitosk has no contractual recourse for late-arising food safety claims. Even if it survives indefinitely, the escrow is gone after 18 months, and (per Issue 2) set-off against the Seller Note is the sole recourse above escrow, capped at $4.85M.

**Recommendation.** Add express language in SPA §9.4 (or §9.1) stating that the Food Safety Specific Indemnity survives for a period longer than 18 months — ideally until the later of (a) the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations for any claim arising from the Pre-Closing Food Safety Matters, or (b) 36 months after the Closing Date. Consider extending the escrow release date for food safety claims specifically (e.g., retain a portion of the escrow for 36 months for §9.4 claims).

### 8. Seller Note Requires Monthly Company Financials Certified by Saitosk's CFO

**Documents:** Seller Note §7.1(a), §8.1(c).

**Issue.** For three years (until the Maturity Date of April 30, 2029), Saitosk must deliver to Seller, within 30 days after each month-end, unaudited monthly financial statements of the Company "certified by the chief financial officer of Maker as fairly presenting in all material respects the financial position and results of operations of the Company." Failure to perform Article 7 covenants is an Event of Default after a 30-day cure period (Seller Note §8.1(c)).

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** This is an ongoing administrative and compliance burden. The CFO certification creates personal accountability for Saitosk's CFO. A missed delivery (even by a day, before the 30-day cure) could technically trigger a default. In a stressed scenario (e.g., if Saitosk is dealing with FDA issues or integration challenges), this covenant could become a point of leverage for Seller.

**Recommendation.**
- Negotiate an extension of the delivery deadline (e.g., 45 days) or a longer cure period for this specific covenant.
- Ensure internal processes are in place to generate and certify monthly Company financials within the required timeframe.
- Consider whether the certification standard ("fairly presenting in all material respects") is appropriate for unaudited monthly statements or whether it should be softened to "prepared in good faith based on books and records."

### 9. SPA §6.5 Post-Closing Covenant to Release Seller from Guarantees — No Deadline or Consequence

**Documents:** SPA §6.5, §9.3(b).

**Issue.** SPA §6.5 requires Buyer to use "commercially reasonable efforts" to cause Seller to be released from the Seller Guarantees "following the Closing." There is no deadline, no definition of what constitutes "commercially reasonable efforts," and no specific consequence for failure other than a general covenant breach claim by Seller (capped at $4.85M under §9.5(a) because it would be a breach of Buyer's covenant under §9.3(a)).

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** The uncapped §9.3(b) indemnity (see Issue 3) remains in force as long as the guarantees remain in effect. If Saitosk makes only token efforts to obtain releases, Seller could argue the covenant is breached, but the practical exposure for Saitosk is the ongoing guarantee indemnity, not the covenant breach itself. The lack of a deadline means Seller's guarantee (and Saitosk's uncapped indemnity) could theoretically persist for the full term of the Flight Kitchen lease or the supply contracts.

**Recommendation.**
- Add a specific deadline (e.g., 12 months post-closing) by which Saitosk must use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain releases.
- Define "commercially reasonable efforts" with specific actions (e.g., deliver a request to each counterparty within 30 days post-closing, propose substitute credit support within 60 days, escalate to Seller if a counterparty unreasonably withholds).
- Tie the §9.3(b) indemnity to a sunset: if releases are not obtained by the deadline despite Saitosk's commercially reasonable efforts, the indemnity should be capped or terminated.

### 11. Corriedale Consent Lapses on the Same Date as the SPA Drop-Dead Date — No Buffer

**Documents:** Corriedale Consent §4.2; SPA §8.1(b).

**Issue.** The Corriedale consent "will lapse and be of no further effect" if the Closing has not occurred by June 30, 2026 (Corriedale Consent §4.2). The SPA permits termination by either party if the Closing has not occurred by June 30, 2026 (SPA §8.1(b)). These dates are identical. If the closing is delayed to, say, June 28 and then a last-minute issue arises requiring a two-day extension, the consent lapses on June 30 and Saitosk would need to re-negotiate with Corriedale.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** Corriedale is a key airline customer (the SPA §6.4 specifically references the "change-of-control consent of Corriedale Regional Airways, Inc."). Losing the consent would be a closing condition failure and could also signal to Corriedale that the transaction is in trouble, potentially affecting the commercial relationship.

**Recommendation.** Request that Corriedale extend the consent lapse date to 30 days beyond the SPA drop-dead date (i.e., July 30, 2026), or negotiate the SPA drop-dead date to be later than the consent lapse date to create a buffer.

### 12. SPA §9.4 Food Safety Indemnity Not Subject to Basket but Subject to the Cap (Ambiguity)

**Documents:** SPA §9.4, §9.5(a)–(c).

**Issue.** SPA §9.4 states that Pre-Closing Food Safety Matters claims "are not subject to Section 9.5(b)" (the Basket). It does not expressly state whether they are subject to §9.5(a) (the Cap). Section 9.5(c) carves out Fraud, Fundamental Reps, and Pre-Closing Taxes from both the Cap and the Basket, but does not mention the Food Safety Specific Indemnity.

Read literally, the food safety indemnity is subject to the $4.85M Cap (because §9.5(c) doesn't carve it out), but not the Basket. This means that if food safety losses exceed $4.85M, Saitosk cannot recover the excess from Seller (absent Fraud).

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** A significant FDA enforcement action (e.g., a mandatory shutdown of the Flight Kitchen, a large recall, or substantial civil monetary penalties) could easily exceed $4.85M. If the Cap applies, Saitosk absorbs the excess.

**Recommendation.** Clarify in SPA §9.4 or §9.5(c) that the Food Safety Specific Indemnity is not subject to the Cap (or, at minimum, is subject to a higher cap, such as the Purchase Price). Given that the LOI §5 describes the food safety indemnity as "not subject to the basket" without mentioning the cap, the parties' intent may have been to exclude it from both.

### 13. Bill of Sale — No Buyer Signature or Acknowledgment

**Documents:** Bill of Sale, signature page; SPA §3.2(b).

**Issue.** The Bill of Sale is executed by Seller (Noumisk Holdings) and accepted by the Company (Noumisk Skyline Catering). Buyer (Saitosk) is not a signatory. SPA §3.2(b) requires Seller to deliver "the Escrow Agreement and the Bill of Sale, each duly executed by Seller" at Closing. The Bill of Sale does not require Buyer's execution.

If a title defect surfaces, the Company (now Saitosk's subsidiary) would need to enforce the warranty, not Saitosk directly.

**Recommendation.** This is low-risk given the stock deal structure (the Company is Saitosk's subsidiary and can enforce the Bill of Sale). No change required, but be aware of the privity limitation if a title issue arises.

### 14. SPA §4.6 "Absence of Changes" Rep Is Backdated to December 31, 2025

**Documents:** SPA §4.5, §4.6.

**Issue.** SPA §4.6 represents that "Since December 31, 2025, the Company has conducted the Business in the ordinary course and there has not been any material adverse effect on the Company." The listeria finding occurred in January 2026 — after December 31, 2025. The rep is made "as of the date of this Agreement and as of the Closing Date" (SPA Article 4 preamble).

If the listeria finding and FDA warning letter constitute a "material adverse effect" (which they likely do, given the regulatory response), then §4.6 is inaccurate as of the signing date (March 9, 2026) and the Closing Date. This would be a breach of a general representation, subject to the Cap and Basket.

However, §4.9 specifically discloses the listeria finding and FDA warning letter in Schedule 4.9. The question is whether the §4.9 disclosure is sufficient to negate the §4.6 rep. If Schedule 4.9 describes the listeria finding, a reasonable reading is that the "material adverse effect" in §4.6 is qualified by the §4.9 disclosure. But this is not express — §4.6 does not say "except as set forth in the Disclosure Schedule" in a way that specifically cross-references §4.9.

**Why it matters to Saitosk.** If §4.6 is breached (and not saved by the §4.9 disclosure), Saitosk has an indemnification claim. But it would be subject to the Basket ($242,500) and the Cap ($4.85M), and — per Issue 2 — the Seller Note's limitation on set-off. It would be cleaner to have the food safety matters addressed solely under the specific §9.4 indemnity (which is not subject to the Basket). The overlap creates ambiguity about which provision governs.

**Recommendation.** Add express language to §4.6 (or the Disclosure Schedule) confirming that the listeria finding and FDA warning letter, as described in Schedule 4.9, do not constitute a material adverse effect for purposes of §4.6, and that all claims related to those matters are governed exclusively by §9.4.
